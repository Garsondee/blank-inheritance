# Induction

*Written 2026-09-09. How six strangers arrive, are processed, and become cadets — and how the
players learn the setting at exactly the speed their characters do.*

---

## 1. The premise: everybody is new

**Every PC is new to the city.** Some come from a long way off, some from a few valleys over, but
none of them are at home here. They arrive because they were summoned.

This is a structural decision, not a flavour one. It solves the problem that a lore-rich setting
always has: somebody has to explain it, and every explanation delivered to a character who should
already know it reads as exposition. If the characters are new, the explanations are real — an
induction officer explaining the base's rules is doing their job, not serving the audience.

**The rule that follows:** the players learn the setting at the speed the characters do, and never
faster. If the party has not been told something, the GM has not told the table either.

### They applied. So did several hundred other people.

**Settled 2026-09-09.** The party are **volunteers, not conscripts.** Signing up was something each
of them decided to do next with their life, and **each player defines their own reason** — where
they came from, what they left, why this. The campaign issues nobody a backstory.

**Age is not a barrier.** Seventeen is the age the kingdom's own national service takes people, and
that service is separate from the Directorate (`open-questions.json#directorate-extraterritorial-vs-national-service`).
The Directorate takes candidates who apply or are streamed to it (LEDGER #26), and volunteering has no age. Two
intake streams in one building, and some of these six may be considerably older than the local kids. *(Rewritten
2026-09-27: this said the charter permits conscription at seventeen. The charter says nothing about it, LEDGER #41.)*

### Why all six are flagged: they are a cohort, not a coincidence

**The Directorate takes several hundred candidates a semester and screens every one of them.** A
blood draw, thirty seconds, no explanation offered because none is thought necessary. Most come back
clear. A small number come back with an unusual marker, and **those candidates are quietly streamed
onto a different course, together.**

**Only people carrying that marker can operate a Frame.** That is the entire reason the track
exists, and nobody on it is told so. *(Corrected 2026-09-24, LEDGER #32 and #34: this said any Frame. Gen 1
clankers have no living core, and trained pilots drive them without the marker. The marker is what a **Gen 2**
needs ("these mechs aren't usable by just anyone"), and that is why these six are streamed together ahead of the
day Gen 2 is offered, at the end of Chapter 4.)*

Where the marker comes from varies and does not need to be uniform:

- **Real mitflit ancestry**, generations back and long forgotten.
- **A random mutation**, with no history behind it at all.
- **A resonant component** in an android or a construct, which happens to do the same job by an
  entirely different route.
- Whatever else a given character needs. The marker is a *functional* category, not a bloodline.

**Why this is the right answer**, and better than the two I proposed before it:

1. **It is mundane.** No conspiracy is required to explain why all six are flagged. They are all
   flagged because being flagged is the entry criterion for the course they are on. An institution
   sorting several hundred applicants by a test result is the least sinister thing imaginable, and
   it is doing something enormous.
2. **The mystery moves to the right place.** It is not *why us*. It is *what is this marker, and why
   will nobody say what it means.* The Directorate is not concealing a selection. It is concealing a
   **meaning**, which is precisely its founding sin.
3. **They will not notice for a while.** Being streamed by aptitude is what schools do. The party
   should find the arrangement completely unremarkable until the moment they realise that everybody
   in their section came back irregular, and that this cannot be a coincidence in a cohort of six.

**The reveal to aim for** is not "we are special". It is quieter: *we were sorted, on arrival, by
something in our blood, and the sorting was so ordinary that none of us thought to ask.*

## 2. Everyone is wearing a shape that is not theirs

**At the start of the campaign every character presents as human, elf, halfling or similar.**
Anything else in the SF2E roster is there, and is disguised — by magic, by technology, or by their
own biology.

**Disguise is ordinary in Kurose (LEDGER #44).** Aliens are treated as outsiders, and the magic that hides an unusual
nature is so freely available that glamoured clothing can be bought on the high street. Plenty of aliens in the city
refuse to wear it; Hulda Brack, who runs the machine shop, is one.

Ingram's reason: *"part of the theming of this is people coming out of their shells and revealing
their inner self to friends."*

**This is the campaign's own central image, handed to the players.** Bible §1 already says
everything in this setting is *a thing in the wrong container, and the story is about whether
anything ever gets out* — a biomechanical core in a riveted steel shell, mitflits reverted from a
star-faring form into small frightened bodies, Anabala sharing one body for two centuries, the Hand
detached, the Watcher with no hull at all. Adding the party to that list makes them a working
example of the thesis from the first session, and the *hopeful* version of it: the container that
does open.

Three things it interlocks with, none of which needed changing to accommodate it:

- **The Frames.** The pilot cavity is not a fixed shape somebody measured — it forms a seat for
  whoever links with it. A character in a borrowed shape climbing into a hollow moulded to their
  real one is a scene that writes itself, and it is available the first time they sit in a Frame.
  *(2026-09-24, LEDGER #32: only Gen 2 has a seat that shapes itself. Gen 1 Frames are Golarion clankers with no
  living core, so this scene waits for the first Gen 2, at the end of Chapter 4 at the earliest. How it meets the
  player-owned reveal was decided in LEDGER #33: a disguised pilot can climb in and fly, and finds it uncomfortable
  except in their true form. Nothing forces a reveal; it stays the player's (`open-questions.json#gen2-seat-and-disguise`).)*
- **The mitflits** were mistaken for tools and became tools. A disguised player character is
  someone managing, every day, how they are seen. They are on the same axis.
- **The Directorate's founding sin** is information suppression as a reflex. A party each keeping
  one true thing back is not hypocrisy, it is sympathy — and it makes the institution's habit
  legible from the inside.

### The Directorate's own position on it

**The institution discourages making alien origin publicly known**, and advises keeping it to close
friends and family. That is presented as protection, and it is partly sincere.

This matters because it means the disguise is not six players independently deciding to hide. It is
**policy** — quiet, reasonable-sounding, delivered during induction by somebody being kind. Which
makes it the founding sin again, in its gentlest possible form: an institution managing what people
are allowed to know about each other, for their own good, and calling it discretion.

It also sets the exact shape of the theme. The Directorate says *tell close friends only*. The
campaign is about six people deciding who counts as a close friend.

**The reveal belongs to the player, always.** Not to the GM, not to the induction system, not to a
skill check somebody else rolls. The system may *know*; the table finds out when the player decides.

**Whether a character is already disguising themselves BEFORE induction (during The Road In, their
own journey in) is the player's call too — confirmed 2026-09-10.** Ingram: *"I'll let the players
decide. If a player decides to completely ignore this stricture I'll happily roll with it."* Some will
have been managing how they're seen their whole life, policy or not; a player who wants their
character's journey in to be the last time anyone saw them as themselves gets that beat instead — both
are available, and it's never assumed either way without asking. **If a character is seen undisguised
before or during this window, the world's reaction is calibrated: a little alarm, not hysteria** —
non-terrestrial ancestries are *rare*, not impossible, and a stranger seeing one reacts to something
unusual, not something the setting has never encountered.

## 3. The medical: one adult who already knows

**Shucks Mordant sees every candidate privately, and he knows exactly what each of them is.**

A disguise does not work on the person taking your blood. Whatever a character presents as, the base
physician has the sample, the readings and two centuries of having seen it before. There is no check
to pass and no way to hide it, and **that is the point of the scene rather than a problem with it.**

**He is kind, curious, respectful and extremely knowledgeable.** Not incurious — an earlier draft of
this document made him bored and that was wrong. He has done ten thousand of these and is still
interested in every one, which is the rarest thing about him.

### The pair it forms with the terminal

These two scenes are the same fact meeting two different parts of one institution, and they should
be run close together so the contrast lands.

| | The terminal | The doctor |
|---|---|---|
| Finds out | by tripping a screen | by looking at a blood sample |
| Reaction | lockdown, and a demand to know how they evaded detection | "Yes, I know. Sit down." |
| What it wants | a record | to be sure they are all right |
| What it is | the founding sin | the reason the place is worth saving |

**That contrast is the whole Directorate in two rooms.** The system files you. The man asks how you
are. Both are sincere, and only one of them is any use to a frightened seventeen-year-old.

### What he asks

**A few questions each, tailored, and not many.** Two or three. The aim is a little extra depth and
a good first experience, not an interrogation.

For a character wearing a shape that is not theirs, his questions are practical and gentle, and they
are the questions a doctor would actually ask:

- *"How long can you hold it comfortably? I'm not asking how long you can hold it."*
- *"Does the maintenance hurt? Some of them hurt."*
- *"What do you actually eat? The canteen will not have it and I can sign for alternatives."*
- *"If you come in here unconscious, what do I need to know that I would not be able to see?"*
- *"Who else knows? — I'm not asking for names. I'm asking whether you have anyone."*

**The fourth one is the scene.** It is the kindest question in the campaign and it is also, in
retrospect, the moment the Directorate acquired a complete medical record of something the party
thought was theirs alone. He is not doing that on purpose. He is being a good doctor. Both things
are true and neither cancels the other.

**Every character gets a private moment, not only the disguised ones.** For a character who is
exactly what they appear to be, he asks about the thing they left behind to come here, or an old
badly-set fracture, or a relative he processed forty years ago and remembers clearly. Nobody sits
out this scene.

### What he will not do

He will not say what the marker means or what the track is for. He is forbidden, he thinks the rule
is stupid, and both facts show plainly to anyone who reads him. **He does not pretend not to know
what they are** — the deflection is about the institution's secret, never about the player's.

## 4. The induction terminal

Character creation, run in fiction, as a scene. The players sit at a Directorate terminal and fill
in the induction questionnaire, and what comes out is a character sheet.

This replaces nothing — bible §20's intake quiz already lists eight questions, each of which
becomes a side plot seed or a vulnerability. This is that quiz, given a diegetic housing and two
features.

### The help toggle

Every question has two layers, and a switch between them.

- **In character:** what the form actually asks. *"List any relatives who have served."*
- **Out of character:** what the question is for, and how to answer it well. *"This is asking who
  you are connected to in this city. A good answer names one person and one obligation, because
  that gives us somebody to bring back later."*

The toggle is per-player and remembered. A player who wants the form to stay a form leaves it off;
a player who would rather know what they are feeding the campaign turns it on. Nobody has to guess
what a question is really for, and nobody has the immersion broken who did not ask for it.

This is the same interaction as the scene runner's one-line-with-peekable-detail, deliberately —
one idiom, used twice.

### The alien detection, and the lockdown

A player who enters a non-terrestrial ancestry trips the screen.

The terminal stops. It goes into lockdown. It then wants to know, with the flat persistence of a
form that has all the time in the world, **how they have gone undetected for seventeen years** —
and it asks follow-up questions no other player is asked.

Three rules about this, because it is the kind of joke that goes wrong easily:

1. **It happens on that player's screen only.** The table sees one person go quiet. They do not see
   why, and the reveal stays the player's to make (§2). The module already has the machinery for a
   targeted, one-player push.
2. **It is funny, and it is not a punishment.** The lockdown is bureaucratic, not hostile — the
   institutional absurdity register the bible names as a comic vector. The system is not
   frightened. It is *filing*.
3. **The Directorate now has a record**, which is the founding sin operating exactly as designed,
   and Shucks Mordant has certainly seen this before. Two centuries of intake medicals is a long
   time to keep finding the same thing and saying nothing about it.

## 6. The two-aspect rule

**Every scene carries at least two things that matter.** Not one plant and some connective tissue —
two, and they should be different in kind. A joke and a plant. A relationship and a piece of
geography. A rules lesson and a thread breadcrumb.

A scene that carries one thing is a scene that should be half as long or merged with its neighbour.
This is worth enforcing in the data eventually — a scene brief could declare what it carries, and a
lint could flag any scene declaring fewer than two.

## 7. Writing the Watcher

*(Updated 2026-09-25: this section describes the Watcher's early stages, before it learns. Since LEDGER #31 it is
the campaign's adversary and it changes over the campaign; the current treatment, with all four stages and its later
voice, is `quarantine/ideas/story/dossier-observer.md`.)*

Ingram, 2026-09-09: *"it's a strange and curious mix of godlike and childlike intelligence."*

That is not a contradiction, it is the exact shape: **godlike perception, childlike framework.** It
counts every waking person in the harbour district and cannot stop. It fills the sky past the
horizon in every direction. And it does not know what crying is.

Enormous capability, no framework to hang it on. That is why it can be sympathetic and unsettling
in the same sentence.

**Three rules when writing it:**
- **Never malice.** It is never evil; it is an objective pursued without limit (`quarantine/ideas/story/dossier-observer.md` §6).
- **Scale in the perception, innocence in the conclusion.** It knows an impossible amount and draws
  a child's inference from it.
- **It has Manako's personality, not its own.** Read its behaviour backwards and it is a portrait of
  one grieving man. Natively it is a drowning, uncoordinated mess.

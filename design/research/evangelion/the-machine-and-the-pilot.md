# The Machine and the Pilot

*Part of the [Evangelion research shelf](README.md). **Mined for structure, not for register** — see [tone-guardrail.md](tone-guardrail.md). Synchronisation, the berserk state, and the thing inside the machine, as the series built them. **This campaign has no berserk and takes control of a character away from no player (`quarantine/ideas/story/bible/LEDGER.md` #31, D11).** **This is not a decision document and none of it is lore.** Claims are tagged **[S]** source, **[R]** reading, **[U]** suggested use.*

---

## 1. The relationship, as the series defines it

**[S]**

- The pilot sits inside the machine, in a flooded capsule, and the connection is **mental rather than mechanical**. Its quality is measured as a **synchronisation ratio**, reported aloud as a percentage by a technician during every operation.
- **The ratio moves with the pilot's state of mind.** It rises when they are settled and falls when they are not, and everyone in the control room watches it happen.
- **Damage to the machine is felt by the pilot.** The feedback is not metaphorical.
- The units run on an external cable with a hard limit — one minute at full power, five reduced ([operational-texture-and-the-sortie.md](operational-texture-and-the-sortie.md) §1).
- **The armour is restraints.** The units are living things, and the plating exists to hold them, not to protect them.
- Each contains **the soul of its pilot's dead mother**, which is the actual reason those specific children can pilot at all — the organisation's stated rule about age is a cover story.

## 2. The berserk state

**[S]** When a unit exceeds control it acts on its own: it ignores the power cable entirely, moves in ways no pilot commanded, and in its most famous sequence tears an enemy apart and eats it. The pilot is inside for all of it and does not decide any of it. Afterwards the unit is more dangerous than it was, having absorbed something from what it consumed.

**[R]** Two design facts are doing the work here.

1. **The loss of control is also the win condition.** Every prepared answer has failed, so the thing that saves everyone is the thing nobody sanctioned. That makes the victory frightening instead of triumphant.
2. **The cable stops mattering.** The series' most rigid constraint is revealed to be a constraint on *the pilot's control*, not on the machine — the power supply exists to keep the pilot in charge. **[R] That is an excellent piece of construction: the limitation the audience has accepted for twenty episodes turns out to have been a leash, and the reveal reframes every earlier fight.**

## 3. Why the relationship reads as a relationship

**[R]** Because the machine **occasionally disagrees**. It moves when it should not. It protects its pilot without being asked. The series never says outright that it loves him, and it does not have to — it is his mother, and the audience is allowed to work that out.

Compare the Giant Robo shelf's [inherited weapon](../giant-robo/characters/giant-robo.md): a machine that follows the *gist* of an order and carries a dead parent's will. **Two unrelated series, the same conclusion — a mecha becomes a character the moment it does slightly more than it was told, for a reason the audience can attribute to a person.** That convergence is the strongest single finding across both shelves.

## 4. What is portable **[U]**

- **A measured, publicly-read relationship value** that moves with the pilot's circumstances, and that other characters can see.
- **A hard operational limit that is later revealed to be about control rather than capability.**
- **Feedback damage** — the pilot is hurt by what hurts the machine — as a stated rule, not an occasional dramatic flourish.
- **The machine acting once, on its own, in the pilot's defence**, with no explanation offered. *(Ruled out here: LEDGER #31, D11 — nothing in this campaign takes control away from a player.)*
- **Armour as restraint** — a visual detail that reframes everything it appears on.

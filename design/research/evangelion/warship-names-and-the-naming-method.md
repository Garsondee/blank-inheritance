# Warship Names and the Naming Method

*Part of the [Evangelion research shelf](README.md). **Mined for structure, not for register** — see [tone-guardrail.md](tone-guardrail.md). Nearly every character is named after an Imperial Japanese Navy vessel, and the method behind that is directly usable. **This is not a decision document**; it names nobody in our setting and does not touch `data/campaign/naming-placeholders.json`. Claims are tagged **[S]** source, **[R]** reading, **[U]** suggested use.*

---

## 1. The convention

**[S]** Per the series' own character documentation: **most character names derive from Imperial Japanese Navy warships of the Second World War**, with the remainder taken from comic-book characters and from Ryu Murakami's novel *Ai to gensō no fascism* ("Love and Fantasy Fascism"). The naming is described as strategic — used to connect characters thematically.

**[S] Explicitly attributed in the source material:**

| Character | Named for |
|---|---|
| Kozo **Fuyutsuki** | The Akizuki-class destroyer *Fuyutsuki* |
| Makoto **Hyuga** | The battleship *Hyūga* (possibly also a Ryōtarō Shiba novel; Anno was unsure) |
| **Maya Ibuki** | **Two** cruisers — *Maya* and the 1943 *Ibuki* |
| Shigeru **Aoba** | The cruiser *Aoba*, with a pun on the 1974 film *Aoba Shigereru* |
| Kyoko **Zeppelin Soryu** | The carrier *Sōryū*, plus the German carrier *Graf Zeppelin*; her given name from a comic character |
| Toji **Suzuhara**, Kensuke **Aida**, Hikari **Horaki**, Shiro Tokita | Ryu Murakami's *Ai to gensō no fascism* |
| **Keel Lorenz** | The biologist Konrad Lorenz — he was "Konrad" in the original proposal |
| **Naoko** Akagi | An acquaintance of Anno's from primary school |

**[S] Two more naming systems run alongside it:**

- **Meaning-names for the thematically loaded pair.** *Yui* (唯, "only one") is set against *Rei* (零, "zero, nothingness"). The contrast is deliberate.
- **A second corpus for a second group.** The Horaki sisters — Kodama, Hikari and Nozomi — are named after **Tōkaidō Shinkansen train services**, which run slowest to fastest in that order. The ordinary family gets ordinary-world names from a timetable while the naval names go to the organisation.

**[R]** The other famous identifications — Ayanami (destroyer), Katsuragi (carrier), Akagi (carrier), and Ikari written with the character for a ship's anchor — are the standard readings of the same scheme and follow from the stated convention, but were not individually attributed in the articles read for this sweep. Treated here as consistent rather than as separately sourced.

## 2. Why it works **[R]**

1. **Coherence without effort.** A real ship register was already internally consistent when it was compiled. Names drawn from one produce a cast that sounds like it belongs together, and no individual name has to be invented or justified.
2. **It encodes rank and role for free.** Battleships, carriers, cruisers and destroyers carry different weights, and assigning them by a character's function in the organisation makes the roster legible to anyone who notices — and costs nothing to anyone who does not.
3. **A second corpus separates a second group.** The classmates come from a novel, the family from a railway timetable, the staff from a navy. **The naming scheme itself tells you which world a character belongs to.**
4. **The audience is not required to know.** Not one of these names needs decoding to work as a name. This is the same rule the Giant Robo shelf reaches from the other direction: [the borrowed name has to work cold](../giant-robo/legendary-sources-china-and-japan.md) §6.
5. **[R]** The one place it is slightly uncomfortable is worth saying plainly: the scheme draws on the naval register of a wartime navy, and the series is not making an argument about that. It is a sound bank. A project that reuses the method should choose a corpus it is willing to be asked about.

## 3. The method, in five steps **[U]**

1. **Choose one real corpus with many entries and internal coherence.** A ship register, a rail timetable, a street index, a plant list, a mountain range, a catalogue of tools, a list of decommissioned equipment.
2. **Map the corpus's own internal hierarchy onto your roster's roles.** The big steady one gets the big steady name.
3. **Use a second corpus for a second social world**, so the naming scheme itself does the sorting.
4. **Reserve meaning-names for the two or three characters carrying a theme**, and set them in opposition to each other as a pair.
5. **Test every name cold**, out loud, on someone who has never heard of the corpus. If it needs the footnote, it has failed.

## 4. A separate device worth stealing: two titles per episode

**[S]** Every episode carries **two title cards** — the original Japanese title and an English title chosen by the studio. The pairs are often very different in register: the plain *Kessen, daisan shin Tōkyō-shi* ("Decisive Battle, Third New Tokyo City") is shown alongside *Rei II*; the last episode's Japanese title is a long, strange phrase and its English card reads *Take care of yourself*.

**[R]** The effect is that each episode has an **operational name and a human name**, and the gap between the two is where the tone lives.

**[U]** This is immediately usable for session or chapter titles: an operational designation for the record — the kind of thing that would appear in an after-action report — and a human phrase underneath it. Our campaign already renders documents with real classification markings (`design/visual-identity.md`), so the operational half has somewhere to live. Compare the Giant Robo shelf's chapter-title rule — [name the person, the place or the confrontation, never the feeling](../giant-robo/series-shape-and-unmade-arcs.md) §3 — which governs the operational half well; the human half is where the feeling is permitted.

## 5. The contrast between the two shelves **[R]**

Worth holding side by side, because the shelves disagree productively:

- **Giant Robo names by reputation.** An epithet says what a person is known for: Shockwave Alberto, Silent Chujo. The name is a claim about the character, spoken aloud by other characters.
- **Evangelion names by register.** A surname says which world a person belongs to. The name is invisible to everyone inside the story and only legible from outside.

**[U]** They are compatible. A campaign can use a corpus for surnames and epithets for reputations, and get both the coherence and the theatre. If our setting ever adopts one, both halves are documented: [epithets](../giant-robo/epithets-and-name-calling.md) there, the corpus method here, and `data/campaign/naming-placeholders.json` as the register of what is still undecided.

# Borrowed Symbolism — Texture, Meaning, and Being Honest About Which

*Part of the [Evangelion research shelf](README.md). **Mined for structure, not for register** — see [tone-guardrail.md](tone-guardrail.md). The most argued-about aspect of the series, and a genuine craft question for any project that puts sigils on its institutions. **This is not a decision document and none of it is lore.** Claims are tagged **[S]** source, **[R]** reading, **[U]** suggested use.*

---

## 1. What is actually in there

**[S]** The series draws on mystical traditions in Judaism and Christianity, including Midrashic literature and Kabbalah: Adam, Lilith, Eve, the Lance of Longinus, the Dead Sea Scrolls, Adam Kadmon, the Tree of Life. The Human Instrumentality Project's ending has been compared to the Kabbalistic concept of *tikkun olam*. Alongside that run Shinto allusions — the *Kojiki* and *Nihongi*, the primordial cosmos, and the lances of Izanagi and Izanami. The Evangelions themselves resemble Japanese *oni* and have been compared to golems.

**[S]** Two institutional emblems carry it visually. The secret committee's logo is **seven eyes in an asymmetrical triangle** — the same symbol that appears on the mask of the being in the basement, with the implication that the logo was copied from it. The operating organisation's logo is **a fig leaf**, carrying the motto *God's in his heaven — all's right with the world*, a line of Robert Browning.

## 2. What the staff said

**[S]** Assistant director Kazuya Tsurumaki said the religious visual references were used to make the series more interesting and exotic for a Japanese audience, denying a religious meaning behind the Christian symbols.

**[S]** Anno's position is not the same. He said that **as the symbols are mixed together, for the first time something like an interrelationship or a meaning emerges.**

**[S]** The scholar Gavin McDowell has noted that Tsurumaki's remark is usually quoted out of context in fandom, and does not imply the individual references are meaningless.

**[R]** So the honest summary is neither of the two versions people usually repeat. It is not "the crosses mean nothing", and it is not "there is a decoded theology in here". It is: **the imagery was chosen for its look, applied consistently, and acquired meaning through use.** That is a real and legitimate way for symbolism to work, and it is worth naming because it is also the way most game settings actually acquire theirs.

## 3. The craft question

**[R]** Three positions, and the case for each.

1. **Symbolism as pure texture.** Chosen for atmosphere, no decoding intended. Cheap, effective, and honest as long as nobody claims otherwise. The risk: an audience that starts decoding will not stop, and an invitation you did not mean to issue is still an invitation.
2. **Symbolism as meaning.** Every element decodes, consistently, to something the work is saying. Expensive. Rewarding when done. Fatal when half-done, because a system that decodes 70% of the way teaches the audience to expect the other 30%.
3. **Symbolism as accumulated meaning.** Anno's position. Chosen for texture, applied rigorously, and allowed to become meaningful through repetition and juxtaposition. **[R] This is the only one of the three that works without being planned in advance**, and it requires exactly one discipline: consistency.

**[U] The rule that follows:** a symbol may be decorative, but it must be **consistent**, because players decode. If the same mark appears on a uniform, a door and a document, the players will assume the three are connected — and they should be right. The cost of consistency is a list; the cost of inconsistency is every deduction the players make being wrong, which is worse than having no symbols at all.

## 4. The specific trap

**[R]** Borrowed profundity, named in [tone-guardrail.md](tone-guardrail.md) §5.4: ancient imagery looks meaningful because it is ancient. A project can acquire an unearned appearance of depth by using it, and the people most likely to be fooled are the people making the thing.

**[U]** The test is simple and worth running: **take the symbol out and see whether anything changes.** If the scene, the institution or the scene's meaning is unaffected, the symbol is decoration — which is allowed, as long as it is filed as decoration and not as theme.

**[R]** A second, quieter caution. Using a living religious tradition's iconography as exotic set-dressing reads differently to people inside that tradition than to people outside it. The series did it, took criticism for it, and the staff's own explanation is the one quoted in §2. A project reusing the method should choose material it is willing to be asked about, and should expect to be asked. `design/japanese-narrative-conventions.md` makes the same point about using a culture's forms rather than wearing them.

## 5. For this campaign **[U]**

Our project already has an unusually rigorous visual identity — one CSS identity across the module, the system and our own interface, in a 1950s military-technical register, with real Japanese classification markings on documents (`design/visual-identity.md`, and the standing directive behind it). That is precisely the position where this question becomes live:

- **Decide, per mark, whether it decodes.** Keep a short list: this sigil means an organisation, this stamp means a clearance level, this shape is decorative. The list is the whole discipline.
- **The Encyclopaedia-check pattern raises the stakes.** `design/encyclopaedia-checks.md` gives players an optional insight roll that always succeeds at being offered and never punishes failure. **If a player can roll to decode a symbol, the symbol must decode to something.** A campaign with a decoding mechanic cannot afford purely decorative iconography in the places that mechanic can point at.
- **An emblem copied from something found in a basement** — the committee's logo taken from the thing it worships — is a strong, cheap device: the institution's branding is a piece of evidence. Whether our institutions have anything like it is a story-bible question and is not decided here.

# Operational Texture and the Sortie

*Part of the [Evangelion research shelf](README.md). **Mined for structure, not for register** — see [tone-guardrail.md](tone-guardrail.md). The procedural detail that makes a fantastical organisation feel like a real one with a budget. **This is not a decision document and none of it is lore.** Claims are tagged **[S]** source, **[R]** reading, **[U]** suggested use.*

---

## 1. The constraints the series invented for itself

**[S]** None of these is a plot device. They are standing rules, stated early and obeyed thereafter.

- **The umbilical cable.** An Eva runs on external power delivered by a cable. Cut off from it, it has **one minute at full power, five in a reduced mode.** An early episode has a pilot physically swapping his own power cord to reach an area the current one will not stretch to.
- **The launch.** Units are catapulted from the underground base to street level in seconds.
- **The city is the weapon.** Tokyo-3's buildings retract into the ground on command, clearing firing lines and exposing the armoured cavity beneath. The fortress is the terrain, and it is operated.
- **Conventional forces exist and are useless.** The military fields tanks, aircraft and **N2 mines** — enormous non-nuclear bombs. They are used first, every time, and they never work. The first one damages a main character's car, and she frets about the three years of payments still owed on it.
- **Synchronisation is a published number.** A technician reads out each pilot's sync ratio as a percentage. It rises and falls with their state of mind, everyone in the control room hears it, and it is discussed like a performance review.
- **Everything is awesome and impractical.** The series is explicit about this: the Evas are the most powerful thing humanity has and can barely operate for five minutes, with unpredictable faults nobody fully understands.

**[R]** The cumulative effect is that **victory is a resource-management outcome rather than a power outcome.** The question in a fight is never "can we win" — it is "for how long can we afford to be here".

## 2. Operation Yashima — the best planning episode in the genre

**[S]** An Angel that automatically fires a particle beam at anything approaching cannot be attacked conventionally. The answer is an engineering project: a positron rifle, a single shot, fired from a mountain at extreme range, requiring **the electrical output of the entire country rerouted to one gun**, with a second unit holding a shield to cover the shooter. The shot is planned, rehearsed, and taken once.

**[R]** Everything good about this is copyable and none of it requires the setting:

1. **The enemy's gimmick is the mission statement.** The obstacle defines the plan, so the plan is a solution rather than a procedure.
2. **The cost is national and legible.** An entire country's lights go out. The players can picture the bill.
3. **The support cast has jobs**: the engineers, the power authority, the person holding the shield.
4. **There is one shot.** A single attempt converts a fight into a held breath.
5. **[R] It is a fight in which the mecha does almost nothing but aim.** Which is precisely why it is remembered as the best one.

## 3. The bureaucracy, played straight and funny

**[S]** During an imminent nuclear emergency — a rival organisation's unmanned mech running out of control — the operations captain needs authorisation to issue a shutdown command. She goes through a **five-person chain of command**, who eventually agree to grant her clearance **effective on arrival of the paperwork.** She says, in effect, forget it, deploys a unit to hold the runaway mech still, climbs onto its back and enters the shutdown code by hand.

**[S]** That runaway mech, Jet Alone, was a competing programme — a nuclear-powered unmanned robot built by a rival organisation as an alternative to NERV. It is implied NERV sabotaged it so that the competitor could not be used as a point of comparison.

**[R]** Two of the most useful institutional beats available, and both are warm enough for our tone:

- **The authorisation chain as an obstacle**, in which the correct answer is a competent officer deciding to be in trouble later. That is a heroic act made entirely of paperwork.
- **The rival programme.** A competitor for the same budget, with a worse product and better politics, is an antagonist who never fights anybody. It also gives the players' organisation something to lose that is not a battle.

## 4. The replacement threat

**[S]** The organisation develops a **dummy system** — an autonomous control package that operates a unit without its pilot. It is used once, over the objection of a technician who considers it inhuman, to force a unit to destroy an infected one with a friendly pilot still inside.

**[R]** The pilots' employer has built, and is willing to use, the thing that makes them unnecessary. As a standing background fact this does more work than any villain speech: it tells the players exactly how their organisation values them, without anyone saying it.

## 5. For this campaign **[U]**

The Directorate is currently a faction file and three unnamed poles, and `design/strategy-layer.md` already models the campaign above the session as five tracks with Clearance rungs and an Observer's turn. Suggestions, all narrow:

- **Deployment should cost something stated and visible each time.** The Giant Robo shelf reaches the same conclusion from the other direction ([the inherited weapon](../giant-robo/characters/giant-robo.md) §4). Two independent sources agreeing is the strongest signal on either shelf.
- **The invoice.** Damage caused by the party's own operations should occasionally arrive as a bill, a complaint, or a repair queue. This is the single cheapest way to make a city feel inhabited, and it is funny, which suits our register.
- **A rival programme** competing for the same budget is a ready-made strategy-layer track. Whether the strategy layer gains one is the user's call and `design/strategy-layer.md`'s business, not this file's.
- **A publicly-read performance number.** Our campaign has Resonance bands, which are already public-facing in a comparable way — **but see [the-machine-and-the-pilot.md](the-machine-and-the-pilot.md) §5 before drawing any line between the two systems**, because that comparison touches live open questions.
- **The authorisation chain** belongs in the Clearance rungs that `design/strategy-layer.md` already defines. Using an existing rung as an obstacle costs no new system.

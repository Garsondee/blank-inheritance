# Kaworu Nagisa (渚カヲル)

*The Fifth Child; the seventeenth Angel · unconditional acceptance, arriving late, from the wrong side. Part of the [Evangelion research shelf](../README.md). **Research, not lore — this character does not exist in our setting, and nothing here decides anything. Mined for structure, not for register** ([tone-guardrail.md](../tone-guardrail.md)). Tags: **[S]** source, **[R]** reading, **[U]** suggested use.*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Role** | Sent as the replacement pilot of Unit-02 after Asuka's collapse. **He is the final Angel, Tabris** |
| **Born** | 13 September 2000 — the day of Second Impact |
| **Design** | Anno called him his Jungian shadow. Originally conceived as a cat controlling a boy |
| **Appearances** | **One episode** of the television series |
| **Fate** | Asks the protagonist to kill him, and is killed |

## 1. What the series gives us **[S]**

- He arrives, sits down next to the most isolated person in the story, and is **immediately, completely, unconditionally kind to him.** He says so out loud. Nothing is asked in return.
- He is an outstanding pilot, effortlessly.
- He is the last Angel. He infiltrates the base looking for the first Angel, finds something else in the basement, and stops.
- **Having understood what he is and what continuing would mean, he asks the protagonist to kill him** — and explains that this is the only outcome in which anything survives. The protagonist holds him for a long time and then does it.
- **[S]** He appears in a single episode and has repeatedly topped popularity polls. The homoerotic reading of the relationship was controversial at the time and is now generally treated as the point.
- **[S]** Later adaptations move him much earlier, explicitly because of that popularity.

## 2. Why he works **[R]**

- **He is the only person in the series who gives without conditions**, in a story where every other relationship is transactional, institutional or damaged. The contrast is the entire effect, and it takes about four minutes to establish.
- **The reveal does not retroactively make him a manipulator.** This is the crucial construction. He is an enemy and he meant every word. Learning what he is changes *what he is*, not *what he meant* — which is why the episode is devastating rather than a betrayal plot.
- **He hands the protagonist an impossible choice and then takes responsibility for it**, which is the opposite of what the story's authority figures do.
- **[R] One episode is enough.** He is on screen for roughly forty minutes of a twenty-six-episode series and is one of its two or three most remembered characters. **The lesson is about concentration: a character with one clear function, given one uninterrupted scene, will outperform a recurring character with none.**

## 3. The archetype **[U]**

**Name:** *The Late Arrival Who Accepts You.* Someone who appears near the end, offers uncomplicated warmth to the party's most isolated member, and turns out to belong to the other side.

**The one rule:** **His kindness must be genuine and must remain genuine after the reveal.** If it was a tactic, the archetype collapses into an ordinary infiltration plot. He meant it; he is also the enemy; both stay true.

**Voice:** calm, warm, unhurried, slightly formal. Says the direct emotional thing without embarrassment, which unbalances everyone around him. Talks about large ideas plainly. Never defensive.

**Audition lines** (written for this shelf, not quotations):

- *On meeting:* "You looked like you wanted someone to sit down. So I sat down. That's the whole of it."
- *Unconditional:* "I'm not going to want anything from you. I know you're waiting for the part where I do. There isn't one."
- *The reveal:* "Yes. I'm one of them. I've been one of them the entire time we've been talking, and everything I said was true."
- *The request:* "One of us has to, and you're the one who can live with it afterwards. I'm sorry. That's a real apology, not a kind one."

## 4. For Blank Inheritance **[U]**

**Worth taking:** the enemy whose affection is sincere; the concentration principle — one function, one uninterrupted scene; an ally who hands a PC a decision and takes responsibility for having handed it over.

**Worth avoiding:** using the shape to justify a betrayal twist. The archetype only works if nothing was faked. **A version where the kindness was a ploy is a different and much weaker character**, and players can tell the difference immediately.

**Where it might attach:** the Giant Robo shelf's antagonist material is full of honourable enemies, and this is the intimate version of the same idea — an enemy the party likes, met individually rather than across a battlefield. If our antagonists ever acquire a code (see [that shelf's chairman archetype](../../giant-robo/characters/hanzui.md)), this is the character who walks out of it and sits down next to somebody. **Not proposed for our setting; offered as a shape.**

# Withheld Information, and the Ending Problem

*Part of the [Evangelion research shelf](README.md). **Mined for structure, not for register** — see [tone-guardrail.md](tone-guardrail.md). How the series withholds, where that works, and the famous failure at the end. This is the shelf's cautionary tale, and the counterpart to the Giant Robo shelf's four-pass reveal. **This is not a decision document and none of it is lore.** Claims are tagged **[S]** source, **[R]** reading, **[U]** suggested use.*

---

## 1. What the series does well

**[S/R]** Four techniques, all sound.

1. **Different characters hold different pieces.** The commander knows one thing, his deputy another, the scientist a third, the investigator a fourth, and the children almost nothing. No single conversation could assemble the truth, which is a structural reason for secrecy rather than an authorial one.
2. **A stated operational rule is later revealed as a cover story.** The organisation says only children born after the disaster can pilot. It is true in practice and false in explanation — the real reason is inside the machines. **[R] Revealing that a rule the audience has been *using* was a lie is far stronger than revealing a fact they were merely told.**
3. **A character is planted whose whole function is to find out.** The investigator is working for three masters at once and spends the series prying. When he finally learns something, he passes it to one person and is killed for it. **[R] That is how to make an audience feel that the truth is worth something: charge somebody for it.**
4. **The questions are posed early and concretely.** What happened at the disaster, what is in the basement, what the committee wants. Specific questions, asked out loud, by characters who want answers.

## 2. Where it fails, and exactly how

**[S]** The final two episodes abandoned the planned conclusion under time pressure and turned inward — abstract animation, still frames, line drawings and voice-over, staged inside the characters' minds. The criticism was directed mainly at **the lack of storyline resolution**. The *Mainichi Times* reported that after the penultimate episode nearly all viewers felt betrayed. A complaint from the commentator Eiji Ōtsuka prompted a nationwide debate. *The End of Evangelion* was made afterwards to provide a conventional narrative conclusion, though the staff maintain its tone was always the intention.

**[S]** A related habit: substantial backstory was pushed into guidebooks and a PlayStation 2 game, with the canonicity of that material still debated.

**[R]** Two distinct failures, worth separating because only one is about the ending.

- **The plot was replaced rather than concluded.** Interiority is not an answer to a question about events. An audience holding a specific question — what is in the basement, what the committee wanted — is not satisfied by a character achieving self-acceptance, however well done.
- **The answers moved outside the work.** If the explanation lives in a guidebook, the work has not explained itself. **[U] For a campaign, the equivalent is worse than it sounds: an answer that exists only in the GM's notes and is never reachable in play did not exist.**

## 3. The table-level problem

**[R]** A director can refuse to answer. **A GM cannot**, for one specific reason: the players will ask, directly, in character, repeatedly, and the GM has to say something every time.

This is the same failure the Giant Robo shelf identifies in its own strategist — a character who refuses to explain, at length, while events proceed, is unplayable at a table because a GM answering "he won't say" four times has stopped running a scene. **Two very different series produced the same problem, which suggests it is a property of the technique rather than of either work.**

**[U] The fix already documented, restated:** the withholding character gives up **one true thing per encounter**. They choose which true thing. It can be the one that hurts most. The mystery survives; the wall does not.

## 4. The four-pass alternative

**[U]** The Giant Robo shelf's [buried atrocity](../giant-robo/the-buried-atrocity.md) documents the better structure in detail: the same event explained four times, each pass a **correction of interpretation** rather than a contradiction of fact, so nothing earlier becomes false — only incomplete.

The difference between the two series is precise and worth holding on to:

| | Evangelion | Giant Robo |
|---|---|---|
| Questions posed | Early, specific, repeatedly | Early, specific, repeatedly |
| Information released | Late, partial, some never | In four staged passes |
| The final pass | Replaced by interiority | Delivers the actual answer |
| Audience result | Debate, and a film made to fix it | A reveal that reframes the whole story |

**[R]** Both series withhold. Only one of them pays.

## 5. Rules for a campaign **[U]**

1. **Every question you pose gets an answer that is reachable in play.** Write it down when you pose it. Both shelves independently reach this rule; it is the single most repeated instruction across the two.
2. **Make the answer cost somebody something.** An investigator who is hurt for finding out is the cheapest way to make a fact feel valuable.
3. **Distribute the pieces across factions**, so secrecy has an in-world cause.
4. **Reveal a rule as a cover story at least once.** The players have been relying on it; the reframe is free and enormous.
5. **Never answer a question about events with a scene about feelings.** Both are good; they are not interchangeable.
6. **Nothing lives only in the notes.** `design/objectives.md`'s accumulating log and `design/npc-social-intel.md`'s Facts-with-reveal-triggers are where a planned answer becomes a tracked one.

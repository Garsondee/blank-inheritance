# The Tone Guardrail — What Not To Take

*Part of the [Evangelion research shelf](README.md), and the governing document of it. **Mined for structure, not for register.** Read this before any other file here. **This is not a decision document and none of it is lore.** Claims are tagged **[S]** source, **[R]** reading, **[U]** suggested use.*

---

## 1. Why this shelf exists at all

**[S]** The campaign's tone is locked to Giant Robo, by a standing rule: fun anime, warm, funny, uplifting; comedy is load-bearing; dark is allowed, **depressing is a fault**. That rule names this series as the thing the campaign is *not*.

**[R]** So a sweep of it is only worth doing for one reason: **Giant Robo has structural gaps that this series fills exactly.** The Giant Robo shelf's own audit lists them — no peer rival of the PCs' own age, no school life or teachers, no named civilians, no institution whose treatment of the children is the subject, and no relationships that degrade. Evangelion is the canonical source for every one of those, and it is better at the operational and encounter-design detail than almost anything else in the genre.

**The two shelves do different jobs. Giant Robo decides how it feels. Evangelion supplies things to put in it.** Neither of those sentences is negotiable in the other direction.

## 2. What the register actually is, and where it came from

**[S]** The production facts matter, because they explain the tone as a circumstance rather than as a target.

- Anno began development in 1993 after a depression following *Nadia* and a collapsed film project, and conceived the series as **a metaphor of his own four-year depression**, deliberately imprinting his feelings into it. He has said all the characters are aspects of himself.
- The schedule was broken from the start: twelve episodes done in two years, the remaining fourteen needed in about a third of that time, with only three Gainax staff on it at a time.
- **The turn is locatable.** Up to roughly episode 13 the series is a monster-of-the-week mecha show with jokes and comic relief. From episode 16 it discards the grand narrative for an exploration of the cast's psyches, after Anno became interested in psychology when a friend lent him a book on mental illness.
- Episodes 25–26 abandoned the planned finale entirely under time pressure, using still frames, line drawings and voice-over. *The End of Evangelion* was made afterwards as the theatrical conclusion, and the staff maintain its dark and violent tone was the intention from the outset rather than a reaction to the backlash.

**[R]** Read that list again as a project record rather than as an artistic statement. Much of what the series is famous for is **a schedule collapse and a director's illness, retroactively canonised as style.** That is not a criticism of the work, which is genuinely great. It is a reason not to treat its shape as a model.

## 3. The quarantine list

**[U]** Specific things not to import. Each is an instruction, not a suggestion.

1. **The despair as a destination.** The series ends with its cast at their worst and calls that honesty. Our campaign may put characters at their worst; it may not leave them there and call it an ending.
2. **Reluctance as the engine.** The protagonist spends the series not wanting to do the thing the show is about. **At a table this is actively harmful** — a PC who does not want to be there stops the session, and a GM who builds a campaign around coaxing a reluctant protagonist has built a campaign about arguing. Import the *cost* of the job; do not import the refusal to do it.
3. **Punishing characters for connecting.** The hedgehog's dilemma is the series' thesis: closeness injures, so people withdraw. [relationships-that-degrade.md](relationships-that-degrade.md) explains what is worth taking from it — the mechanism, not the verdict. A campaign where reaching out reliably makes things worse teaches players to stop reaching out.
4. **Adults who all fail.** Virtually every adult in the series is compromised, absent or using the children. **Our campaign needs warm, competent adults**, and the Giant Robo shelf supplies them by the dozen. Take this series' institutional realism and that series' people.
5. **Withdrawal from plot into interiority.** See [withheld-information-and-the-ending-problem.md](withheld-information-and-the-ending-problem.md). A table cannot be given an internal monologue instead of an answer.
6. **The sexualisation of minors.** The series contains it; several of its most-criticised moments are exactly this. **It is not imported in any form.** No exceptions, no "in the style of".
7. **Suicide, self-harm and psychological breakdown as texture.** The series uses all three repeatedly — a mother's suicide discovered by her child, a mind-attack that induces reliving trauma, a catatonic pilot. These are legitimate subjects and this campaign is not forbidden them, but they are **content decisions for the user and the table, made in advance with safety tools in place** (`design/ttrpg-campaign-and-session-craft.md` covers the tools and why this material's weight makes them necessary). They are never atmosphere.

## 5. Four ways this material leaks

**[R]** Named so they can be watched for.

1. **Osmosis.** Reading a lot of this changes how you write for a week — sentences get shorter, scenes get colder, characters stop being funny. **Mitigation: after a writing session sourced from this shelf, re-read [the Giant Robo ham-and-sincerity file](../giant-robo/ham-and-sincerity.md) before drafting player-facing prose.**
2. **The misery ratchet.** Each session lands slightly darker than the last, because darker feels more serious, and nothing pulls it back. `design/pacing-and-the-responsive-campaign.md` already names this as a live risk for our campaign and gives the correct fix: **the relax beat is a scheduled part of the sawtooth, not the leftover space between crises.**
3. **Weight mistaken for quality.** A scene that hurts is not automatically a better scene. Ask what the table got to *do* in it.
4. **Borrowed profundity.** The series' symbols look meaningful because they are ancient, not because they were chosen to mean anything — the staff said so. See [borrowed-symbolism.md](borrowed-symbolism.md). Decoration is fine as long as nobody, including us, mistakes it for depth.

## 6. The check to run

**[U]** After drafting anything sourced from this shelf, ask two questions in order:

1. **What did the players get to do in this scene?** If the answer is "witness", rewrite it.
2. **Would someone enjoy having played it?** Not "would it be powerful" — enjoy. If no, the scene belongs to the other shelf, or to no shelf at all.

**[R]** That is the whole guardrail in practice. Everything else in this document is an example of it.

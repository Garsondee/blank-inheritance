# The Enemy as Puzzle — Sixteen Angels, Sixteen Solutions

*Part of the [Evangelion research shelf](README.md). **Mined for structure, not for register** — see [tone-guardrail.md](tone-guardrail.md). The single most directly useful thing on this shelf: a complete encounter-design catalogue in which no enemy has the same gimmick and no fight is won the same way twice. **This is not a decision document and none of it is lore.** Claims are tagged **[S]** source, **[R]** reading, **[U]** suggested use.*

---

## 1. The design pattern, stated

**[S]** Every Angel shares exactly one property: an **AT Field**, a barrier that conventional weapons cannot penetrate and that must be neutralised before anything can hurt the core. Beyond that shared rule, each one is different: different body plan, different capability, different approach, different weakness. They never speak. They have no stated motive beyond reaching Lilith. Production material notes their designs deliberately avoid consistent anthropomorphism.

**[R]** That is the whole pattern, and it is worth stating as a rule because it is portable to any system:

> **One universal rule that makes the enemy class a class, plus one unique gimmick per individual that makes every encounter a different problem.**

The shared rule does the work of making them feel like a species and gives the players one thing to master. The unique gimmicks mean mastery is never sufficient. **[R]** This is a much better engine than "the next one is stronger", which is the default in the genre and what most campaigns drift into.

## 2. The catalogue

**[S]** In order, with the gimmick, the solution, and the transferable lesson. Numbering follows the series' own.

| 12 | **Leliel** | A vast sphere whose visible body is a shadow; inside is an inverted AT Field, a pocket of imaginary space that swallows an Eva whole | The trapped pilot survives on dwindling air until an escape is forced from inside | **The enemy that removes a PC from the board.** The objective stops being victory and becomes survival and retrieval |

| 17 | **Tabris** | A boy. Arrives as a replacement pilot, befriends the protagonist, and is the final Angel | He asks to be killed, and is | **The last enemy is a conversation.** The fight is a decision, and the decision is a person's |

## 3. What the catalogue proves

**[R]**

1. **No solution repeats.** Across sixteen encounters the winning move is, in turn: a demonstration, a knife, a national-scale engineering project, a naval ambush, six days of rehearsal, a capture under hazard, a blackout firefight, a three-unit intercept, a software counter-attack, a survival ordeal, a command decision with a human cost, an unsanctioned loss of control, an expended irreplaceable weapon, a self-sacrifice, and a conversation. **A campaign that manages even half that variety will never feel repetitive.**
2. **The gimmick is often a circumstance, not a creature.** Matarael is an ordinary enemy that arrives during a power cut. Bardiel is dangerous because of *where* it is. The cheapest way to make a new encounter is to change the conditions, not the stat block.
3. **Several fights are won by people who are not the protagonists.** The science staff beat Ireul; the navy kills Gaghiel; the command staff decide Bardiel. In a campaign with a large NPC cast this is how the supporting characters earn their place.
4. **The enemies never explain themselves.** No dialogue, no motive, no negotiation — until the last one, who is all three at once, which is why it lands.
5. **[R] The honest limit.** This works because the series has a finite, prophesied number of enemies, and everyone in-universe knows the count. A campaign that wants this shape should consider telling the players the number too. A known, finite enemy list turns each encounter into a chapter of a countable story.

## 4. For this campaign **[U]**

Our antagonist material is currently build rules rather than a bestiary — `data/antagonists/claimants/claimant-guidelines.json` describes how a Claimant is constructed and no individual Claimants exist yet. That is the right moment to take this pattern seriously, and the suggestion is narrow:

- **Define the one universal property** every Claimant shares, which the players learn once and rely on forever. This is a setting and rules question that belongs to the user (the Watcher's children, `quarantine/ideas/story/dossier-observer.md`), not here.
- **Give each individual exactly one gimmick**, and write the intended solution down at the same time — mirroring the rule in [the Giant Robo antagonist engine](../giant-robo/the-recurring-antagonist-engine.md) §3 about writing the counter before the first appearance.
- **Rotate the solution type deliberately.** Keep a list of which kinds of solution have been used — planning, rehearsal, terrain, support staff, sacrifice, conversation — and do not repeat one twice in a row.
- **Design at least one encounter the Frames cannot solve at all**, where the ground-layer kids or the support staff win it. The campaign already has the machinery for a personnel-scale fight (`data/subsystems/day-1-ground-layer.json`, and the ground-layer simulator) — this is the encounter shape that makes it matter.

**[U]** Anything that changes what a Claimant *is* touches live design; this file proposes a method for populating a bestiary, not the bestiary.

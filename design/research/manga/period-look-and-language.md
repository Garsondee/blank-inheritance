# The period look and language: youth magazines and recruitment material, c. 1950–1963

*Part of the [manga research shelf](README.md), gathered 2026-09-26 for [`design/propaganda-manga.md`](../../propaganda-manga.md). **Research, not lore, and not a decision:** it says what the sources say and what might transfer, and the plan decides. Every factual claim links its source; **(unverified)** marks what could not be confirmed. It names real works, people and places because it is GM-facing; none of them belongs in the comic.*

Research note for the in-universe recruitment manga (a front cover and at most four pages, in Japanese and English). Every factual claim links a source. Anything I could not confirm is marked **(unverified)**. My recommendations are labelled as recommendations, not findings.

**Project caution:** this note names real magazines, artists and years so the GM can decide things. None of those names belong in the finished manga, because players will read it.

## Summary

- **The nearest real model** is a B5 monthly children's magazine from about 1956 to 1961 ([少年 (雑誌)](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%B0%91%E5%B9%B4_(%E9%9B%91%E8%AA%8C)), [少女ブック](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%B0%91%E5%A5%B3%E3%83%96%E3%83%83%E3%82%AF)).
  - The cover often carried a photograph of a real person, plus a count of the free gifts ([なかよし](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%81%AA%E3%81%8B%E3%82%88%E3%81%97), [Kaiju Korner on furoku](http://kaijukorner.blogspot.com/2014/05/furoku-part-1.html)). How common painted covers were is **(unverified)**.
  - Inside came some colour and two-colour pages, then black ink on cheap paper ([復刊ドットコム](https://www.fukkan.com/fk/CartSearchDetail?i_no=68320851), [OPTPiX Labs blog](https://www.webtech.co.jp/blog/k1/7485/)).
- **Manga pages** used regular rows of panels ([Theisen](https://comicsforum.org/2014/10/08/manga-studies-5-takeuchi-osamu-and-manga-expression-pt-1-tezuka-osamu-as-manga-locus-by-nicholas-theisen/)), a mostly straight-on camera, rounded characters descended from Disney and very little screentone. Speech was set in metal type: mincho at first, then antique-style kana with gothic kanji. That mix became standard in big publishers' children's magazines around 1960 ([劇画](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%8A%87%E7%94%BB), [TCJ on Tezuka](https://www.tcj.com/tezuka-osamu-and-american-comics/), [Meiji University screentone archive](https://www.meiji.ac.jp/manga/yonezawa_lib/archives/exh-ic/exh-ic_archive.html), [アンチゴチ](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%82%A2%E3%83%B3%E3%83%81%E3%82%B4%E3%83%81)).
- **Girls' manga** invented the tools for a bubbly heroine inside this period: star-lit eyes, and the full-length "style drawing" that cuts down through the panel rows, first used in January 1958 ([Meiji shōjo exhibition](https://www.meiji.ac.jp/manga/yonezawa_lib/exh_shoujomanga/corner03.html)).
- **A young woman as the face of a paramilitary force is ahead of the real record.** Women served in Japan's defence forces as nurses from 1952 and appeared heavily on recruiting posters only from the 1970s. The reasoning behind it is documented, though: women were used to make the force look unlike the old army ([自衛官](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E8%87%AA%E8%A1%9B%E5%AE%98), [nippon.com, Satō Fumika](https://www.nippon.com/ja/in-depth/d00838/)).
- **The 1946 script reforms** limited kanji, simplified kana spelling and discouraged furigana. Children's print kept furigana anyway. Several period words also differ from today's in small, telling ways ([当用漢字表](https://ja.wikisource.org/wiki/%E5%BD%93%E7%94%A8%E6%BC%A2%E5%AD%97%E8%A1%A8), [振り仮名](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%8C%AF%E3%82%8A%E4%BB%AE%E5%90%8D)).

### 1.2 From general magazine to manga magazine

In this period a "manga magazine" was really a general-interest magazine that contained manga.

- **なかよし** was built on three pillars: picture stories, photo features and manga. Manga filled half its pages only by November 1958 ([なかよし](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%81%AA%E3%81%8B%E3%82%88%E3%81%97)). It was aimed at girls in the third to fifth grades of primary school and designed to be highly visual, because television was spreading ([gendai.media](https://gendai.media/articles/-/143239?page=2)).
- **りぼん** opened with photo pages, articles on fashion and hobbies, and manga. It became recognisably a manga magazine around 1958 ([りぼん](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%82%8A%E3%81%BC%E3%82%93)).
- **The two weeklies launched on the same day.** Magazine cost 40 yen and had the sumo wrestler Asashio on the cover. It carried only five manga; more than 60% of its pages were fiction, news, sport and science ([週刊少年マガジン](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E9%80%B1%E5%88%8A%E5%B0%91%E5%B9%B4%E3%83%9E%E3%82%AC%E3%82%B8%E3%83%B3)).
  - Sunday cost 30 yen and had the baseball star Nagashima on the cover. Its first issue outsold Magazine by 300,000 copies to 205,000, and Magazine cut its price by its fifth issue ([週刊少年サンデー](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E9%80%B1%E5%88%8A%E5%B0%91%E5%B9%B4%E3%82%B5%E3%83%B3%E3%83%87%E3%83%BC)).

### 1.3 Covers, format, colour and paper

**Covers.** なかよし used photographs of child actresses on its covers until about 1969. Illustrations took over from October 1969, and manga artists drew the covers only from August 1971 ([なかよし](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%81%AA%E3%81%8B%E3%82%88%E3%81%97)). Both weeklies launched with sports stars on their covers (sources above). 少年 still had a baseball player on its cover in December 1965 ([manga-bookshelf](https://manga-bookshelf.com/category/%E5%B0%91%E5%B9%B4%E9%9B%91%E8%AA%8C/)).

A real-looking person, photographed or painted, was therefore the period norm for many covers. A manga character on the cover came later. How the 1950s boys' monthlies divided painted covers from photographic ones is **(unverified)**.

**Format.** B5 was the monthly trim size: 少年 moved to B5 in 1954, and 少女ブック was the first B5 girls' magazine (table sources). Manga free gifts often came as small separate booklets. One 1964 example was a 116-page B6 booklet ([manga-bookshelf](https://manga-bookshelf.com/category/%E5%B0%91%E5%B9%B4%E9%9B%91%E8%AA%8C/)).

**Colour.** Two-colour pages (black plus one vermilion ink) gave colour for less than four-colour printing, and artists found ways to get extra shades from the two inks ([OPTPiX Labs blog](https://www.webtech.co.jp/blog/k1/7485/)). This happens to match the campaign's vermilion-and-black official printing exactly.

- **Colour inside 1950s serials is documented.** The "colour complete" reprint of Princess Knight reproduces its colour pages exactly as the magazine printed them ([復刊ドットコム](https://www.fukkan.com/fk/CartSearchDetail?i_no=68320851)).
- **How widespread two-colour pages were in 1950s children's monthlies in particular is (unverified).**

**Paper.** The coloured paper now linked with manga magazines arrived in about 1965–74. It hid the greyness of recycled pulp, and printing black on several tinted papers was cheaper than using several coloured inks ([NDL reference database](https://crd.ndl.go.jp/reference/entry/index.php?id=1000165895&page=ref_view)). Pink or yellow sections would therefore be a late touch for a 1950s look.

A hobbyist guide to imitating 1955–64 manga recommends a blue-black ink cast and yellowed paper ([Y氏は暇人](https://y-ta.net/syowa30-manga/)). Whether that was the general rule is **(unverified)**.

### 1.4 Free gifts (付録) and reader pages

**Gifts were an arms race.**

- 少年's February 1951 issue carried 15 gifts, and 少年ブック's January 1962 issue carried 18 ([Kaiju Korner](http://kaijukorner.blogspot.com/2014/05/furoku-part-1.html)).
- A 1961 manga magazine advertised ten gifts on its cover (same source).
- Astro Boy got its first gift booklet in August 1953 (same source). Tetsujin 28 started life in a separate gift booklet in July 1956 ([鉄人28号](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E9%89%84%E4%BA%BA28%E5%8F%B7)).
- Gifts included paper toys, craft kits, games and masks (Kaiju Korner). Girls' magazines added brooches, hairbands and stationery. Gifts designed by Naitō Rune in the mid-1950s were said to sell issues on their own ([Japanese Kawaii Culture](https://japanesekawaiiculture.com/furoku-the-fun-and-fabulous-world-of-japan-magazine-freebies/)).

**Episodes were short.** 少年's August 1958 issue ran eight serials of 7–15 pages each. Tetsujin 28 had eight pages in the magazine plus a separate booklet ([manga-bookshelf, 少年 1958年8月号](https://manga-bookshelf.com/%E3%80%8E%E5%B0%91%E5%B9%B4%E3%80%8F1958%E5%B9%B4%E6%98%AD%E5%92%8C33%E5%B9%B48%E6%9C%88%E5%8F%B7/)). A four-page piece is therefore a normal short story length, not a stub.

**Reader pages.** 漫画少年's contribution pages kept the magazine alive near its end ([漫画少年](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%BC%AB%E7%94%BB%E5%B0%91%E5%B9%B4)). Girls' magazines judged a serial's popularity partly by how many readers mailed in copies of its style drawings or portraits of the heroine ([Meiji shōjo exhibition](https://www.meiji.ac.jp/manga/yonezawa_lib/exh_shoujomanga/corner03.html)).

### 1.5 Picture stories (絵物語)

A picture story told its tale in pictures and prose, with no speech balloons. The form grew out of the "magazine paper theatre" idea, and its first clear example appeared in 少年倶楽部 in July 1939 ([Emonogatari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emonogatari)).

- **Layout:** early postwar pages paired a rectangular picture with a block of narration, like a paper-theatre card and its spoken script ([TCJ, Emonogatari in the Age of Comics](https://www.tcj.com/emonogatari-in-the-age-of-comics-1948-1957/)).
- **Popularity:** whole magazines were devoted to the form, and Yamakawa Sōji briefly out-earned Tezuka (same sources).
- **Decline:** manga took over youth magazines in the mid-1950s. Picture stories then faded or merged into the new comics language, picking up comics devices such as speech balloons and sound effects on the way ([TCJ](https://www.tcj.com/emonogatari-in-the-age-of-comics-1948-1957/)).

### 1.6 What the manga pages looked like

**Characters.** Tezuka adopted Disney's "stuffed-animal" style and the bow-legged, big-shoed figures of silent comedy. He studied American comics from 1946, and by 1951 his studio held stacks of American ten-cent comic books ([TCJ, Tezuka and American Comics](https://www.tcj.com/tezuka-osamu-and-american-comics/)). This is where the rounded, big-eyed children's-manga figure comes from.

**Layout and camera.** Tezuka was celebrated for "cinematic" montage and a variety of shots and angles ([Theisen, Comics Forum](https://comicsforum.org/2014/10/08/manga-studies-5-takeuchi-osamu-and-manga-expression-pt-1-tezuka-osamu-as-manga-locus-by-nicholas-theisen/)). New Treasure Island (1947) linked its panels with matching eyelines, a film-editing technique ([METI, The "Grammar" of Manga](https://artsandculture.google.com/story/EAWB4YvyVDqJJQ)). Two cautions apply:

- **The "cinematic" reputation rests on very little.** It comes largely from that book's first two pages, and partly reflects how later gekiga critics looked back ([TCJ](https://www.tcj.com/tezuka-osamu-outwits-the-phantom-blot-the-case-of-new-treasure-island-contd/)).
- **Three-row pages were not new.** They were already in use in the 1930s ([Theisen](https://comicsforum.org/2014/10/08/manga-studies-5-takeuchi-osamu-and-manga-expression-pt-1-tezuka-osamu-as-manga-locus-by-nicholas-theisen/)).

Reading order settled only gradually, and older manga numbered their panels to guide readers ([METI](https://artsandculture.google.com/story/EAWB4YvyVDqJJQ)). Before gekiga, the viewpoint in manga was almost always fixed and straight-on ([劇画](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%8A%87%E7%94%BB)).

**Screentone.** Before adhesive tone existed, artists specified dot or ground patterns and the printer applied them at the platemaking stage, a practice dating back to the 1910s ([スクリーントーン](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%82%B9%E3%82%AF%E3%83%AA%E3%83%BC%E3%83%B3%E3%83%88%E3%83%BC%E3%83%B3)).

- **1952:** designer Seki Saburō made an adhesive tone based on the American Zip-a-Tone and founded 日本トーン in October ([Meiji University screentone archive](https://www.meiji.ac.jp/manga/yonezawa_lib/archives/exh-ic/exh-ic_archive.html)).
- **About 1954–55:** manga artist Nagata Takemaru found it in a Jimbōchō shop. Within two or three months it was widespread in children's magazines (same source).
- **Early limits:** the first sheets had dots printed on the back and a wax adhesive, so they could not be scraped or shaded ([スクリーントーン](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%82%B9%E3%82%AF%E3%83%AA%E3%83%BC%E3%83%B3%E3%83%88%E3%83%BC%E3%83%B3)).
- **About 1970:** front-printed Letraset tone arrived (Letraset Japan was founded in 1966). Scraping and cutting tone became possible, and IC Screen followed in 1971 (Meiji archive; スクリーントーン).

**In short, 1950s grey is flat dots used sparingly. Gradients and scraped highlights belong to the 1970s.**

**Balloon lettering.**

- **Printing method:** 1950s printing was letterpress. Printers had to stock enormous quantities of metal type in every face and size, and when type was pasted onto artwork it went in as clean proofs ([アンチゴチ](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%82%A2%E3%83%B3%E3%83%81%E3%82%B4%E3%83%81)).
- **Before the 1950s:** balloons were plain mincho for both kanji and kana ([Typography Boogie Back](https://mt.webheibon.jp/typography-boogie-back/2020/10/post-14.html)).
- **The antique-kana-plus-gothic-kanji mix (アンチゴチ):**
  - It appears in 少年画報 and similar magazines in the early 1950s.
  - Around 1960 it replaced mincho in most manga in the big publishers' children's magazines ([アンチゴチ](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%82%A2%E3%83%B3%E3%83%81%E3%82%B4%E3%83%81)).
  - Another account dates its postwar arrival to the mid-1950s, through artists who came up via 漫画少年 ([Typography Boogie Back](https://mt.webheibon.jp/typography-boogie-back/2020/10/post-14.html)).
  - When gothic body text spread through general print after about 1950, children's manga magazines were the stated exception ([ゴシック体](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%82%B4%E3%82%B7%E3%83%83%E3%82%AF%E4%BD%93)).
- **Rental-book publishers** worked with small jobbing printers and kept plain thin mincho. The magazines for older readers that grew out of gekiga kept thin mincho into the 1990s, as a marker that separated them from children's manga ([アンチゴチ](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%82%A2%E3%83%B3%E3%83%81%E3%82%B4%E3%83%81)).
- **Later phototypesetting:** typesetters printed dialogue onto photographic paper and it was pasted in by hand. The standard pairing was Shaken's アンチックAN kana with 太ゴB101 kanji, and this method lasted until desktop publishing arrived in the early 1990s ([HIKE](https://voice.hike.inc/n/n0e012f20efda)).

### 1.7 What rental manga and early gekiga changed

**Rental shops.** A 1957 survey by the Tokyo police counted 1,241 rental-book shops in Tokyo. They had about 1.13 million monthly readers, about 427,000 of them primary-school children, and manga made up 32.5% of their stock ([貸本屋](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E8%B2%B8%E6%9C%AC%E5%B1%8B)).

**The word gekiga.** Tatsumi Yoshihiro's "Yūrei Taxi" was the first work to carry the label, at the end of 1957. The Gekiga Workshop followed in 1959 ([Gekiga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gekiga)).

**What gekiga brought:**

- realistic art with dense linework
- high and low angles instead of the fixed straight-on view
- bold sound effects, speed lines and focus lines
- an audience of working-class youth ([劇画](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%8A%87%E7%94%BB))

**What happened next.** Rental manga declined as the weeklies and television arrived ([貸本劇画](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E8%B2%B8%E6%9C%AC%E5%8A%87%E7%94%BB)). Gekiga artists entered Weekly Shōnen Magazine from 1965 ([劇画](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%8A%87%E7%94%BB)).

**Recommendation:** gekiga's toolkit is the vocabulary of "modern" action manga, and a cheerful state recruitment piece would have kept away from it.

## 2. Cutaways (図解), future-world features and the first giant robot

### 2.1 Komatsuzaki Shigeru

- **Early career:** Komatsuzaki began as a teenager, drawing military hardware for a mechanical journal ([SF Encyclopedia](https://sf-encyclopedia.com/entry/komatsuzaki_shigeru)).
- **Picture stories and colour plates:** he drew the picture story 地球SOS (August 1948 to October 1951) and colour frontispieces for many boys' magazines, including 少年 (1952–55) (same source).
- **Style:** his signature was the large double-page scene with little text, full of disasters and inventions (same source).
- **Mechanical drawing:** he also drew future weapons and vehicles, including cutaway cross-sections (断面図) ([小松崎茂](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%B0%8F%E6%9D%BE%E5%B4%8E%E8%8C%82)).
- **Film and box art:** he designed for the films 地球防衛軍 (1957), 宇宙大戦争 (1959) and 海底軍艦 (1963), and painted plastic-model box art from 1961 (same source).
- **Mostly painted scenes:** a gallery of his work shows dramatic painted scenes rather than labelled cross-sections ([Pink Tentacle](https://pinktentacle.com/2010/06/sci-fi-illustrations-by-shigeru-komatsuzaki/)).

### 2.2 "The world of the future" features

A 2012 collection of children's "future forecast" illustrations from the 1950s to the 1970s groups them under daily life, transport, robots, computers, space and doomsday ([Seigensha](https://www.seigensha.com/books/978-4-86152-315-1/)).

Primary examples from Kōdansha's school-year magazines show the caption voice ([web Mu](https://web-mu.jp/column/76602/)):

- **1959, for third-graders:** an early "vehicles that run on atomic power" feature. An atomic locomotive's caption says such trains will be running 「もうすぐ」 ("soon"). Its title is written almost entirely in hiragana for young readers.
- **1961:** the feature 「さあ行け 宇宙特急アルデバラン」 promised a trip to the moon for 100,000 yen by 1980.

The 1959 weeklies also filled much of their pages with science articles ([週刊少年マガジン](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E9%80%B1%E5%88%8A%E5%B0%91%E5%B9%B4%E3%83%9E%E3%82%AC%E3%82%B8%E3%83%B3)).

### 2.3 The big labelled cutaway (大図解) is a late-1960s form

Ōtomo Shōji planned Shōnen Magazine's cutaway photo-and-illustration features from 1966 to 1972. They were painted by 南村喬之, 石原豪人, 柳柊二, 梶田達二 and 水木しげる ([大伴昌司](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%A4%A7%E4%BC%B4%E6%98%8C%E5%8F%B8)).

The dense, numbered anatomy of a monster or machine therefore comes about five years after a 1955–62 target. A painted Komatsuzaki-style scene with a few captions is the in-period form.

**Recommendation:** use a painted cutaway of the Frame with five to eight numbered callouts. Treat it as a small, knowing step ahead of the period, not a strict period feature.

### 2.4 Tetsujin 28 (1956)

- **Run:** 少年, July 1956 to May 1966. It launched through gift booklets ([鉄人28号](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E9%89%84%E4%BA%BA28%E5%8F%B7)).
- **The robot:** a secret weapon from the war, built as a last-ditch comeback. It obeys whoever holds its small remote control, so good and evil depend on the controller. The boy detective 金田正太郎 holds it (same source).
- **Firsts:** it was the first Japanese series about a giant robot, and its creator also created Giant Robo ([Tetsujin 28-go](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetsujin_28-go)).
- **Page composition:** how its pages were composed beyond the short instalments noted in 1.4 is **(unverified)**.

## 3. Early girls' manga: the toolkit for a bubbly heroine

**Two lineages of cute.** Before the war, schoolgirls split into two camps. Nakahara Jun'ichi drew prim, European-style girls. Matsumoto Katsuji drew sunny, mischievous "American-type" girls ([TCJ, Matsumoto Katsuji](https://www.tcj.com/matsumoto-katsuji-and-the-american-roots-of-kawaii/)).

Matsumoto's Kurumi-chan ran in 少女の友 from 1938 for 35 years. She was a tomboy who beat the boys at sport and study, and her look came from Kewpies and the Campbell Kids (same source). For a bubbly recruit, this is the lineage to draw on.

**Tomboy heroines in the new magazines.** りぼん opened in 1955 with 上田トシコ's ぼんこちゃん, the slapstick daily life of an energetic tomboy ([BAILA](https://baila.hpplus.jp/lifestyle/entertainment/72368)). In the 1950s, men drew most girls' manga. Women such as 上田トシコ, Mizuno Hideko and Maki Miyako were a minority ([Shōjo manga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sh%C5%8Djo_manga)).

**Princess Knight** ran in 少女クラブ from January 1953 to January 1956. It was the first story manga for girls, and Tezuka built its atmosphere from the Takarazuka Revue ([手塚治虫 official site](https://tezukaosamu.net/jp/manga/559.html)).

**Starry eyes:**

- **Tezuka came first.** Mizuno Hideko recalled that he was the first to put a small cross-shaped star in a manga eye.
- **Ishimori made it huge.** Ishimori Shōtarō's giant stars in 二級天使 came as a shock.
- **Takahashi's rounded star took over.** Takahashi Macoto's version grew out of the eye highlights in Nakahara's illustrations. It appeared at the end of the 1950s and then featured in almost every girl on a title page ([Meiji shōjo exhibition](https://www.meiji.ac.jp/manga/yonezawa_lib/exh_shoujomanga/corner03.html)).

Takahashi started in rental books in 1953 and moved to the magazine 少女 in 1957 ([高橋真琴](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E9%AB%98%E6%A9%8B%E7%9C%9F%E7%90%B4)). The style Takahashi became known for had four main features ([Macoto Takahashi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macoto_Takahashi)):

- slender bodies with large sparkling eyes
- panels laid over one another
- full-length portraits
- backgrounds that carry emotion rather than setting

**The style drawing (スタイル画).** It first appeared in Takahashi's ballet manga あらしをこえて (少女, January 1958) ([Meiji](https://www.meiji.ac.jp/manga/yonezawa_lib/exh_shoujomanga/corner03.html)). It is a full-length figure of the heroine, unconnected to the plot, that runs down through three rows or the whole height of the page. Nothing like it existed before ([Project MUSE](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/488607/summary)).

Readers copied and mailed in style drawings. Maki Miyako's clothes were practical enough that magazines gave away real garments made from her designs as prizes. The term shifted from スタイル絵 (1961) to スタイル画 (1966) ([Meiji](https://www.meiji.ac.jp/manga/yonezawa_lib/exh_shoujomanga/corner03.html)).

**Decoration.** Flowers, ribbons, fluttering dresses and large sparkling eyes carried on the prewar tradition of lyrical illustration ([Shōjo manga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sh%C5%8Djo_manga)). European settings were fashionable around 1964, when ordinary Japanese could first travel abroad freely as tourists ([Meiji](https://www.meiji.ac.jp/manga/yonezawa_lib/exh_shoujomanga/corner03.html)).

**Recommendation:** a style drawing of the recruit in full uniform is exactly in period (1958 onward). It is also the natural place to show off the uniform.

## 4. Recruitment and public-relations imagery, 1946–1963

### 4.1 Women police officers (from 1946)

- **Recruitment:** the Tokyo Metropolitan Police began recruiting women on 21 February 1946, on the occupation authority's instruction. Of 1,673 applicants, 63 were hired ([トウシル](https://media.rakuten-sec.net/articles/-/51583)).
- **Early duties:** 62 went on duty on 27 April 1946, without powers of arrest. They handled juvenile guidance, traffic and public morals ([女性警察官](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%A5%B3%E6%80%A7%E8%AD%A6%E5%AF%9F%E5%AE%98)).
- **Uniform and name:** they wore skirts, and the period short form was 婦警. That term lasted until 1999 (same source).

### 4.2 The National Police Reserve, National Safety Forces and Self-Defense Forces

- **1950:** the National Police Reserve was created on 10 August 1950 ([National Archives](https://www.archives.go.jp/ayumi/kobetsu/s25_1950_02.html)). Recruiting began on 13 August, and testing started on 17 August at 183 sites. By 12 October, 74,158 recruits aged 18 to 35 were in training, chosen from more than five applicants per place. All were appointed at a police-style entry rank ([NIDS, Kuzuhara](https://www.nids.mod.go.jp/english/publication/kiyo/pdf/bulletin_e2006_5_Kuzuhara.pdf)).
- **The poster slogan:** a widely remembered recruiting line, 「平和日本はあなたを求めている」, is **(unverified)**. I could not confirm it in any source I reached. Check it against an archive before using it as a model.
- **1952–54:** the reserve became the National Safety Forces on 15 October 1952, and the Ground Self-Defense Force on 1 July 1954 ([保安隊](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E4%BF%9D%E5%AE%89%E9%9A%8A)).

**Women in the defence forces:**

- **1952:** women were first hired as nurses, then called 看護婦 ([imidas](https://imidas.jp/genre/detail/F-102-0051.html), [自衛官](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E8%87%AA%E8%A1%9B%E5%AE%98)).
- **1967:** university-educated women could become officers.
- **1968:** enlisted women joined the Ground Self-Defense Force, in accounting, signals and clerical work.
- **1974:** the navy and air force followed.
- **2003:** until March 2003 the term was 婦人自衛官 ([自衛官](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E8%87%AA%E8%A1%9B%E5%AE%98)).

Satō Fumika shows that women were used to set the SDF apart from the old military. Women featured often on recruiting posters only from the 1970s ([nippon.com](https://www.nippon.com/ja/in-depth/d00838/), [English](https://www.nippon.com/en/in-depth/d00838/)).

**In-world reading:** a 1950s-style force fronted by a bubbly young woman is a deliberate, slightly ahead-of-its-time PR move. It follows real-world logic, so it will feel right rather than anachronistic.

### 4.3 "Career girl" imagery

- **Bus guides:** female sightseeing-bus guides began in 1928. はとバス used women from its start in 1949 ([バスガイド](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%83%90%E3%82%B9%E3%82%AC%E3%82%A4%E3%83%89)).
  - By the 1950s and early 1960s the job was an aspiration for girls, ranked alongside kindergarten teacher, nurse and stewardess.
  - The uniform was a suit in the company colours, with a cap and white gloves (same source).
  - The song 東京のバスガール (October 1957) was modelled on a はとバス guide, and a film version followed in 1958 ([東京のバスガール](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%9D%B1%E4%BA%AC%E3%81%AE%E3%83%90%E3%82%B9%E3%82%AC%E3%83%BC%E3%83%AB)).
- **Telephone operators:** women had worked Japan's exchanges since service began in 1890 ([電話交換手](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E9%9B%BB%E8%A9%B1%E4%BA%A4%E6%8F%9B%E6%89%8B)).
- **Office women:** after the war, female office workers were called BG ("business girl"). The term fell out of favour because abroad it could suggest prostitution. The weekly 女性自身 introduced "OL" on 25 November 1963, chosen in a readers' contest ([トウシル](https://media.rakuten-sec.net/articles/-/50745), [Josei Jishin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Josei_Jishin)).

### 4.4 Design and typography of period posters and advertisements

- **The design profession:** Japan's first association of graphic designers was founded in 1951 (JAAC, known as 日宣美). Its exhibitions showed hand-drawn and painted modernist work. Kamekura Yūsaku's 1964 Olympic designs marked a turn toward art direction and typography ([Creative Review](https://www.creativereview.co.uk/a-history-of-japanese-poster-art/), [Yusaku Kamekura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yusaku_Kamekura)).
- **Type in ordinary advertisements:** titles and main copy in ordinary magazine advertisements were either hand-lettered or set solid. Solid setting means evenly spaced, with no tightening between characters. Tight hand-kerning began with Sugiura Kōhei and colleagues around 1957, and became common in ordinary advertisements only between about 1965 and 1973 ([Abe Takuya](https://researchmap.jp/abetakuya/published_papers/32586777/attachment_file.pdf)).
- **Gothic type:** through the 1940s it was used mainly for headings and emphasis. Improved gothic kana spread gothic body text through general print after about 1950 ([ゴシック体](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%82%B4%E3%82%B7%E3%83%83%E3%82%AF%E4%BD%93)).

**Correction:** the brief proposed mincho headlines over gothic body text. These sources describe the reverse as the older habit: gothic or hand lettering for headings, mincho for the body.

**A precedent to handle with care.** 翼賛一家 (1940) was a wartime propaganda family manga. A dozen or more cartoonists drew the same characters, it ran in the government's 寫眞週報, and it had its own songs ([翼賛一家](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E7%BF%BC%E8%B3%9B%E4%B8%80%E5%AE%B6)).

Postwar institutions worked hard not to look military: the reserve used police-style ranks, and the SDF later used women to soften its image (sources above).

**Recommendation:** have the in-world manga lean cheerful and civic, not martial. That is how a real 1950s agency would have played it.

### 4.5 Campaign names and phrasing

- **1951:** NHK's charity drive opened with 「みんなで明るいお正月を」 ([赤い羽根共同募金](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E8%B5%A4%E3%81%84%E7%BE%BD%E6%A0%B9%E5%85%B1%E5%90%8C%E5%8B%9F%E9%87%91)).
- **1952 and 1965:** the fair-elections league founded in 1952 was 公明選挙連盟. The phrase 明るく正しい選挙 was chosen only in 1965 ([明るい選挙推進協会](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%98%8E%E3%82%8B%E3%81%84%E9%81%B8%E6%8C%99%E6%8E%A8%E9%80%B2%E5%8D%94%E4%BC%9A)).
- **1960:** 防災の日 (Disaster Prevention Day) was set on 17 June 1960, fixed on 1 September ([防災の日](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E9%98%B2%E7%81%BD%E3%81%AE%E6%97%A5)).
- **Today:** the defence ministry's recruitment material still uses the header 自衛官募集 ([防衛省](https://www.mod.go.jp/gsdf/jieikanbosyu/chihon/vol07.html)). The period equivalent was probably "〇〇隊員募集" **(unverified wording)**.

## 5. Language

### 5.1 The 1946 reforms and what printed text looked like

**The kanji list.** The 当用漢字表 was issued on 16 November 1946 with 1,850 characters. It was followed in 1948 by a list of 881 characters for compulsory schooling and a table of readings, and in 1949 by a table of standard, often simplified, character forms ([当用漢字](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%BD%93%E7%94%A8%E6%BC%A2%E5%AD%97)). Its preface and usage notes said ([当用漢字表 on Wikisource](https://ja.wikisource.org/wiki/%E5%BD%93%E7%94%A8%E6%BC%A2%E5%AD%97%E8%A1%A8)):

- Furigana are not used, in principle.
- Pronouns, adverbs, conjunctions, interjections, auxiliary verbs and particles should preferably be written in kana.
- Foreign place and personal names, loanwords, animal and plant names, and words written with borrowed-sound kanji go in kana.

**Words outside the list** had to be replaced or partly written in kana. This produced mixed spellings such as けん引 ([当用漢字](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%BD%93%E7%94%A8%E6%BC%A2%E5%AD%97)). **Period flavour:** kana-heavy text is correct, and a stray half-kana compound would be too.

**New kana spelling.** 現代かなづかい (Cabinet Notice No. 33, 16 November 1946) merged ゐ/ゑ into い/え and replaced spellings such as てふ with ちょう and いふ with いう. It kept は, へ and を as particles. It spread quickly through official documents, textbooks and newspapers, and was replaced in 1986 ([コトバンク](https://kotobank.jp/word/%E7%8F%BE%E4%BB%A3%E3%81%8B%E3%81%AA%E3%81%A5%E3%81%84-60826)).

**Old forms lingered in names.** 冒険王 launched in 1949 under old-form kanji (冐險) and changed its title in 1956 ([冒険王](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%86%92%E9%99%BA%E7%8E%8B_(%E6%BC%AB%E7%94%BB%E9%9B%91%E8%AA%8C))). 少年倶楽部 became 少年クラブ in April 1946 ([少年倶楽部](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%B0%91%E5%B9%B4%E5%80%B6%E6%A5%BD%E9%83%A8)).

**Recommendation:** use new-form kanji throughout. If the organisation is meant to predate 1946, an old-form masthead is a subtle touch.

### 5.2 Furigana

- **Before the war,** kanji in general publications carried furigana throughout ([振り仮名](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%8C%AF%E3%82%8A%E4%BB%AE%E5%90%8D)).
- **1938:** Yamamoto Yūzō attacked furigana as 「黒い虫の行列」 ("a procession of black insects") (same source).
- **1946:** the 当用漢字表 cut furigana use. Today, publications for children or foreigners normally carry furigana (same source).
- **Children's books around 1950:** Ishii Momoko recalled that when 岩波少年文庫 started in 1950, the Education Ministry's line was to cut kanji and furigana as far as possible. The imprint avoided furigana on every kanji ([Tanaka 2011, J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/nfcc/36/0/36_KJ00006996179/_pdf/-char/ja)). The same author notes that balloons in modern boys' and girls' manga carry furigana on every kanji (same source).
- **1950s children's manga:** how much furigana 1950s children's manga magazines used, magazine by magazine, is **(unverified)**. Primary features for young readers were written mostly in hiragana ([web Mu](https://web-mu.jp/column/76602/)).

**Recommendation:** put furigana on every kanji in the balloons. It is right for children's print and helps the players.

### 5.3 Exclamation marks and tone of address

The exclamation mark is not native to Japanese writing; it spread through novels, scripts and manga ([感嘆符](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%84%9F%E5%98%86%E7%AC%A6)). It suits balloons and headlines, but would look odd in the formal body text of an official notice.

Documented period slogan patterns:

- **Everyone together:** 「みんなで〜を」, as in NHK's 1951 drive.
- **Here we go:** 「さあ行け」, as in the 1961 future feature.
- **Soon, this will happen:** 「もうすぐ〜ようになる」, as in the 1959 atomic locomotive caption.
- **Abstract civic words:** 公明, 明るい.

(Sources for all four: 4.5 and 2.2.)

- **"You too" and "Let's" slogans:** 「君も〜！」 as a recruiting formula, and 「〜しよう！」, fit these patterns. They are **(unverified)** as documented 1950s recruitment slogans.
- **Group address:** 少年少女諸君 is **(unverified)** in magazine editorials.

**Pastiche suggestions (mine, not period quotations):**

- きみも　〇〇隊へ！
- みんなで　まもろう　明るい〇〇！
- もうすぐ　きみの　町にも……

## 6. What a modern manga has that a period one lacked

- **Tone.** Modern manga uses heavy, gradient, scraped tone, which was only possible from about 1970. The 1950s used flat back-printed dots from 1954–55, or printer-applied tints before that ([Meiji](https://www.meiji.ac.jp/manga/yonezawa_lib/archives/exh-ic/exh-ic_archive.html), [スクリーントーン](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%82%B9%E3%82%AF%E3%83%AA%E3%83%BC%E3%83%B3%E3%83%88%E3%83%BC%E3%83%B3)).
- **Sound effects and speed lines.** Bold hand-drawn sound effects, focus lines and dramatic high and low angles are gekiga's contribution. Before gekiga, framing was almost always straight-on ([劇画](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%8A%87%E7%94%BB)).
- **Lettering.** The mix of antique kana and gothic kanji is period-correct for children's magazines from about 1960 ([アンチゴチ](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%82%A2%E3%83%B3%E3%83%81%E3%82%B4%E3%83%81)). What is modern is the smooth, tightly spaced digital finish and switching fonts for emotion ([HIKE](https://voice.hike.inc/n/n0e012f20efda)). Period type was evenly spaced metal ([Abe](https://researchmap.jp/abetakuya/published_papers/32586777/attachment_file.pdf)).
- **Pages.** Splash pages and broken panel borders are modern. The period page was a stack of rows. Takahashi's style drawing (1958) was the first sanctioned break ([Meiji](https://www.meiji.ac.jp/manga/yonezawa_lib/exh_shoujomanga/corner03.html)). Intricately overlapping panels are a 1970s girls'-manga development ([METI](https://artsandculture.google.com/story/EAWB4YvyVDqJJQ)).
- **Colour and paper.** Clean digital colour is modern. Coloured paper stock is late 1960s ([NDL](https://crd.ndl.go.jp/reference/entry/index.php?id=1000165895&page=ref_view)). Two-colour vermilion-and-black is a genuine cheap-colour magazine technique ([OPTPiX Labs](https://www.webtech.co.jp/blog/k1/7485/)).
- **English lettering.** Period American comics were hand-lettered on the art in pen and ink, using a lettering guide. Digital comic fonts date from about 1990 ([Letterer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letterer)). Whether all capitals was universal is **(unverified)**.

## Choices for a period-flavoured propaganda manga

| Feature | Period-authentic option | Modern option | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Format | B5 magazine insert, or a small B6 gift booklet | Paperback volume or webcomic | Present it as a gift booklet or an insert: period-true, and it explains why it is only four pages |
| Cover | Photo or painted portrait, crowded masthead, "〇大付録" gift-count banner | Clean character illustration | A painted portrait of the recruit beside the Frame, with a busy masthead and a gift-count banner |
| Colour | Four-colour cover; two-colour (vermilion plus black) opening pages; black-only interior | Full digital colour | Four-colour cover, vermilion-and-black page 1, black pages 2–4 |
| Paper and ink | Off-white cheap paper, slightly uneven black ink | Clean white, flat black | Light paper texture and uneven ink; skip tinted paper (late 1960s) and heavy yellowing |
| Panel layout | Three or four even rows; panels once numbered | Splash pages, broken borders | Even rows, with one full-height style drawing of the recruit (1958 onward) |
| Camera | Mostly straight-on, with Tezuka-style changes of shot | Gekiga angles, dynamic distortion | Straight-on for the recruit; one low angle for the robot |
| Character design | Rounded, Disney-derived; star-lit eyes; tomboy energy | Contemporary anime proportions | Rounded figures with star highlights: a Kurumi-chan type heroine |
| Grey values | None, or sparse flat dots; hatching | Gradients, scraped tone | Flat dots only, used sparingly |
| Sound effects | Few, small, hand-lettered | Dense, huge, stylised | Restrained, with one or two big effects saved for the Frame |
| Japanese balloon type | Letterpress mincho (earlier), or antique kana with gothic kanji (about 1960), evenly spaced | Phototypeset or digital AN kana with 太ゴ kanji, tightly spaced | Antique kana with gothic kanji, evenly spaced; mincho for a deliberately "official" caption |
| Headlines | Hand-lettered or gothic headings over mincho body | Display fonts, tight kerning | Hand-lettered headings, mincho body text |
| Furigana | Heavy furigana and hiragana for young readers, against official policy | Furigana on every kanji in boys' and girls' manga | Furigana on every kanji in balloons |
| Spelling and kanji | New forms and the new kana spelling; old forms survive in legacy titles | Current standards | Period standards throughout, with an optional old-form masthead |
| Vocabulary | 婦人〜, 〜ガール, BG, 公明〜, 防空 | 女性〜, OL, 明るい〜, 民間防衛 | Period terms, and invent in-world words in the period's way |

| Cutaway page | Painted scene with a few captions (Komatsuzaki) | Infographic | A painted Frame cutaway with five to eight numbered callouts (a slight 1966-style anticipation) |
| Extras | Gift cut-outs, reader contest, 「つづく」, coupon | QR code or social handle | A mail-in coupon or a paper-craft gift panel |

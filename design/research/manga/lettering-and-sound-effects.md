# Balloons, lettering and sound effects, in Japanese and English

*Part of the [manga research shelf](README.md), gathered 2026-09-26 for [`design/propaganda-manga.md`](../../propaganda-manga.md). **Research, not lore, and not a decision:** it says what the sources say and what might transfer, and the plan decides. Every factual claim links its source; **(unverified)** marks what could not be confirmed. It names real works, people and places because it is GM-facing; none of them belongs in the comic.*

**How the sourcing works.** Every claim has a link next to it. The sources fall into three tiers, most authoritative first:
1. Standards bodies and primary publishers: W3C, Unicode, Microsoft, Morisawa, Google Fonts metadata and the licence texts themselves.
2. Japanese reference works and industry how-to pages: NINJAL, the Shogakukan *Digital Daijisen* (read through Weblio), Clip Studio's official tips and Shogakukan's manga school.
3. One large fan-run dictionary: The JADED Network. One person runs it as a hobby, and it holds 1,999 SFX entries ([JADED](https://thejadednetwork.com/sfx/)).

"(unverified)" marks anything I could not confirm. A few conversions are my own arithmetic; they are labelled as arithmetic.

## 1. Balloon (吹き出し) taxonomy in Japanese manga

### 1.1 Background
- Japan adopted balloons late. 『正ちゃんの冒険』 (1923) mixed explanatory captions with American-style balloons, and 『ノンキナトウサン』 (also 1923) was the first to use balloons alone ([ja.wikipedia ふきだし](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%81%B5%E3%81%8D%E3%81%A0%E3%81%97)).
- Japan also moved to typeset balloon text earlier than the West. From the 1960s, phototypeset text was cut out and pasted into balloons before offset printing. That is why all non-handwritten balloon text is still called 写植 (*shashoku*, "phototypesetting"), even though it is now done in desktop publishing (DTP) ([same](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%81%B5%E3%81%8D%E3%81%A0%E3%81%97)).

- Conventions vary from artist to artist. For example, a Shogakukan lesson uses dashed borders for inner monologue, where other guides use them for whispers ([Shogakukan lesson](https://www.clipstudio.net/oekaki/archives/152081)).
- Balloon size itself signals volume and how important the line is ([choge](https://www.choge-blog.com/hobby/mangafukidashi/)).
- Western comics mark radio, TV and phone speech with a spiky balloon, a lightning-bolt tail and italic text ([Blambot](https://blambot.com/pages/comic-book-grammar-tradition)). The Japanese guides I found describe double-lined or angular shapes instead. Whether Japanese manga also use the lightning tail is (unverified).

### 1.3 Tails
- **Speech:** the tail points at the speaker's mouth. Crossed tails confuse the reader, and tails that stop short make attribution unclear ([egaco](https://comic.smiles55.jp/guide/10094/)). Western practice aims the tail as though the line continued to the mouth, but stops it roughly halfway between balloon and head ([Blambot](https://blambot.com/pages/comic-book-grammar-tradition)).
- **Thought:** a chain of small circles leads toward the head ([ja.wikipedia](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%81%B5%E3%81%8D%E3%81%A0%E3%81%97), [Blambot](https://blambot.com/pages/comic-book-grammar-tradition)).
- **Tailless:** the speaker is hidden from the reader and from the characters, for example an anonymous member of a crowd ([ja.wikipedia](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%81%B5%E3%81%8D%E3%81%A0%E3%81%97)).
- **Inward tail (a Japanese invention):** a notch that points *into* the balloon. It indicates an off-panel speaker in the direction beyond the far side of the balloon ([ja.wikipedia](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%81%B5%E3%81%8D%E3%81%A0%E3%81%97)).
- **Off-panel speaker, Western practice:** the tail butts against the panel border, or the balloon has no tail ([Blambot](https://blambot.com/pages/comic-book-grammar-tradition)).

### 1.4 Placement, reading order and linked balloons
- Balloons are read right to left and top to bottom. Each balloon sits close to its speaker, and crossed placement (たすきがけ) makes a panel hard to read ([Shogakukan lesson](https://www.clipstudio.net/oekaki/archives/152081)).
- Long speeches are split across several balloons at natural phrase breaks ([Shogakukan lesson](https://www.clipstudio.net/oekaki/archives/152081)).
- Balloons should not cover faces ([egaco](https://comic.smiles55.jp/guide/10094/)).
- Text stays inside the inner frame (基本枠) so that trimming cannot cut it off ([Shogakukan lesson](https://www.clipstudio.net/oekaki/archives/152081), [futaba guideline](https://futaba-dd.jp/dcguideline/size.html)).
- **Linked balloons:** when one character has several balloons in a panel, only the balloon nearest the speaker's head gets a tail. The others join it in sequence, either touching directly or through a thin band ([ja.wikipedia](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%81%B5%E3%81%8D%E3%81%A0%E3%81%97)). In Western practice, touching balloons read as one continuous thought, while a connector line suggests a pause or a back-and-forth ([Blambot](https://blambot.com/pages/comic-book-grammar-tradition)).

### 1.5 Text outside balloons
- **描き文字 (*kakimoji*, hand-drawn lettering)** covers sound effects and some hand-lettered lines. Readers take it in as part of the picture. Shogakukan calls it secondary text: legible, but something a reader can skip, with no strict rules on where it goes ([Shogakukan lesson](https://www.clipstudio.net/oekaki/archives/152081), [MediBang](https://medibangpaint.com/use/2020/06/how-to-draw-characters-for-cartoon/)).
- **The shōjo monologue** puts a character's voice straight into the panel with no balloon, which moves the reader inside that character's head. It grew up in 1970s shōjo manga, with Hagio Moto established by the early 1970s, and was a dominant device by the 1980s ([box96](https://box96.hatenablog.com/entry/20120722/1342957583)). Our recruit's inner cheerfulness could float like this.

## 2. Japanese commercial typesetting

### 2.1 From 写植 to DTP
- Phototypesetting paste-up became standard in the 1960s ([ja.wikipedia ふきだし](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%81%B5%E3%81%8D%E3%81%A0%E3%81%97)).
- DTP removed the equipment barrier, so アンチゴチ (§2.2) spread to every genre ([ja.wikipedia アンチゴチ](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%82%A2%E3%83%B3%E3%83%81%E3%82%B4%E3%83%81)).
- *Manga Time Kirara* switched from Shaken fonts to Morisawa and Fontworks around 2012–13 ([kirara note](https://note.com/soudakyoto_ikou/n/nd63330df85d8)). *Kingdom* moved from Shaken type to digital antique fonts between 2006 and 2011 ([webheibon](https://mt.webheibon.jp/typography-boogie-back/2020/10/post-14.html)).

### 2.2 アンチゴチ (*anchi-gochi*)
- **What it is.** Kana are set in an antique face (アンチック体) and kanji in a Gothic face. The name comes from the proofreader's marks アンチ and ゴチ, written side by side to order the mix ([ja.wikipedia](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%82%A2%E3%83%B3%E3%83%81%E3%82%B4%E3%83%81)).
- **History.**
  - Antique kana appeared in the 1890s. They grew out of 籠文字, the lettering on shrine lottery tickets, and have near-uniform strokes, unlike Mincho. Founders paired them with Gothic kanji, which had no kana of their own.
  - In the 1950s, general printing moved to all-Gothic setting. Children's manga magazines such as 『少年画報』 kept アンチゴチ as a house style. Rental-book (貸本) and gekiga publishers used thin Mincho because they lacked antique type.
  - By the 1960s the split was settled: アンチゴチ for children's manga, Mincho for adult manga. DTP later spread アンチゴチ everywhere ([ja.wikipedia](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%82%A2%E3%83%B3%E3%83%81%E3%82%B4%E3%83%81)).
  - One account dates manga use to the mid-1950s, among the *Manga Shōnen* artists, but could not confirm the first work ([webheibon](https://mt.webheibon.jp/typography-boogie-back/2020/10/post-14.html)).
- **Why manga use it.**
  - Wikipedia stresses the visual match with Gothic kanji and the weight of convention ([ja.wikipedia](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%82%A2%E3%83%B3%E3%83%81%E3%82%B4%E3%83%81)).
  - The commonly repeated explanation is that the softened stroke contrast survives cheap recycled magazine paper ([kirara note](https://note.com/soudakyoto_ikou/n/nd63330df85d8)).
  - Adobe says low-contrast kana plus clean Gothic kanji stay legible in narrow balloons ([Adobe](https://blog.adobe.com/jp/publish/2023/11/16/cc-design-tenmin-antique)).
  - Morisawa describes its antique as thick kana born to match Gothic kanji, used for dictionary headwords, picture books and manga balloons ([Morisawa アンチックAN1](https://www.morisawa.co.jp/fonts/specimen/1165)).

### 2.3 Faces professionals use
- **The standard pair:** Morisawa アンチックAN1 for kana and 太ゴB101 for kanji ([kirara note](https://note.com/soudakyoto_ikou/n/nd63330df85d8)). Morisawa pairs the AN family with its ゴシックMB101 and 太ゴ Gothics ([Morisawa](https://www.morisawa.co.jp/fonts/specimen/1165)). 太ゴB101 is one of Morisawa's "basic seven" typefaces ([ja.wikipedia 太ゴB101](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%A4%AA%E3%82%B4B101)).
- **Publisher history** ([webheibon](https://mt.webheibon.jp/typography-boogie-back/2020/10/post-14.html)):
  - Shaken's アンチック体 served Shogakukan, Shueisha and Hakusensha.
  - Kodansha used Morisawa's アンチックAN.
  - 学参かなアンチック, a variant with clearer hiragana, has been used for 『名探偵コナン』 since 2007.
- **Iwata:** Iwata's antique (I-OTFアンチックStd B) is described as the magazine standard ([Clip Studio TIPS](https://tips.clip-studio.com/ja-jp/articles/9582), [oekaki-zukan](https://oekaki-zukan.com/articles/31084)).
- **Role fonts in *Kirara*** ([kirara note](https://note.com/soudakyoto_ikou/n/nd63330df85d8)):

  | Font (foundry) | Role |
  |---|---|
  | スーラ (Fontworks), rounded Gothic | Inner monologue |
  | 新ゴ (Morisawa) | Forceful lines |
  | マティス (Fontworks) | Sorrowful lines |
  | リュウミン (Morisawa) | Formal or grave lines |
  | ロダンハッピー (Fontworks) | Comedy |
  | コミックレゲエ (Fontworks) | Shouting |

- **Adobe:** 貂明朝アンチック (2023) comes in six weights and carries an "Adobe-Manga1-0" glyph set, which covers manga punctuation and sound-extension marks ([Adobe](https://blog.adobe.com/jp/publish/2023/11/16/cc-design-tenmin-antique)).
- All of these are commercial. Morisawa and Iwata faces are sold by subscription or licence (unverified details).

### 2.4 Size, line length and spacing
- **Units.** 1Q (級) = 0.25 mm ([tttombo](https://www.tttombo.com/tombo2-manga-seisakuyougo-ka-kyusu.html), [Clip Studio TIPS](https://tips.clip-studio.com/ja-jp/articles/9582)). So 13Q = 3.25 mm ≈ 9.2 pt, and 20Q = 5 mm ≈ 14.2 pt (arithmetic, using 1 pt = 0.3528 mm).
- **Manuscript sizes.** Commercial B4 manuscripts letter dialogue at 18–20Q, about 12–14 pt ([Clip Studio TIPS](https://tips.clip-studio.com/ja-jp/articles/851)). A size table gives these ranges ([clipstudio-comic](https://clipstudio-comic.com/font-size/)):

  | Manuscript | pt | Q |
  |---|---|---|
  | B4 | 12–15 | 18–20 |
  | B5 | 10–14 | 15–20 |
  | A5 | 8–10 | 12–15 |

- **Reduction.** B4 boards shrink to 83% for a B5 magazine ([comici](https://comics.comici.jp/episodes/6d3288898168b)). So 20Q prints at about 16.6Q in the magazine. Scaling again to B6 (182 mm tall) or 新書判 (174 mm) from B5's 257 mm gives about 11–12Q (arithmetic). The A5 row above, which prints 1:1, suggests 12–15Q.
- **Book sizes:** 新書判 112×174 mm, B6 128×182 mm, A5 148×210 mm, B5 182×257 mm (the magazine size) ([mangazenkan](https://www.mangazenkan.com/special/890.html)).
- **Line spacing:** 125–140% of the type size for commercial work, and about 150% with ruby ([Clip Studio TIPS](https://tips.clip-studio.com/ja-jp/articles/851)).
- **Line length.** One how-to site's rule of thumb for vertical text is at most 10 characters per line and at most 3 lines per balloon, with 5–7 characters for high-impact lines ([otona](https://www.xn--68jza3a7az043ascn47v.jp/comic/%E6%BC%AB%E7%94%BB%E5%88%B6%E4%BD%9C/0003.html)). Break lines at phrase units (文節) and never mid-word ([umakose](https://umakose.com/notes/manga-dialogue)).
- **Alignment.** How columns align inside a balloon (top-aligned or centred) is (unverified); make it a setting.

### 2.5 Vertical punctuation and how each glyph behaves
- **縦中横 (tate-chū-yoko)** sets a short horizontal run inside a vertical line, in one character frame. It is typical for two-digit numbers and for !? pairs ([W3C JLReq §3.2.5](https://www.w3.org/TR/jlreq/)). Clip Studio applies it automatically to half-width alphanumerics ([Clip Studio TIPS](https://tips.clip-studio.com/ja-jp/articles/851)).
- **Orientation classes** from Unicode's UTR #50 ([UTR #50 data](https://www.unicode.org/Public/vertical/revision-17/VerticalOrientation-17.txt)):
  - ‼ ⁇ ⁈ ⁉ (U+203C, U+2047–2049) are **U** (upright), so a font that has them can set !!, ?!, !? as a single glyph.
  - Full-width ！ and ？, 、 and 。, and small kana such as っ ッ are **Tu** (upright, but shifted by the vertical glyph).
  - ー (U+30FC), 「」 and 〜 are **Tr** (use the vertical glyph if the font has one, otherwise rotate).
  - … (U+2026), ‥ (U+2025) and the dashes — and ― are **R** (rotate).
- **OpenType features.** `vert` swaps in vertical forms, such as rotated brackets and shifted small kana, and should be on by default in vertical text. `vrt2` is a superset meant for engines that expect pre-rotated glyphs. Never apply both ([OpenType features u–z](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/typography/opentype/spec/features_uz)).
- **Paired marks.** Publishing convention sets the three-dot leader in pairs (……) ([reiwa](https://reiwasyuppan.jp/santen/)). The two-em dash ―― is paired in the same way (unverified). Manga house styles vary ([umakose](https://umakose.com/notes/manga-dialogue)):
  - some drop a balloon-final 。 because the balloon already acts as the quotation marks;
  - some drop 、 too;
  - some use ‥.

  Pick one style and keep it for the whole book.

### 2.6 Ruby (furigana)
- **総ルビ vs パラルビ.** Children's books and shōnen manga put ruby on every kanji (総ルビ). Adult publications gloss only hard words (パラルビ) ([ja.wikipedia ルビ](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%83%AB%E3%83%93)). *Jump*, *Magazine* and *Sunday* are all 総ルビ, while top webtoons are not, and ruby roughly doubles typesetting time ([Studio No.9](https://note.com/studio_no9/n/n6b7b7881907d)).
- **Form.** Ruby is about half the base size and sits on the right in vertical text. It comes as mono-ruby (one reading per character), group ruby (one reading per word), or 熟語 (compound-word) ruby ([ja.wikipedia ルビ](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%83%AB%E3%83%93), [JLReq §3.3](https://www.w3.org/TR/jlreq/)).
- **For us:** a recruitment comic aimed at young people would plausibly be 総ルビ, which is a nice diegetic touch.

### 2.7 Line-break rules (禁則処理)
- **Never at the start of a line:** closing brackets, 、 and 。, middle dots, small kana, the long-vowel mark ー, iteration marks, and ！？.
- **Never at the end of a line:** opening brackets ([JLReq §3.1.7–3.1.9](https://www.w3.org/TR/jlreq/)).
- **Unbreakable sequences:** JLReq has a class for these (§3.1.10). That …… and ―― belong to it is (unverified).

### 2.8 Emphasis and narration boxes
- **傍点 (emphasis dots):** sesame dots set to the right of the vertical characters ([JLReq §3.3.9](https://www.w3.org/TR/jlreq/)).
- **Size:** larger text means shouting and smaller means whispering ([oekaki-zukan](https://oekaki-zukan.com/articles/31084)). Emphasis also comes from switching typeface (*Kirara*'s 新ゴ for force), rather than from faux bold ([kirara note](https://note.com/soudakyoto_ikou/n/nd63330df85d8)).
- **Caption boxes** carry narration and moods for several characters at once, not tied to any one speaker ([ja.wikipedia](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%81%B5%E3%81%8D%E3%81%A0%E3%81%97)). Rectangles read as calm ([jpneet](https://jpneet.com/twodimension/comic-fukidashi/)).
- **Outlines:** use a white outline (フチ) 0.6–0.8 mm wide when text sits over artwork ([oekaki-zukan](https://oekaki-zukan.com/articles/31084)).

## 3. Onomatopoeia

### 3.1 Categories
Kindaichi (1978) splits sound-symbolic words into five groups. One word can fall into several of them; ごろごろ can fall into all five ([NINJAL column](https://www2.ninjal.ac.jp/Onomatope/column/nihongo_1.html)).

| Category | Covers | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 擬声語 | Human and animal voices | わんわん |
| 擬音語 | Object and nature sounds | ざあざあ, がちゃん |
| 擬態語 | States of non-living things | きらきら, つるつる |
| 擬容語 | Movements of living things | うろうろ, ぐんぐん |
| 擬情語 | Feelings and sensations | いらいら, わくわく |

NINJAL, the national Japanese-language institute, also runs a learner's site that illustrates these words with manga ([NINJAL](https://www2.ninjal.ac.jp/Onomatope/)).

### 3.2 Katakana or hiragana
- Publishing and broadcasting use a guideline, not a rule: katakana for 擬音語, hiragana for 擬態語. In usage data, words that imitate sound skew toward katakana ([kansei-ai](https://www.kansei-ai.com/post/20250523)).
- In manga the scripts carry meaning: katakana suggests motion and intensity, hiragana suggests stillness and gentleness ([terastorys](https://raon-terastorys.com/kakimoji/)). Hiragana reads soft; katakana reads mechanical or sharp ([kansei-ai](https://www.kansei-ai.com/post/20250523)).

### 3.3 How SFX are drawn
- **Size is loudness.** Big letters mean an impact; tiny letters mean a quiet sound ([egaco](https://comic.smiles55.jp/guide/10918/), [terastorys](https://raon-terastorys.com/kakimoji/)).
- **Shape.** Rounded letterforms read as soft sounds, angular or jagged ones as hard or metallic. Black letters feel heavy; white letters feel light and sharp ([terastorys](https://raon-terastorys.com/kakimoji/), [egaco](https://comic.smiles55.jp/guide/10918/)).
- **Outline.** A white outline keeps SFX legible over the art ([egaco](https://comic.smiles55.jp/guide/10918/), [Shogakukan lesson](https://www.clipstudio.net/oekaki/archives/152081)).
- **Motion.** Put the lettering in perspective along the direction of movement ([mediag, Agency for Cultural Affairs](https://mediag.bunka.go.jp/article/article-20297/)). Varying size and angle within a single word adds energy ([egaco](https://comic.smiles55.jp/guide/10918/)).
- **Scale.** SFX can fill a whole panel: Mizushima Shinji did this with crowd ワーワー. A character can also own a signature sound, as with Tanioka's ペタシペタシ ([mediag](https://mediag.bunka.go.jp/article/article-20297/)).
- **Omission.** Leaving a sound out on purpose can make a moment more subjective ([manga-school.jp](https://manga-school.jp/sound-effect/)).
- **Clipping and stretching.** A final ッ clips a sound (ドキッ, ビクッ); ー sustains it (ドーン, シーン); tripled syllables make a continuous rumble (ゴゴゴ, ドドド). These are variants seen across the lexicon entries below ([JADED](https://thejadednetwork.com/sfx/)).
- **Placement.** SFX usually sit next to their source and may break panel borders (unverified as a rule; Shogakukan says placement is free).

### 3.4 Two historical facts
- Tezuka coined シーン for silence in 『新世界ルルー』 (1951–52) and said so himself ([magmix](https://magmix.jp/post/76749)).
- ゴゴゴ and ドドド as tension marks are tied to Araki's *JoJo* ([magmix](https://magmix.jp/post/76749)). JADED also glosses ゴゴゴ as a rumble or an engine whirr ([JADED](https://thejadednetwork.com/sfx/browse/gogogo/)).

## 4. English localisation practice

### 4.1 Fonts
- **CC Wild Words** (Comicraft):
  - John Roshell drew it in 1995 for *WildC.A.T.s Adventures*.
  - It is widely used for manga translation.
  - The family has four weights and added manga characters.
  - A desktop licence costs $139 for two users ([Comicraft](https://www.comicbookfonts.com/Wildwords-font-p/bl003i.htm)).
  - Comicraft itself was founded in 1992 by Richard Starkings and Roshell ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comicraft)).
  - In 2016 Comicraft ran a Kickstarter for a Japanese comic-lettering font, "Wildwords Manga" ([downthetubes](https://downthetubes.net/comicraft-turns-japanese-with-an-ambitious-new-font-project-that-is/)).
- **Anime Ace 2** (Blambot) comes in Regular, Italic and Bold, has limited European characters, and offers a "Non-Profit/Indie Comics" licence option ([Blambot](https://blambot.com/products/anime-ace-2)).
- **What working letterers use:**
  - Comicraft's Tight Spot, Samaritan Tall, Face Front, Joe Kubert and Meanwhile.
  - Wildwords for dialogue, Zoinks for SFX and Arial Narrow for signs.
  - InDesign for typesetting and Photoshop for retouching ([SWET](https://swet.jp/members/article/manga_lettering_and_translation)).

### 4.2 Casing, emphasis and punctuation
- **Western comics grammar** ([Blambot](https://blambot.com/pages/comic-book-grammar-tradition)):
  - A crossbarred I is used only for the pronoun "I" and for acronyms.
  - Emphasis is set in bold italic.
  - Ellipses are exactly three dots, including when a line trails into the next balloon.
  - A double dash marks interrupted speech.
  - SFX carry no punctuation.
  - Hyphenation is avoided.
- **Casing in manga.** Manga is mostly lettered in capitals; one letterer says she would like to see more mixed-case fonts ([Otaku News interview](https://www.otakunews.com/Article/8789/an-interview-with-sara-linsley-manga-letterer)). Which publisher uses which case is (unverified).
- **Style guides.** Letterers keep a style guide for each series, fixing the fonts for dialogue, captions, flashbacks, handwriting and author notes ([same](https://www.otakunews.com/Article/8789/an-interview-with-sara-linsley-manga-letterer)).

### 4.3 Reshaping tall balloons for English
- Balloons drawn for vertical Japanese are tall. Horizontal English has to be split and hyphenated to fill them ([SWET](https://swet.jp/members/article/manga_lettering_and_translation)), and long, narrow balloons are the hardest to fill ([Bovia interview](https://j-entranslations.com/brandon-bovia-manga-letterer-interviews-with-localizers/)).
- Publishers' art files come flattened, so letterers erase the Japanese and retouch the art ([Otaku News](https://www.otakunews.com/Article/8789/an-interview-with-sara-linsley-manga-letterer)).
- **We avoid this whole problem:** our software draws the balloons, so each edition can get balloons shaped for its own language.

### 4.4 How publishers handle sound effects
- **Three methods** ([SWET](https://swet.jp/members/article/manga_lettering_and_translation)):
  - a full retouch, where the SFX is redrawn as an original graphic;
  - a styled subtitle, where the Japanese stays and styled English is fitted in beside it;
  - a plain subtitle.
- **By publisher**, from a 2010 survey ([Core Dump](https://www.starkeith.net/coredump/2010/12/31/sound-effects-in-manga/)):

  | Publisher | Approach |
  |---|---|
  | Viz | Full replacement |
  | Yen Press | Romanisation plus English in the panel, e.g. "gachan (door locking)" |
  | Dark Horse | Margin notes, e.g. in *Appleseed* |
  | ADV / Bandai | Plain English equivalents |

- Yen Press's *Yotsuba&!* kept the kana and added romaji plus English, where ADV's earlier edition had used English sounds such as "bonk" ([4thletter](https://4thletter.net/2009/09/yotsuba-translation-issues/)).
- Tokyopop's 2002 line left the Japanese SFX untranslated ([ANN](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/news/2002-01-29/tokyopop-commits-to-unflopped-manga)).
- The current policies of Kodansha USA and Seven Seas are (unverified).

### 4.5 Honorifics and translation notes
- The translator and editor decide for each title. The rule of thumb is to drop honorifics when the story is set outside Japan and keep them when it is set in Japan and the relationships matter ([ANN Answerman, Deb Aoki](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/answerman/2018-09-28/.137444)):
  - Viz keeps them in *Haikyu!!*;
  - Kodansha keeps them in *Sailor Moon* but drops them in *Vinland Saga*;
  - Yen Press drops them in *Emma* and *A Bride's Story*.
- Yen Press adds translation notes for cultural references ([4thletter](https://4thletter.net/2009/09/yotsuba-translation-issues/)).

### 4.6 Flipped versus unflipped
- Early English editions were mirror-flipped ("flopped") so they would read left to right ([ANN](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/news/2002-01-29/tokyopop-commits-to-unflopped-manga)), which distorted the art ([Wikipedia, Tokyopop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyopop)). Akira Toriyama asked for his books to keep the right-to-left format ([Wikipedia, Manga outside Japan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manga_outside_Japan)).
- On 29 January 2002, Tokyopop committed to publishing every new title unflopped from April 2002. It gave three reasons: artistic integrity, releases three to six times faster, and SFX kept intact without white-out gaps ([ANN](https://www.animenewsnetwork.com/news/2002-01-29/tokyopop-commits-to-unflopped-manga)).
- The line sold at $9.99, against $12.99–16.99 for competitors ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tokyopop)). By the end of 2002 it held 33 of the top 50 graphic novels, which pushed the whole industry toward unflipped books ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manga_outside_Japan)).

## 5. Fonts we can legally use (private, non-commercial; rasterised images plus a PDF)

### 5.1 Licence ground rules
- **SIL Open Font License (OFL):** free for artwork and print; embedding in PDFs, whole or subset, is allowed; documents keep whatever licence we choose; attribution is optional. A *modified font file* may not use the font's reserved name ([OFL FAQ](https://openfontlicense.org/ofl-faq/)). Converting glyphs to outlines inside our artwork is ordinary artwork use.
- **Windows fonts:** Microsoft treats graphics files like print output. Document embedding is allowed when the application honours the font's embedding flag (`fsType`). You may not redistribute, convert or modify the font files themselves ([Microsoft FAQ](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/typography/fonts/font-faq)).
- **Blambot's free indie licence:** covers print or "flattened" web graphics for a comic. The free clause does not extend to industries other than comic creation, so a comic made as a prop for an RPG is a **grey zone** ([Blambot licences](https://blambot.com/pages/licenses)).
- **Comicraft:** print and images are fine. PDFs with embedded fonts are allowed only for read-only, non-commercial documents, and outlining the text is recommended ([Comicraft licence](https://www.comicbookfonts.com/help_answer.asp?ID=12)). The only free Comicraft font is Hooky Solid Free, given with a purchase or a newsletter signup ([Comicraft](https://www.comicbookfonts.com/Free-Font-s/1836.htm)).

### 5.2 Japanese dialogue faces (true アンチゴチ)
- **源暎アンチック (Gen'ei Antique)**, from 自家製フォント工房 (okoneya.jp):
  - Licence: OFL 1.1; reserved names 源暎 and GenEi ([okoneya](https://okoneya.jp/font/)).
  - Built for manga dialogue. Current version v6.0a, 1 October 2025 ([窓の杜](https://forest.watch.impress.co.jp/library/software/geneiantique/)).
  - Hiragana come from F910新コミック体, adjusted to match 源ノ角ゴシック kanji; katakana are based on bold 源ノ明朝; Latin and symbols come from Linux Biolinum ([窓の杜](https://forest.watch.impress.co.jp/library/software/geneiantique/)).
  - About 18,000 glyphs, including every Japanese kanji in Source Han Sans. Vertical setting is supported, and some symbols come from GL-アンチック ([okoneya](https://okoneya.jp/font/genei-antique.html)).

  - Siblings: 源暎ぽっぷる (POP style for casual manga dialogue), 源暎エムゴ, 源暎きわみゴ and more ([okoneya downloads](https://okoneya.jp/font/download.html)).
- **Shippori Antique and Shippori Antique B1** (FONTDASU, OFL):
  - Made to follow manga dialogue standards.
  - Thick kana derived from Shippori Mincho, which is itself based on Taishō-era 築地体 type; kanji from Genseki Gothic, a Source Han derivative.
  - B1 adds rounded corners and ink pooling ([Google Fonts repo](https://github.com/google/fonts/tree/main/ofl/shipporiantique), [README](https://github.com/fontdasu/ShipporiAntique)).
- **Zen Antique and Zen Antique Soft** (Yoshimichi Ohira, OFL):
  - Here "antique" means old-fashioned (古風). Hiragana and Latin are lighter; katakana and kanji are heavier.
  - It is not described as a manga アンチゴチ face ([repo](https://github.com/googlefonts/zen-antique)), so it suits posters and narration better than dialogue (my judgement).

## Implications for the lettering software

1. **Units and canvas.**
   - Lay out in millimetres on the final trim. Default to B6 (128×182 mm); also offer 新書判 (112×174) and B5 (182×257) ([sizes](https://www.mangazenkan.com/special/890.html)).
   - Bleed of 3 mm ([futaba](https://futaba-dd.jp/dcguideline/size.html)); render at 600 dpi ([comici](https://comics.comici.jp/episodes/6d3288898168b)).
   - Keep all text inside an inner "safe" frame.

2. **Japanese dialogue defaults.**

   | Setting | Default |
   |---|---|
   | Font | 源暎アンチック (OFL), falling back to Shippori Antique (OFL) |
   | Size (B6) | **12.5Q** = 3.125 mm ≈ 8.9 pt ≈ 74 px at 600 dpi. Range 11–13Q (§2.4 arithmetic). B5 ≈ 16Q; A5 ≈ 13–14Q |
   | Line spacing | 130% |
   | Ruby | Half size (6.25Q), right side of the column, 150% line spacing when present |
   | Line length | At most 10 characters per line, 3–4 lines per balloon |
   | Line breaks | The script supplies them at phrase units (for example with a `/` marker). Only auto-wrap as a fallback, with 禁則 enforced |

   - Enforce the JLReq line-start and line-end classes.
   - Apply `vert`. Rotate the R-class glyphs (…, ―) if the font has no vertical form.
   - Set !! and !? as a half-width 縦中横 pair, or as U+203C or U+2049 when the font's character map has them.
   - Always emit …… and ―― as pairs. House style: no 。 at the end of a balloon.
   - Build per-codepoint font routing (kana → antique face, kanji and Latin → Gothic face). Then a licensed Morisawa AN1 + B101 pair can be swapped in later without code changes.

3. **Role fonts (Japanese).**

   | Role | Font |
   |---|---|
   | Thought | Zen Maru Gothic (the スーラ role) |
   | Narration boxes | Shippori Mincho B1 |
   | Shouts | Dela Gothic One or Reggae One, 1.3–1.6× the dialogue size, in a burst balloon |
   | PA or bureaucratic voice | BIZ UDGothic in an angular polygon balloon |
   | Radio or loudspeaker | Double-outline angular balloon plus a broken outline for static |
   | Typed forms | BIZ UDMincho |
   | Signage and title burst | Kaisei Tokumin |

   Use 傍点 for stressed words.

4. **Balloon generator.**
   - Shape types: ellipse, burst, cloud with a bubble trail, flash, uni-flash, black flash, dashed, wavy, angular, double-angular, broken outline, caption rectangle, borderless monologue.
   - Tails: aim at the mouth and stop about halfway. Also support the inward-notch tail, the thought-bubble chain, no tail for off-scene crowds, and linked balloons (only the balloon nearest the head gets a tail; the rest join by overlap or a band).
   - Proportions: size each balloon from its text box per language, tall for Japanese and wide for English.
   - Starting values (my choices, unverified): outline 0.3 mm at print, inner padding about 0.8 em.
   - Automated checks: order balloons right to left, top to bottom; flag crossed tails; forbid covering faces (take face boxes from the art).

5. **SFX engine.**
   - Turn OFL display glyphs into outlines. Apply per-glyph scale ramps, rotation jitter, perspective along the motion vector, and a white outline of 0.6–0.8 mm ([oekaki-zukan](https://oekaki-zukan.com/articles/31084)). Allow solid or hollow letters, and allow SFX to break panel borders.
   - Script choice: katakana for machines and impacts, hiragana for cute and soft sounds.
   - Size tiers relative to the dialogue em (my defaults): quiet 1–1.5×, normal 2–4×, big 6–10×, and panel-filling.
   - Suggested pairings:

     | Sound | Font |
     |---|---|
     | Explosions | Potta One or Dela Gothic One |
     | Mecha | Rampart One or Reggae One |
     | Trams | Train One (JIS level 1 kanji only, fine for kana SFX) |
     | Electronics | DotGothic16 |
     | The recruit's bubbly asides | Hachi Maru Pop or Mochiy Pop One |

6. **English edition.**
   - Keep right-to-left page order, the post-2002 norm.
   - Redraw the balloons wide.
   - Dialogue in Comic Neue Bold, set in caps, at about 8.5–9 pt on B6 (unverified default). Bold italic for emphasis; three-dot ellipses; double dashes.
   - Offer three SFX modes: (a) replace (Viz), (b) keep the Japanese plus a small English subtitle (Yen and Tokyopop style), (c) margin notes (Dark Horse). **Mode (b) suits a "translated in-universe artefact" best.**
   - Keep honorifics, since the story is set in Japan and relationships matter.
   - Avoid Anime Ace (licence grey zone) and CC Wild Words (paid).

7. **Licence manifest.** Ship a `fonts.json` that records each font's licence (OFL, Apache 2.0 or Windows) and source URL, and fail the build if a font without an entry is used.

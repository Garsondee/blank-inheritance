# Propaganda, institutional comics, and documents that tell on themselves

*Part of the [manga research shelf](README.md), gathered 2026-09-26 for [`design/propaganda-manga.md`](../../propaganda-manga.md). **Research, not lore, and not a decision:** it says what the sources say and what might transfer, and the plan decides. Every factual claim links its source; **(unverified)** marks what could not be confirmed. It names real works, people and places because it is GM-facing; none of them belongs in the comic. It covers hard history (wartime propaganda, a nuclear disaster, political purges) for its techniques only: the campaign's register stays warm and funny (`feedback_tone_giant_robo_not_eva`), and the Directorate is "calcified rather than sinister" (`ideas/story/dossier-the-directorate.md` §1).*

## The short version

- **The closest precedent is Japanese and wartime:** *Yokusan Ikka* (1940–41), a cheerful cartoon family licensed to many artists, drawn by readers too, whose comedy normalised neighbours watching one another.
- **Humour teaches the job:** Eisner's *PS* magazine taught soldiers maintenance for decades through a klutz, talking equipment and slang.
- **Jokes survive only while the institution isn't the butt:** *Norakuro* was stopped in 1941 partly for mocking the army; the US Army had Eisner's klutz removed in 1955.
- **A reassuring mascot is weakest when its claims can be checked:** *Pluto-kun* (1993) had a boy drink plutonium and was withdrawn.
- **Sexualised recruiting art backfires fast and publicly:** "it's the anime's own costume" failed in Japan in 2019; the US Army redesigned its pin-up in 1980.
- **Retouching is caught by comparing editions:** removals leave traces (too-smooth water, gaps, mismatched light), and replacement pages survive in libraries.
- **Recruitment material omits the core risk by vocabulary:** one UK Army site used "enemy" on 36 of 296 pages and never "kill".
- **Readers look at words more than pictures, and at faces within the pictures:** background detail is missed on a first read unless something fails to add up.
- **Handouts are read in fragments:** one page per sheet, and never the only route to a conclusion.
- **Fiction's see-through documents reuse a small toolkit:** fine print, self-incriminating statistics, dates that don't fit, a grin on a grim picture, a backstage counter-document.

## 1. Japanese precedents

### 1.1 Organising the drawers (1940–43)

Under wartime pressure on paper and publishing, the New Manga Faction (新漫画派集団), the Sankō Manga Studio and other groups merged into the **New Japan Cartoonists Association (新日本漫画家協会)** in 1940, with the magazine *Manga* as its organ; Kondō Hidezō was central to the merger. By 1943 younger members were taking commissions from the army and navy information offices ([ja.wikipedia: 漫画集団](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E6%BC%AB%E7%94%BB%E9%9B%86%E5%9B%A3)), and a further body, the Japan Manga Service Association (日本漫画奉公会), followed; the Shōwa-kan museum holds papers of both ([Shōwa-kan collection list](http://www.showakan.go.jp/main/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/18_showakan_collection.pdf)). The state did not need to draw anything itself. It organised the people who drew.

### 1.2 *Yokusan Ikka*, the shared family (1940–41)

- **What it was.** The association created *Yokusan Ikka* (翼賛一家, "the Imperial Rule Assistance family") in 1940, working with the Imperial Rule Assistance Association (大政翼賛会). The Yamatos were an 11-person model wartime household: father Sanpei, 48, a middle-school PE teacher; mother Tamiko, 45; seven children aged 2 to 25; and Sanpei's elderly parents ([ja.wikipedia: 翼賛一家](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E7%BF%BC%E8%B3%9B%E4%B8%80%E5%AE%B6)).
- **How it was licensed.** The character settings were fixed. Many cartoonists paid a "cooperation fee" for the right to draw the family ([ja.wikipedia](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E7%BF%BC%E8%B3%9B%E4%B8%80%E5%AE%B6)). A regional paper introduced the family on 7 December 1940 as a character free for public use. It then turned up in savings drives (1941), puppet-theatre scripts (1943), printed textiles, and the comfort parcels schoolchildren sent to soldiers ([note.com, Shinshū war-materials collector](https://note.com/sensou188/n/n9a54be54ab0b)).
- **Where it ran.** The strip ran from December 1940 in the Cabinet's photo weekly *Shashin Shūhō*, in *Manga* and in *Shūkan Asahi*, and records followed in March 1941 ([ja.wikipedia](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E7%BF%BC%E8%B3%9B%E4%B8%80%E5%AE%B6); [Niwa lab, University of Tokyo, 2019](https://media-journalism.org/blog/field-review/2019/12/02/5766/)).
- **Beloved artists drew it.** Hasegawa Machiko, later the creator of *Sazae-san*, drew a four-panel version for *Asahi Graph* from 5 February to 14 May 1941. It was collected in April 1942, and its gentle humour already resembles her postwar family strip ([ja.wikipedia: 翼賛一家大和さん](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E7%BF%BC%E8%B3%9B%E4%B8%80%E5%AE%B6%E5%A4%A7%E5%92%8C%E3%81%95%E3%82%93)).
- **Exported and simplified.** The family appeared in Taiwan, Manchuria and occupied China, where "manga instruction meetings" taught local people to draw it. The designs were deliberately simplified so that anyone could draw them. One side effect was that the characters became hard to tell apart, and the drawing technique drifted free of the message ([Niwa lab](https://media-journalism.org/blog/field-review/2019/12/02/5766/)).
- **Otsuka's reading.** Ōtsuka Eiji's book 大政翼賛会のメディアミックス (Heibonsha, December 2018) is subtitled "participatory fascism". He argues that the regime solicited readers' own submissions and fan works, and that the domestic comedy normalised the neighbourhood associations (the *tonarigumi*, which watched one another) along with the wider wartime ideology ([Heibonsha](https://www.heibonsha.co.jp/book/b377958.html)). He traces the postwar media mix and fan practice back to this campaign ([Otsuka, English article listing](https://www.academia.edu/143516119/Otsuka_Eiji_The_Wartime_Media_Mix_and_Participatory_Fascism_as_the_Internalization_of_an_Information_Space)).

**Why it matters here:** a warm family comic was the delivery system for mutual surveillance, and its readers ended up drawing their own propaganda. The campaign's themes are mass surveillance and runaway systems; this precedent sits squarely on the first.

### 1.3 *Norakuro*: the comedy of failure, and its end

*Norakuro* began in January 1931 in Kodansha's *Shōnen Kurabu* ([Google Arts & Culture](https://artsandculture.google.com/story/ggWBxJ2LommkKQ)). A stray dog joins the Fierce Dog Regiment and rises from private to captain, fighting a "pig army" on "the continent", a thin cover for the war in China. Its creator had served in the army from 1919 to 1922, and the magazine's circulation reached 750,000 by 1936 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norakuro)). In 1941 the Interior Ministry objected that such frivolous manga was unacceptable in wartime. It cited paper shortages and complained that the jokes about military blunders ridiculed the forces. Norakuro retired as a captain and went to the continent prospecting, where he found a gold mine ([ja.wikipedia: のらくろ](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%81%AE%E3%82%89%E3%81%8F%E3%82%8D)). The strip's engine was comic failure, and that stopped being acceptable once the institution hardened.

### 1.4 National-policy kamishibai (国策紙芝居)

The Japanese Educational Kamishibai Association was founded by Matsunaga Ken'ya in 1938 and produced the first national-policy kamishibai the same year ([Hoover Institution, *Fanning the Flames*](https://fanningtheflames.hoover.org/shorthand-story/8)). Kamishibai is paper theatre: a narrator slides illustrated cards through a small wooden frame ([Hoover](https://fanningtheflames.hoover.org/shorthand-story/7)). These were standalone stories of about twenty cards. The performer had to follow strict written instructions on the backs of the cards, and scripts were censored before publication under regulations issued in 1938 ([same](https://fanningtheflames.hoover.org/shorthand-story/8)). The stories were informational, exhortatory or offered heroes to emulate. Titles included *Tonarigumi* (1941, neighbourhood associations), *Tetsu no Kobito* (1941, recycling) and *Kokoro no Kagi* (1943, building shelters). The uniforms were pristine, and neither battlefields nor enemies were shown ([same](https://fanningtheflames.hoover.org/shorthand-story/8)). Kanagawa University holds 241 of these works, mostly from 1938–41 ([Kanagawa University](http://himoji.kanagawa-u.ac.jp/english/collection/index.html)). **The script travelled with the pictures and dictated the voice.** A GM reading the manga aloud would stand where that performer stood.

### 1.5 *Momotarō: Sacred Sailors* (1945)

The Navy Ministry's feature-length animation has cute animal soldiers make a paratroop landing, then teach the island animals the Japanese syllabary in song, framing occupation as kindness. A negative long presumed destroyed was found in 1983 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Momotaro:_Sacred_Sailors)).

### 1.6 Postwar explainers, white papers and mascots

- **Educational manga (学習まんが) conventions.** Gakken's manga line began in 1975. Shogakukan's 1981 Japanese-history series started the 1980s history-manga boom and had sold more than 20 million copies by 2018. The standard devices are a guide (a teacher, mascot or knowledgeable adult) explaining to curious children, narration boxes, subject experts supervising, and titles on the pattern "understand it through manga" (まんがでわかる). Companies and government offices use the same form for PR ([ja.wikipedia: 学習漫画](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%AD%A6%E7%BF%92%E6%BC%AB%E7%94%BB)).
- **The Defence White Paper in manga.** The Ministry of Defense issues まんがで読む防衛白書. The FY2015 edition (A5, 70 pages, February 2016) centred on building the Japan–US bond ([government bookstore](https://www.gov-book.or.jp/book/detail.php?product_id=304729)). The FY2018 edition (March 2019, 38 pages) covered the Self-Defense Forces' (SDF) daily activities and is catalogued as a children's book ([National Diet Library](https://ndlsearch.ndl.go.jp/books/R100000002-I029620918)).
- **An alliance explained by children.** US Forces Japan published *Our Alliance – A Lasting Partnership* around the treaty's 50th anniversary, with the team behind *Magical Marine Pixel Maritan*. Its leads, Anzu Arai and Usa-kun, have names that pun on "alliance" and "USA", and CNN noted that it presents the relationship very positively ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Our_Alliance_%E2%80%93_A_Lasting_Partnership)).
- **Mascots with ranks, and with surveillance built into their design.** The Tokyo police mascot Pipo-kun (17 April 1987) has big ears to hear citizens' voices, an antenna to catch social trends, and wide eyes to watch society ([ja.wikipedia: ピーポくん](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%83%94%E3%83%BC%E3%83%9D%E3%81%8F%E3%82%93)). The Coast Guard's harp-seal mascot Umimaru (10 April 1998) holds a coast guard officer's rank, and his name was chosen by public submission ([ja.wikipedia: うみまる](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%81%86%E3%81%BF%E3%81%BE%E3%82%8B)). The Tokyo Fire Department's Kyuta has an antenna for sensing hazards and a thumbs-up meaning "leave disasters to us" ([ja.wikipedia: キュータ](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%82%AD%E3%83%A5%E3%83%BC%E3%82%BF)).

### 1.7 Nuclear PR before 2011

- **Atoms for Peace exhibitions (1955–57).** The US Information Agency co-sponsored these with Japanese newspapers, among them the *Yomiuri*. About 2.6 million people visited across ten cities, and the Hiroshima leg (27 May – 17 June 1956) was held in the Peace Memorial Museum ([ja.wikipedia: 原子力平和利用博覧会](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%8E%9F%E5%AD%90%E5%8A%9B%E5%B9%B3%E5%92%8C%E5%88%A9%E7%94%A8%E5%8D%9A%E8%A6%A7%E4%BC%9A)). Accounts that the museum's bomb exhibits were moved out to make room are (unverified). The *Yomiuri*'s owner, Shōriki Matsutarō, chaired the new Atomic Energy Commission from January 1956 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matsutar%C5%8D_Sh%C5%8Driki)).
- ***Pluto-kun, Our Reliable Friend* (プルト君).** The Power Reactor and Nuclear Fuel Development Corporation (動燃) made this 11-minute animation in 1990 and revised it in 1993 ([imidas](https://imidas.jp/ryuko/detail/N-101-1420.html)); about 250 copies went to PR centres ([ja.wikipedia](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%83%97%E3%83%AB%E3%83%88%E3%83%8B%E3%82%A6%E3%83%A0%E7%89%A9%E8%AA%9E_%E9%A0%BC%E3%82%8C%E3%82%8B%E4%BB%B2%E9%96%93%E3%83%97%E3%83%AB%E3%83%88%E5%90%9B)). The mascot opens dressed as a ghost, likening fear of plutonium to fear of ghosts. It claims reactor plutonium is too impure for bombs and theft impossible, says alpha radiation cannot pass skin and swallowed plutonium mostly passes through, and walks through criminals poisoning a reservoir ([Pink Tentacle](https://pinktentacle.com/2011/03/cute-pluto-kun-cartoon-dispels-plutonium-fears/)). A boy drinks plutonium solution, reassured that it is not instantly fatal like cyanide. The US Secretary of Energy (Hazel O'Leary at the time, inferred from the dates) asked for its withdrawal as underplaying the danger, local residents called it excessive safety PR, and it was withdrawn ([ja.wikipedia](https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E3%83%97%E3%83%AB%E3%83%88%E3%83%8B%E3%82%A6%E3%83%A0%E7%89%A9%E8%AA%9E_%E9%A0%BC%E3%82%8C%E3%82%8B%E4%BB%B2%E9%96%93%E3%83%97%E3%83%AB%E3%83%88%E5%90%9B)).
- **Claims that could later be checked.** In May 2010 the head of the nuclear safety agency told parliament that the plants were so well designed that a total loss of cooling was practically impossible. The later parliamentary commission called the Fukushima disaster profoundly man-made ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_power_in_Japan)).
- **Futaba.** Futaba's entrance arch carried a banner reading roughly "nuclear power: energy for a bright future" and was photographed derelict in 2013 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Futaba,_Fukushima)). That the slogan came from a local schoolchild's contest entry is widely reported but (unverified). So are the 2010 school readers that reassured pupils about earthquakes and tsunamis and were withdrawn in 2011 (unverified).

### 1.8 SDF anime tie-ins and "moe" recruiting, and what went wrong

(Moe: the anime style of cute, endearing characters.)

- **The trend.** The SDF's Tokushima recruiting office has run moe posters every year since 2010. Okayama brought in three cute mascots and reported about 20% more volunteers in 2013. The 4th Anti-Tank Helicopter Squadron painted moe girls on its AH-1S Cobras (2011–13), and female members dressed as the characters at air shows ([Vice](https://www.vice.com/en/article/the-japanese-military-is-getting-offensively-cute/)). Ibaraki's April 2014 posters gave each branch a girl (Koume for land, Nobara for sea, Hibari for air). Supporters pointed to the SDF's peacetime work in peacekeeping, rescue and disaster relief, while critics said it made the forces look like a children's summer camp ([SoraNews24](https://soranews24.com/2014/04/09/more-jsdf-recruitment-posters-get-a-moe-makeover-in-ibaraki/)). In July 2015 the Tokyo office tied in with the anime *Gate*, written by a former Ground SDF member, using a slogan about becoming someone who can protect someone ([SoraNews24](https://soranews24.com/2015/07/24/new-anime-gate-entertains-tries-to-recruit-you-to-the-japan-self-defense-forces-at-the-same-time/)).
- **Scholars' readings.** Yamamura reads this as the military being turned into fantasy "content" to be consumed, rather than a revival of militarism ([Yamamura, *Journal of War & Culture Studies*](https://eprints.lib.hokudai.ac.jp/dspace/handle/2115/74922)). Frühstück, the first scholar allowed through SDF basic training, describes a PR emphasis on "cute" popular culture and on militarised masculinity and femininity, used to win legitimacy ([*Uneasy Warriors*, UC Press 2007](https://www.ucpress.edu/books/uneasy-warriors/paper)). She calls the process the "moe-fication" of militarism ([Wikipedia: Maritan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magical_Marine_Pixel_Maritan)).
- **What went wrong (1): the Shiga poster, 2019.** The Shiga office put up posters of three *Strike Witches* characters in the show's very short uniforms at six sites in the prefecture and online ([J-CAST](https://www.j-cast.com/2019/03/01351606.html)). In February 2019 people complained it amounted to sexual harassment. The office first defended the image as "pants, not underwear", following the show's own convention, then took the posters down and deleted the image ([SoraNews24](https://soranews24.com/2019/03/09/pantie-flashing-anime-girl-japan-self-defense-forces-recruiting-poster-shot-down-by-critics/)).
- **What went wrong (2): Connie Rodd, 1950s–1980.** The US Army's *PS* magazine drew its women (Connie, and later Bonnie) as pin-ups. A 1972 *San Francisco Chronicle* piece attacked Connie's appearance in an M16 manual. After pressure from Congresswoman Bella Abzug and Senators Proxmire and Hatch, both characters were redesigned in March 1980 to look professional and modest ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PS_Magazine); [TCJ](https://www.tcj.com/a-brief-history-of-one-of-the-original-explainers-preventive-maintenance-monthly/)).
- **What went wrong (3): tone.** On 14 March 2024 the Ground SDF's 1st Division posted an anniversary poster parodying an old comedy-show title as "Emergency! Everyone gather!", built around an unexplained "13.2%" (the article reads it as survey data on willingness to fight). It was deleted after criticism ([MAG2 News](https://www.mag2.com/p/news/595228)).
- **The lessons.** The recruiting channel changes what an image means, so "it's the official costume" is no defence. The trigger is teen-coded bodies, sexual framing and state endorsement together. Both real fixes were redesigns toward competence, jokes about the war itself read as trivialising, and critics also flag art that uses a group as decoration ([ForcesWatch/Medact 2019](https://www.forceswatch.net/wp-content/uploads/Selling_the_Military_WEB.pdf)).

## 2. Elsewhere

### 2.1 Eisner: teaching the job through jokes (*Army Motors*, then *PS*)

In the Second World War, Corporal Will Eisner created Private Joe Dope, who embodied every maintenance mistake, and Corporal Connie Rodd for *Army Motors*. When the Korean War showed that maintenance guidance was needed quickly, the Army hired Eisner's company, and *PS* launched in June 1951 as a "postscript" to the technical manuals. Eisner directed it until 1971 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PS_Magazine)). His techniques: a klutz whose failures are memorable, soldiers' own slang, illustrated letters answered by Master Sergeant Half-Mast, continuity strips, and equipment drawn with faces and feelings so that soldiers felt responsible for it ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PS_Magazine)).

A 1955 study, *Humor and the American Soldier*, defended the slang against officers who objected. Because *PS* invited letters, readers exposed real design faults, which annoyed ordnance officers. Circulation reached about 190,000 by 1989 ([TCJ](https://www.tcj.com/a-brief-history-of-one-of-the-original-explainers-preventive-maintenance-monthly/)). Joe Dope and a fellow klutz were **removed in 1955 after Army complaints**. Eisner's comic M16A1 manual (1968–69) went to troops in Vietnam ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PS_Magazine)).

### 2.2 The atom made friendly

- ***Dagwood Splits the Atom* (1949).** Joe Musial of King Features' educational division made it at General Groves's suggestion, with Groves writing the preface, and distributed it free to schools ([Comics Kingdom](https://comicskingdom.com/trending/blog/2012/10/10/ask-the-archivist-dagwood-splits-the-atom)). Mandrake shrinks the family to atom size and Dagwood splits a uranium atom. There are pages on industry, medicine and farming, and it ends on a Lincoln quotation. **The bomb itself is absent** ([Gizmodo](https://gizmodo.com/when-dagwood-bumstead-split-the-atom-5908636)). The strip's famous creator's photo appears in the book, but Musial drew all of it ([Comics Kingdom](https://comicskingdom.com/trending/blog/2012/10/10/ask-the-archivist-dagwood-splits-the-atom)).
- ***Adventures Inside the Atom* (General Electric, 1948).** A GE scientist, Ed Powers, walks his little brother through an atomic exhibit. The story showcases GE's own Hanford and Knolls facilities and shows safety as mechanical hands handling hot material ([Google Arts & Culture / miSci](https://artsandculture.google.com/story/adventures-inside-the-atom-museum-of-innovation-and-science/gAVR41UqMct2Iw?hl=en)). It was **reissued in 1955 and 1965, with art and text reworked** to keep up with a changing reality ([ORAU](https://www.orau.org/health-physics-museum/collection/toys/comics/inside-the-atom.html)).

### 2.3 Civil defence

The Federal Civil Defense Administration (FCDA) issued a 16-page booklet, *Bert the Turtle Says Duck and Cover*, in 1951 ([UNL Media Commons](https://mediacommons.unl.edu/luna/servlet/detail/UNL~113~113~604~1453573:Bert-the-turtle-says-duck-and-cover)). The film followed in 1952 and was shown to millions of schoolchildren ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duck_and_Cover_(film))). The National Park Service's history says the messaging stressed survivability while underplaying the hydrogen bomb and fallout, until the 1954 Castle Bravo test made fallout undeniable ([NPS](https://home.nps.gov/articles/coldwar_civildefense_thru-ike.htm)). The FCDA's "Alert America" push reportedly put out about 400 million pieces of survival literature (unverified; [Virginia Museum of History & Culture](https://virginiahistory.org/learn/duck-and-cover)). One late-1950s civil-defence comic had Li'l Abner on its cover but not in the story ([ORAU](https://www.orau.org/health-physics-museum/collection/civil-defense/miscellaneous/comic.html)).

### 2.4 Recruitment and conversion comics (US)

- ***Steve Canyon*.** The Air Force gave Milton Caniff's pilot hero a real serial number, and its Chief of Staff called him "an officer in my command". When the strip ended in 1988 the Air Force "retired" him with a full personnel record ([Air & Space Forces Magazine](https://www.airandspaceforces.com/article/0413caniff/)).
- ***Treasure Chest*.** This subscription comic, distributed in Catholic schools from 1946 to 1972, ran *This Godless Communism* from 28 September 1961, opening on America under Soviet rule ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/This_Godless_Communism)); an FBI-director foreword is reported (unverified). The Catechetical Guild's *Is This Tomorrow* (1947) imagined a communist coup and was briefly banned in Detroit for gore ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Is_This_Tomorrow)).
- ***America's Army* comics.** IDW published these with the Army from 2013 to 2015 as tie-ins to the recruiting game ([ComicBook.com](https://comicbook.com/comicbook/news/u-s-army-teams-with-idw-publishing-for-americas-army-comics/)).

### 2.5 China: lianhuanhua and Lei Feng

Lianhuanhua (连环画) were palm-sized books with one image and a few lines of text per page. In 1985 alone, 8.1 billion copies were printed ([TCJ](https://www.tcj.com/lianhuanhua-chinas-pulp-comics/)). After 1949 they were used to spread new government policy. They faded in 1966, and Zhou Enlai revived them in the early 1970s with heroic stories ([University of Hawaiʻi Library](https://digital.library.manoa.hawaii.edu/storybook)).

The model soldier **Lei Feng** died on 15 August 1962, and Mao launched the "Learn from Comrade Lei Feng" campaign on 5 March 1963. Twelve professional-quality photos showed him doing good deeds. AP reporters questioned them first, and scholars now think they were commissioned re-enactments for a travelling exhibition. His diary of about 200,000 words was long thought forged; newer work argues it was edited rather than invented ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lei_Feng)). Material about him existed before his death because he had already been named an "Economizing Model Soldier" in September 1960, and his image then poured out as posters and comic strips ([chineseposters.net](https://chineseposters.net/themes/leifeng)). **The tell was too much good documentation of an ordinary private.**

### 2.6 Soviet agitprop and the vanishing commissar

- **ROSTA windows.** The telegraph agency's ROSTA windows (September 1919 – February 1922) were sequenced like comic strips, usually in 4 to 12 panels. Teams stencilled them and hung them in shop windows, stations and markets, and Mayakovsky wrote most of the texts ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ROSTA_windows)).
- **What retouchers actually did.**
  - Nikolai Yezhov, walking beside Stalin at the Moscow–Volga canal (April 1937), was painted out and replaced by water.
  - A 1917 shop sign reading "Watches, gold and silver" was repainted as a revolutionary slogan.
  - Trotsky and Kamenev were cut from Lenin photos for a 1967 book.
  - A hall attendant pointing the way was erased so that Stalin would not seem to need guidance.
  - Politburo faces were pasted into a 1953 photomontage of Stalin lying in state ([Newseum: *Photographic Lies*](https://newseumed.org/tools/artifact/photographic-lies-stalins-russia-online-exhibit)).
- **The warmest-looking example.** Six-year-old Gelya Markizova hugging Stalin (1936) was captioned "Friend of the Little Children". A party secretary standing beside her was removed from reproductions, and her father was shot the next year ([Newseum](https://newseumed.org/tools/artifact/photographic-lies-stalins-russia-online-exhibit)). Parade marchers carried the image's slogan, "Thank you, Comrade Stalin, for our happy childhood!" ([NotMoscow](https://nemoskva.net/en/2026/07/06/gelya-markizova-devochka-s-portreta-stalina/)).
- **Readers censoring their own copies.** Alexander Rodchenko inked out the purged officials' faces in his own copy of the book *Ten Years of Uzbekistan* (1934), and a later printing cut them out cleanly ([Rauner Library, Dartmouth](https://raunerlibrary.blogspot.com/2014/02/10-let-uzbekistan.html)).
- **Replacement pages.** After Beria was executed in 1953, subscribers to the *Great Soviet Encyclopedia* were sent pages to paste in: expanded entries on the Bering Sea and neighbouring topics, with instructions to destroy his article. UC Berkeley had received them by April 1954 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Soviet_Encyclopedia)).
- **How it was noticed.**
  - The traces themselves: unnatural water, gaps in a composition, mismatched light ([Newseum](https://newseumed.org/tools/artifact/photographic-lies-stalins-russia-online-exhibit)).
  - Comparing printings. David King began collecting in 1970, gathered about a quarter of a million images, and set versions side by side in *The Commissar Vanishes* (1997) ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Commissar_Vanishes); [Photographers' Gallery](https://thephotographersgallery.org.uk/whats-on/stories-russia-david-king-collection)).
  - Institutions quietly keeping both versions, as the Berkeley library did.

## 3. Theory

### 3.1 The Institute for Propaganda Analysis's seven devices (1937)

The Institute was founded on 23 September 1937 and suspended in January 1942, when neutral analysis seemed impossible in wartime. Its seven devices: **name-calling** (a bad label for the opponent), **glittering generality** (appealing, empty words), **transfer** (a respected symbol's borrowed prestige), **testimonial** (an authority's endorsement), **plain folks** (the message as ordinary people's), **card-stacking** (only the favourable facts) and **bandwagon** (everyone's joining) ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Propaganda_Analysis)). Critics call the list too simple, because real messages mix devices ([same](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_for_Propaganda_Analysis)). It still maps cleanly onto a recruitment manga: the bubbly ordinary recruit (plain folks), the emblem on everything (transfer), a commander's foreword or a famous creator's photo on a book he didn't draw (testimonial), the missing bomb (card-stacking), and "everyone's signing up!" (bandwagon).

### 3.2 Ellul: agitation and integration

Jacques Ellul (*Propagandes*, 1962) separates **agitation** propaganda, which stirs people to revolt and destroy, from **integration** propaganda. Integration propaganda is the long, quiet adjustment of people to an established order's patterns, and established institutions are the ones that use it ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propaganda:_The_Formation_of_Men%27s_Attitudes)). He also describes "sociological" propaganda that spreads through a way of life, argues that educated people are the most susceptible, and holds that propaganda must be continuous ([same](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propaganda:_The_Formation_of_Men%27s_Attitudes)). Jowett and O'Donnell likewise describe integrative propaganda as making an audience passive, accepting and unchallenging ([Jowett & O'Donnell, 4th ed., ch. 1, p. 16](https://www.ffri.hr/~ibrdar/komunikacija/seminari/Propaganda%20&%20persuasion%20-%20difference%20(Chapter1).pdf)). **The Directorate's manga is integration propaganda:** it asks readers to settle in, not to rise up.

### 3.3 Jowett and O'Donnell: white, grey, black

These definitions come from pages 16–21 of the [same chapter](https://www.ffri.hr/~ibrdar/komunikacija/seminari/Propaganda%20&%20persuasion%20-%20difference%20(Chapter1).pdf):

- **White propaganda** names its source correctly and is broadly accurate. It is built to earn credibility that can be spent later.
- **Grey propaganda** may or may not name its real source, and its accuracy is uncertain. Their examples include identical "letters from soldiers" printed in 11 US newspapers in 2003, which the named soldiers had not written, and companies that distort statistics in annual reports.
- **Black propaganda** comes from a false or hidden source and trades in lies.

A publication signed by the Directorate is white propaganda. It turns grey the moment a "letter from the heroine" was written by the press office.

### 3.4 How recruitment media idealise the journey

ForcesWatch and Medact's 2019 analysis of UK campaigns found three traits: the forces make your best self, the belonging there is unique, and self-fulfilment is detached from what the institution is for. Its five themes: service life shown as superior to civilian life; risks, legal obligations and daily reality glossed over; diversity used as decoration; camaraderie that hides loneliness; and personal growth that takes the politics out of the purpose. The pitch is an escape from limited opportunity into respect ([*Selling the Military*](https://www.forceswatch.net/wp-content/uploads/Selling_the_Military_WEB.pdf)).

David Gee's *Informed Choice?* (2007) found the minimum service terms buried and confusing ([ForcesWatch](https://www.forceswatch.net/resources/informed-choice-armed-forces-recruitment-practice-in-the-united-kingdom/)), and traced the omission down to vocabulary: the Army Jobs website used "enemy" on 36 of 296 pages and never "kill", the infantry brochure spoke of "decisive strikes", and a costly real battle got a brief, misleading account ([review summary](https://layreadersbookreviews.wordpress.com/2016/12/25/informed-choice-armed-forces-recruitment-practice-in-the-united-kingdom-david-gee/)).

### 3.5 Words that say one thing while the picture shows another

- **McCloud's word/picture combinations.** Scott McCloud's *Understanding Comics* (1993, chapter 6) sorts panels into seven kinds: word-specific, picture-specific, duo-specific, additive, **parallel** (words and pictures follow separate courses that never intersect), montage, and interdependent, which is the most common ([summary](https://laurenericksondotcom.wordpress.com/2011/09/26/word-image-combinations-in-scott-mcclouds-show-and-tell/); [notes](https://kate-nepveu.livejournal.com/116874.html)). The parallel mode is the tool for this manga: a caption that narrates the brochure over a picture of something else.
- **Picturebook theory's "counterpoint".** Picturebook scholars call this counterpoint, including irony and contradiction ([Nikolajeva & Scott, *How Picturebooks Work*, 2001](https://www.routledge.com/How-Picturebooks-Work/Nikolajeva-Scott/p/book/9780415979689)). The textbook case is *Rosie's Walk* (1968). Its text follows a hen's placid stroll and never mentions the fox who chases her and suffers accident after accident in every picture. Nodelman's reading is that the irony comes from each medium exposing what the other leaves out ([case study](https://whatmakesasuccessfulpicturebook.wordpress.com/)).
- **Where eyes actually go.** In one eye-tracking study, text drew more attention than images despite covering far less of the panel, and expert comics readers gave pictures more attention than novices did ([Yang 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11726626/)). A picture-story study found viewers, in its example, looking mostly at characters' faces, and that when a causal step is missing they make about 20% more fixations, hunting the regions that would explain the gap ([Hutson, Magliano & Loschky 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6587724/)).
- **The implication.** The caption gets read and the background gets glanced at. A contradiction therefore belongs where the eye goes second (a face, a sign, a number), or it should be triggered by a small gap that sends the reader searching.

## 5. Tabletop handout craft

- ***Masks of Nyarlathotep* (Chaosium, 1984).** The box shipped a cardstock sheet of cut-out handouts (clippings, letters, business cards, a matchbox). From 1989 the handouts were printed inside the books, and a historian of the game credits their tactile realism with holding players between action scenes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masks_of_Nyarlathotep)).
- **The HPLHS prop set for *Masks*.** It is sorted into location folders on period paper, and some items are crumpled or torn. Newspaper clippings carry real period adverts and articles on the back. An audio item comes with advice to play it once and withhold the transcript so that players must remember it ([HPLHS](https://www.hplhs.org/masksgps.php)).
- ***Delta Green*.** Agents operate undercover inside real agencies, and a Bureaucracy skill governs requisitions ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_Green)).
- ***Trail of Cthulhu*.** Its GUMSHOE rules hand over core clues automatically, and points buy the extras ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trail_of_Cthulhu)). The improvisational campaign *The Armitage Files* (2010) is reportedly built on a bundle of cryptic documents that players interpret and the GM improvises from (structure unverified).
- **The Three Clue Rule.** For any conclusion you need players to reach, plant at least three clues, because they will miss one, ignore one and misread one. Its corollaries are to reward creative finds, have the world push new clues when players stall, sometimes simply tell them, and trust their surprising solutions ([The Alexandrian, 2008](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/1118/roleplaying-games/three-clue-rule)).
- **How people actually read handouts at the table:**
  - Keep a handout to one page, because pages get shuffled. Players read in fragments while play continues, scanning for what matters, so hand it out in downtime ([Gnome Stew, 2015](https://gnomestew.com/getting-handy-with-gm-handouts/)).
  - Use single sheets on heavier paper with legible type. Don't stack codes on codes. Collect handouts and return them next session as memory aids, and redirect attention if a prop distracts ([Gnome Stew, 2024](https://gnomestew.com/adventure-design-maps-and-pc-handouts/)).
  - For contracts, "Anything longer than a page is probably overkill"; one player kept their signed copy with their campaign notes ([The Alexandrian, 2025](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/53028/roleplaying-games/ptolus-running-the-campaign-contract-handouts)).
  - Give an art handout to the player whose check succeeded, so that they voice the expert reading ([The Alexandrian, 2025](https://thealexandrian.net/wordpress/53188/roleplaying-games/ptolus-running-the-campaign-art-handouts)).
- **Reading time (my estimate).** Proofreading runs about 200 words a minute on paper and 180 on a screen ([Wikipedia, citing Ziefle 1998](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Words_per_minute)). A cover plus four pages, at about 24 panels and 400–500 words, should take a first reader roughly two to four minutes: the words, plus a second or two per panel of looking. At the table that will be one skim, likely by one or two players reading aloud. The layer meant for careful readers should expect a second reading between sessions or in downtime, and **it will not be found at the table unless a visible gap prompts a search.**
- **Physical or digital.** Paper wins on tactility and keepsakes; screen reading is slightly slower. A digital copy can be quietly **reissued**, as the Soviet encyclopedia and GE's comics were, and players who kept the first version can compare.

## 6. What this suggests for a cover plus four pages

Observations, not decisions:

1. **Two reading speeds:** a cheerful three-minute story that teaches the job, then a set of seams (caption against art, a statistic, a date, a background sign, a mismatched face).
2. **One seam per page is plenty,** each also supported elsewhere (Three Clue Rule), so the manga is never the only route to a truth.
3. **Keep the jokes warm and never aimed at the institution;** real institutions cut exactly those jokes, and careful readers can notice who is never the butt.
4. **The heroine is funny through what she does, never how she is posed;** no sexualised framing, not even as satire of the PR office.
5. **A later "revised edition"** with one panel quietly changed is a cheap, strong follow-up for a table that keeps handouts.

## A toolkit of techniques

1. **The open character sheet.** Publish the heroine and squad as characters anyone may draw, and print "reader submissions" showing what readers were allowed to imagine. Precedent: *Yokusan Ikka*, 1940–41.
2. **A house style simple enough to copy.** Lines a cadet can doodle; the side effect is look-alike characters. Precedent: *Yokusan Ikka*'s drawing lessons in occupied territories.
3. **Domestic comedy that normalises watching.** Neighbourly reporting framed as warmth and good manners. Precedent: *Yokusan Ikka* and the neighbourhood associations; the kamishibai *Tonarigumi*, 1941.
4. **The helpful klutz.** A sidekick who gets everything wrong, so the lesson lands as a laugh. Precedent: Joe Dope, *Army Motors* and *PS*.
5. **Machines with feelings.** Equipment that talks and sulks, so readers feel responsible for it. Precedent: *PS* magazine.
6. **The guide explains to a newcomer.** Careful readers notice the questions nobody asks. Precedent: 学習まんが conventions; the manga Defence White Paper.
7. **A mascot with surveillance organs.** Ears for citizens, an antenna for trends, eyes for everything, all presented as friendliness. Precedent: Pipo-kun, 1987; Kyuta's hazard antenna.
8. **A service number for a cartoon.** The institution claims the character as a real member, with a personnel file. Precedent: Steve Canyon's serial number and 1988 "retirement"; Umimaru's rank.
9. **Fine print that voids the promise in the only case that matters.** Precedent: the *Fallout* manual's guarantee footnote.
10. **Statistics that tell on themselves.** Capacity against beds, budget against overrun, a bare unexplained percentage. Precedent: the *Fallout* manual's vault statistics; the 2024 "13.2%" SDF poster.
11. **A date that doesn't fit.** A document promised for after the disaster, or a record that predates the person's fame. Precedent: the *Fallout* manual's "available 2078"; Lei Feng.
12. **The vocabulary audit.** Every word for the job except the one for its core risk. Precedent: Gee's "enemy" on 36 of 296 pages, "kill" on none.
13. **The missing centrepiece.** Explain the whole machine and leave out what it is for. Precedent: *Dagwood Splits the Atom*'s absent bomb; the kamishibai's pristine uniforms.
14. **The danger as a cute friend.** Reassurance by comparison ("not instantly fatal") and fear called superstition. Precedent: Pluto-kun, 1993.
15. **Borrowed prestige.** A famous face on the cover who isn't in the story, or a credit for work someone else did. Precedent: the civil-defence comic's cover star; the *Dagwood* creator's photo; the IPA's "testimonial".
16. **The sponsor as destination.** The heroes' reward is a tour of the publisher's own facilities. Precedent: GE's *Adventures Inside the Atom*.
17. **Words and pictures on parallel tracks.** The caption narrates the brochure; the art shows something else. Precedent: McCloud's "parallel"; *Rosie's Walk*.
18. **The grin on the grim picture.** A cheerful face held over an image of harm. Precedent: Vault Boy's perk cards; *Starship Troopers*.
19. **The retouched group photo.** One figure removed and the background painted over, leaving too-smooth water or a gap in the line. Precedent: Yezhov at the canal, 1937.
20. **The erased helper.** Remove the support crew so the hero looks self-sufficient. Precedent: the attendant cut from Stalin's congress photo.
21. **The altered sign.** Change the text inside the picture. Precedent: Petrograd, 1917, "watches, gold and silver" repainted as a slogan.
22. **The quiet second edition.** Reissue with one panel changed; players who kept the first can compare. Precedent: the Great Soviet Encyclopedia's Beria pages, 1953–54; GE's comic reworked in 1955 and 1965.
23. **The defaced personal copy.** A found copy with a face inked out. Precedent: Rodchenko's own *Ten Years of Uzbekistan*.
24. **The too-perfect record of an ordinary recruit.** More professional photos and diary than any private could produce. Precedent: Lei Feng, 1963.
25. **The ghostwritten letters page.** "Letters" from the heroine or cadets that repeat each other word for word. Precedent: identical soldiers' letters in 11 newspapers, 2003 (Jowett & O'Donnell's grey propaganda).
26. **The innocent phrase that is really an instruction.** A recurring courtesy that carries more weight each time. Precedent: *BioShock*'s "would you kindly".
27. **The warning that describes the reader.** A "watch out for" box that fits the players' own characters. Precedent: *BioShock Infinite*'s False Shepherd posters.
28. **The backstage counter-document.** A scribble or margin note contradicting the official voice. Precedent: *Portal*'s hidden dens; *Lab Rat*.
29. **Production value as a tell.** In-house pages cruder than the glossy cover. Precedent: *Control*'s amateurish training films.
30. **The reverse of the clipping.** Incidental ads or notices on the back or in the margins carry the real news. Precedent: the HPLHS *Masks* clippings with real period backs.

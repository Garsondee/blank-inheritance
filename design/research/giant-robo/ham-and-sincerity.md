# Ham and Sincerity — the Register

*Part of the [Giant Robo research shelf](README.md). The series shouts, constantly, and never once winks. This document covers why that holds, what supports it, and how to get a table to that pitch without anybody feeling foolish. **This is not a decision document.** Claims are tagged **[S]** source, **[R]** reading, **[U]** suggested use.*

---

## 1. What the register actually is

**[S]** The series is one of the standard examples of *World of Ham* and *No Indoor Voice*. The final episode is described as consisting of nothing but speeches. Characters announce themselves by epithet, declare their intentions to each other in the middle of a charge, shout the names of techniques, and deliver full orations while dying. One sequence puts a line of brush-written Buddhist invective on screen as two heroes rush to their commander's aid, over an orchestral cue.

**[S]** It is also completely sincere. There is no framing device, no ironic narrator, no character who finds the others ridiculous. The retro-future setting, the epithets and the opera are all played straight.

## 2. The three supports

**[R]** Ham does not stand on its own. Three things hold it up here, and removing any one of them collapses it into camp.

1. **The music says it is serious.** A full orchestra and choir, the Dies Irae, Donizetti. The audience takes its cue from the score rather than from the premise. This is the support a table can least easily replicate — see §5.
2. **The stakes are real.** Major characters die and stay dead. Nothing undercuts a wink faster than a funeral. **[R] The most important single fact about this series' tone is that it earns its volume with consequences.**
3. **The ham is specific.** Nobody is generically loud. Alberto shouts about the dignity of his organisation, Tetsugyu shouts to be noticed, Chujo is silent for forty years and then is not. **Volume is characterisation, or it is noise.**

## 3. Comedy, and where the series puts it

**[S]** The main series carries real comedy — Tetsugyu's buffoonery and vanity, the drinking, a running joke about a man who cannot die and keeps getting up. The broadest material was pushed into three spin-off OVAs made partly to raise funds during production delays, and those go to genuine absurdity: heroes and villains drinking together, cross-dressing, a parody super robot.

**[R]** That split is worth noticing, because **our campaign's tone rule does not permit it.** The `feedback_tone_giant_robo_not_eva` memory is explicit that comedy is load-bearing and belongs in the main line, not quarantined in a special. The series is the model for warmth and theatricality; it is not the model for where the jokes go.

**[R]** What does transfer is the discovery that **the same character can be the comedy and the tragedy** — see [characters/tetsugyu.md](characters/tetsugyu.md). The laughs and the wound are the same material.

## 4. Getting a table to this pitch

**[U]** The obstacle is not willingness; it is that the first person to be sincere is exposed. Practical measures, in order of usefulness:

1. **The GM goes first, and does not hedge.** Deliver an enemy's declaration at full commitment in the first session. If the GM signals embarrassment, the table will match it.
2. **Never mock a player's line.** One raised eyebrow at the table's first grand statement ends the register permanently.
3. **Hand out lines worth saying.** An epithet, a named technique, a title. People perform when the words are already good. See [epithets-and-name-calling.md](epithets-and-name-calling.md).
4. **Declare before rolling.** Announcing the technique before the result is known makes the roll dramatic and the sincerity safe — it is a statement of intent, not a boast about an outcome.
5. **Keep one character quiet.** Contrast is what makes volume readable. The series' most frightening figure never speaks at all ([characters/ko-enshaku.md](characters/ko-enshaku.md)).
6. **Let the ordinary register be ordinary.** The series is not shouting during the mountain scene, the campfire, the conversation with a child. Ham is a peak, not a baseline, and it needs the quiet material to be a peak *of* something.

## 5. The thing a table cannot copy

**[R]** The orchestra. Reviewers consistently name the score as a primary reason the series works, and it does a job — telling the audience how to feel about a man who kills by snapping his fingers — that no GM voice can do alone.

**[U]** The nearest available substitutes are small and real: a fixed musical cue attached to one recurring event, used every time and never otherwise; a consistent phrase that opens a fight; a physical object placed on the table when a named enemy arrives. None of them is an orchestra. **Do not build a tone plan that assumes the music will carry it.**

## 6. Failure modes

**[R]**

- **The wink.** A single line of "yes, this is ridiculous" from the GM retires the register for the whole campaign.
- **Volume without content.** Shouting that is not about anything is exhausting within twenty minutes.
- **Performing at the players rather than to them.** A villain's declaration is an invitation; if the players cannot answer it, it is a monologue and it will be endured rather than enjoyed. See [the-recurring-antagonist-engine.md](the-recurring-antagonist-engine.md) §4.
- **Sustained pitch.** The series is, honestly, exhausting in stretches — the final episode really is nothing but speeches. A session needs the quiet beat. `design/pacing-and-the-responsive-campaign.md` §3 already names this as a standing risk here: tone drift is a *missing scheduled relax beat*, not an excess of drama.
- **Grimness mistaken for weight.** The register is loud and warm. `feedback_tone_giant_robo_not_eva` states the rule for this campaign: dark is allowed, depressing is a fault.

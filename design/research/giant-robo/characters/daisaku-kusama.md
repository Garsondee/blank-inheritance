# Daisaku Kusama (草間大作)

*Experts of Justice · the only ordinary person in a cast of monsters, and the moral centre because of it. Part of the [Giant Robo research shelf](../README.md). **Research, not lore — this character does not exist in our setting, and nothing here decides anything.** Tags: **[S]** source, **[R]** reading, **[U]** suggested use.*

## 1. What the series gives us **[S]**

- The son of the scientist who built the GR series. **Only his voice moves the robot**, which makes a twelve-year-old boy strategically indispensable to an international police organisation full of superhumans.
- He is physically unremarkable even by ordinary standards — though he can hold onto a robot in mid-combat, which the series notes approvingly.
- He inherited the job with his father's death: the machine, and a wristwatch that commands it.
- He is friends with adults, which the series treats as a feature rather than an oddity. The two who matter most are Ginrei, who is a protective older sister to him, and Tetsugyu, who resents him for most of the run.
- **Midway through, his own side takes the robot away from him**, after its nuclear power source is revealed. The decision is defensible and the delivery is graceless, and it isolates him for two episodes.
- His arc is stated plainly: he learns what really happened in the past, meets people, loses them, and stops being an innocent.
- In the final episode, **every living Expert — and one or two who were thought dead — spends themselves getting him back to his robot.** That sequence is the series' emotional climax, and it is about a boy running.

## 2. How he enters and leaves a scene **[S/R]**

**[S]** He is usually brought somewhere by an adult, and usually the adult is wrong about what he can handle.

**[R]** The series repeatedly stages him as cargo and then lets him refuse to be cargo. That is the shape of the whole character: the powerless member of an elite unit deciding, against orders, to be present.

## 3. Why he works **[R]**

- **He is the only person who can be hurt.** In a cast where a fatal wound is an inconvenience, the boy is where the tension lives. Every fight is legible because the audience knows exactly who is fragile.
- **His ordinariness is the moral position.** When he asks whether the cost was worth it, he is not asking from strength. The series' central question — can happiness be obtained without sacrifice — only has weight because it is asked by the person with the least power to enforce the answer.
- **He is defined by what he is given rather than what he is.** A watch, a machine, and a dead man's question. **[R]** That is a risk as well as a design: a protagonist who is only an inheritance is thin, and the series spends real effort — the refusals, the isolation, the mountain scene with the enemy — making him a person who *chooses* the inheritance rather than carrying it.
- **The adults orbit him and it is never patronising**, because they need him. He is not being protected out of sentiment; he is the mission.

## 4. The archetype **[U]**

**Name:** *The Indispensable Ordinary.* The weakest person in the unit, who cannot be replaced, and who therefore has to be in the dangerous place.

**The one rule:** **The powerful characters must need him, structurally, not sentimentally.** If the reason to protect him is that he is a child, he is a liability and the players will feel it. If the reason is that nothing works without him, he is the mission and every escort scene is a real scene.

**Voice:** direct, a bit too loud, asks the question everyone else has agreed not to ask. Not precocious. He does not understand the politics and says so.

**Audition lines** (written for this shelf, not quotations):

- *To an adult:* "You all keep saying it is complicated. Tell me the complicated part and I will decide if I understand it."
- *When benched:* "You are not keeping me safe. You are keeping me out of the way. Those are different, and you know which one this is."
- *To an enemy:* "I know what you lost. It does not make this all right. I am still sorry it happened."

## 5. For Blank Inheritance **[U]**

**Worth stealing:** the ordinary character who is structurally indispensable; the benching scene, which is a genuinely strong piece of drama and a terrible piece of GMing if imposed on a player without agreement; the finale where the whole supporting cast spends itself to get one person to one place.

**Worth avoiding:** **the benching, as written, at a table.** Taking a player's core capability away for two sessions is what the series does, and it works in a film because the audience cannot leave. `design/agency-and-player-choice.md` covers why an imposed removal of capability reads differently to a player than to a viewer. If this beat is wanted here, it has to be offered rather than applied.

**Where it might attach:** a Frame here amplifies its pilot's own sheet rather than replacing it (`data/schema/frame.schema.json`), so the framing carries over directly — **the kid is the reason the Frame is there.**

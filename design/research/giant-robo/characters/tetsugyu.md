# Tetsugyu of the Black Whirlwind (黒旋風の鉄牛)

*Experts of Justice · the loud brawler carrying a twenty-year-old crime. Part of the [Giant Robo research shelf](../README.md). **Research, not lore — this character does not exist in our setting, and nothing here decides anything.** Tags: **[S]** source, **[R]** reading, **[U]** suggested use.*

| | |
|---|---|
| **Japanese** | 黒旋風の鉄牛 — "Tetsugyu of the Black Whirlwind"; the name means "Iron Ox" |
| **Age / role** | 25. Field Expert; Taisou's sworn younger brother |
| **Borrowed from** | Li Kui 李逵, the *Water Margin*'s Black Whirlwind, via Yokoyama's adaptation — **Imagawa's condition for taking the job was that he be allowed to use Tetsugyu and Taisou** |
| **Powers** | Superhuman strength, and *ki* channelled through a pair of hand axes. Spinning the axes generates the whirlwind his name comes from |
| **Fate** | Crushed by an enemy machine, forgotten by the plot for an episode and a half, and alive in bandages at the end |

## 1. What the series gives us **[S]**

- Loud, obnoxious, drinks heavily, fights hard. The physically largest of the human heroes and, for most of the run, the comic relief.
- His first scene tells you everything: he destroys an entire flock of enemy aircraft with two axes, turns to be praised, and is called an idiot — because the elderly scientist they were rescuing was clinging to his back the whole time.
- **He is openly, unpleasantly hostile to the twelve-year-old protagonist.** He tells himself it is about competing for Ginrei's attention. It is not.
- **At twelve, he killed his own father, who was a Big Fire agent, with his own hands.** He has told nobody for over a decade. He recognises his own twelve-year-old face in the boy's expression at a moment of failure, and that recognition is what he has actually been running from.
- His resentment goes further than sulking: he helps the boy take the robot out against orders **specifically so that he can watch him fail** — and then is genuinely appalled when it works out as he intended.
- After losing his mentor, he changes: "from this point, I too will protect you, so that you do not become someone like me". He is crushed by falling debris immediately after saying it, and survives to keep the promise later.
- **[S]** The series has two competing accounts of why he helped the boy disobey orders, and they do not quite reconcile. It is an authentic continuity seam.

## 2. How he enters and leaves a scene **[S/R]**

**[S]** Loudly, first, usually before the plan has been agreed, and he leaves last, usually carrying someone.

**[R]** He is written as furniture for two episodes and then turned over. The trick is that **all the comedy beats are also evidence**: the drinking, the belligerence, the hostility to a child, the need to be praised. None of it is retconned; it is re-read.

## 3. Why he works **[R]**

- **The wham line is the best-constructed reveal in the series** — "when I was twelve, I had already killed a man" — because the audience has spent four episodes with a buffoon and is instantly obliged to re-examine every scene.
- **His cruelty is specific and has a cause.** He is not "a jerk"; he is a jerk *to one person*, and the reason turns out to be the worst thing that ever happened to him.
- **Redemption through succession.** He does not become a better person by deciding to. He becomes one when his own mentor dies and the job of protecting the boy is vacant.
- **[R] He is also, honestly, underpowered and inconsistent.** The series repeatedly tells the audience he is strong and then has him curb-stomped — although he does kill an apparently unkillable enemy with his bare hands once. The fan criticism is fair, and the cause is instructive: **in a cast where everyone is superhuman, "very strong" is not a niche.**

## 4. The archetype **[U]**

**Name:** *The Buffoon With The Buried Crime.* A loud, likeable, slightly embarrassing ally whose worst behaviour is a symptom of something he has never said out loud.

**The one rule:** **The comedy has to be honest before it is re-read.** Every laugh in the first half must still be funny after the reveal, and must also, in hindsight, have been evidence. If the comedy is disposable, the reveal is a swap rather than a turn.

**Voice:** loud, blunt, over-familiar, fishing for praise. Cheerfully rude to the powerful, cutting to the one person he cannot stand to look at. Goes completely quiet when the subject gets close.

**Audition lines** (written for this shelf, not quotations):

- *To an equal:* "Did you see it? You did not see it. Nobody ever sees the good ones."
- *To the child he resents:* "Everybody keeps telling me you are the important one. Fine. Go on being important over there."
- *At the turn:* "You want to know why I cannot look at you. All right. Sit down. It is not a good story and I am going to tell it badly."
- *After:* "I am not apologising. I am going to be useful instead. That is what you are getting."

## 5. For Blank Inheritance **[U]**

**Worth stealing:** the comic ally whose jokes are evidence; hostility to one specific PC with a real cause; the redemption that arrives as a vacancy rather than a decision; the establishing scene that undercuts the character in the same breath as it shows off.

**Worth avoiding:** the niche problem. If an ally's defining trait is raw strength in a setting full of stronger things, he will be sidelined. Give this archetype a job the party genuinely cannot do — carrying, holding, breaking one specific thing — so his competence has somewhere to live.

**Where it might attach:** the campaign's tone rules make comedy load-bearing (`feedback_tone_giant_robo_not_eva`), and this is the clearest worked example in the source of **comedy that pays a debt later**. Any recurring comic NPC written for our table would benefit from being built this way from the start — which is a writing method, not a casting proposal.

# What Makes Giant Robo Tick

*Part of the [Giant Robo research shelf](README.md). Eleven load-bearing decisions behind the OVA, what each one buys, and whether it survives being moved to a table. **This is not a decision document.** Nothing here overrides the story bible or any open question; it is grounding for writing, not a ruling on what our campaign does. Claims are tagged **[S]** source, **[R]** reading, **[U]** suggested use.*

---

## 1. Everyone is superhuman except the hero

**[S]** Daisaku Kusama is twelve years old and has no powers at all. Every other named character can fly, teleport, project shockwaves, control insects, or punch with the force of a nuclear device. Even the anonymous IPO staff roof-hop onto a zeppelin to evacuate it and form a human pyramid to block an energy blast. Daisaku's only asset is that the Giant Robo answers to his voice print and nobody else's.

**[R]** This is the inversion that makes the series work, and it is worth stating plainly because it is the opposite of the usual arrangement. Normally the hero is the exception and the world is ordinary. Here the world is the exception and the hero is ordinary, which makes his ordinariness the scarce and interesting thing in the room. He is the only person present who can be hurt by a rifle, the only one who has to be protected, and therefore the only one whose survival generates tension. It also means his moral position is the one the audience can occupy: when he refuses an order or asks whether the cost was worth it, he is not arguing from strength.

**[U]** This is structurally close to what our campaign already has — level-1 kids standing next to level-12 Frames — which is the hardest problem `design/scale-and-chassis.md` exists to solve. The reading to take from Giant Robo is not mechanical. It is that **the ordinary character must be allowed to be the moral centre because they are weak, not in spite of it**, and that the powerful characters should visibly arrange themselves around protecting them. Do not let the strong characters treat the weak one as luggage; let them treat him as the reason.

## 2. The giant robot is not the protagonist and barely fights

**[S]** For a mecha series, there are very few mecha battles. The human characters do the fighting. Giant Robo is described in production material as a weapon of mass destruction rather than a robot superhero: slow, lumbering, armed with enough ordnance to level cities, and powered by a nuclear reactor rather than the world's clean Shizuma Drive.

**[R]** Scarcity is the whole mechanism. Because Robo is not on screen constantly, every launch is an event, and because Robo is a weapon rather than a friend with fists, deploying it is a decision with a cost rather than a fight scene. The series gets more out of five Robo sequences than a conventional show gets out of fifty.

**[U]** Relevant to Frame deployment. If a Frame is on the field every session, its arrival stops meaning anything. Worth reading alongside `design/science-fiction-and-mecha-conventions.md` on the sortie ritual.

## 3. Retro-future is a fixed register, never a joke

**[S]** The world has nearly infinite clean energy, transforming cars and carrier aircraft the size of cruise ships, and everyone wears 1950s suits and hats. Manual labour is still ordinary. Nothing more advanced than a personal computer appears. Character designers were told to emulate Mitsuteru Yokoyama's 1960s drawing style rather than invent their own, and the vehicles were designed as though conceived at the turn of the twentieth century. The production called the effect "timeless" rather than "past".

**[R]** The register is held absolutely straight. Nobody in the series finds the hats funny. The retro surface is not nostalgia-as-punchline; it is the ordinary texture of the world, which is what lets the fantastic elements land on top of it.

**[U]** This is the single closest match between the source and our own locked visual identity (`design/visual-identity.md`, the `project_visual_identity_directive` memory). Use it as the calibration reference: **the era is the room, not the gag.**

## 4. Wuxia grammar under a super-robot skin

**[S]** Most of the Experts come from Yokoyama's adaptations of *Outlaws of the Marsh* (Water Margin) and *Romance of the Three Kingdoms*. The production explicitly followed wuxia convention: descriptive nicknames earned from a weapon, an appearance or a temperament ("Shockwave Alberto", "Silent Chujo", "Kenji the Immortal"); secret societies plotting against the order of the world; powerful clans at war; honour, loyalty and individual justice as the governing values. The Alberto/Taisou rivalry is named in the production notes as the clearest expression of it.

**[R]** This is why the villains behave the way they do. In a *jiang hu* story, enemies are not vermin to be exterminated — they are peers in a shared world with a shared code, which is why they can respect each other, spare each other, refuse a dishonourable order, and mourn each other. The genre supplies the honour; the honour supplies the recurring villain.

**[U]** See [legendary-sources-china-and-japan.md](legendary-sources-china-and-japan.md) for the specific figures and [the-recurring-antagonist-engine.md](the-recurring-antagonist-engine.md) for what the code buys mechanically.

## 5. The orchestra is a cast member

**[S]** Masamichi Amano composed, arranged and conducted the entire score, performed by the Warsaw Philharmonic Orchestra and Choir. It uses leitmotifs for characters and events. It quotes the Dies Irae — first heard as the Champs-Élysées is destroyed — and "Una furtiva lagrima" from Donizetti's *L'elisir d'amore*, which attaches to the Bashtarle flashbacks. Imagawa said the aria embodies one of the series' themes: the sorrow of others not understanding your true feelings. Reviewers routinely name the score as a primary reason the series works.

**[R]** The music is doing an enormous amount of the tonal labour. It is what keeps a man who snaps his fingers to bisect people from reading as camp. Opera scoring tells the audience that the story takes itself seriously, and the audience follows the music's lead rather than the premise's.

**[U]** At a table, the equivalent lever is much smaller but real: a fixed, serious musical cue for a specific recurring event does the same signalling work. This is a production note, not a design decision.

## 6. Ham with no wink

**[S]** The series is one of TV Tropes' standard examples of *World of Ham* and *No Indoor Voice*; the final episode is described as consisting of nothing but speeches. Characters announce themselves, declare their intentions to each other mid-charge, and shout technique names. In one sequence the phrase "no mercy of the Buddha for the wicked" appears on screen in brush-written kanji while two heroes rush to a colleague's aid.

**[R]** Sincerity is the load-bearing choice, not volume. The series never signals that it knows it is silly. A wink would cost it everything, because the emotional payoffs — a dying mentor, a son learning he misread his father — depend on the audience having agreed to take the surface seriously.

**[U]** See [ham-and-sincerity.md](ham-and-sincerity.md) for how to get a table to this pitch. This is directly downstream of the `feedback_tone_giant_robo_not_eva` memory.

## 7. Honour on both sides, distributed unevenly

**[S]** Most of the Magnificent Ten behave honourably. Hanzui, their leader, is never shown doing anything evil and is kinder than several of the heroes. Alberto and Taisou treat each other as worthy opponents; when Taisou dies of wounds mid-fight, Alberto shouts at him not to die. The Ten collectively object to the plan at the heart of the series on the grounds that destroying the world they intend to rule is self-defeating and mad. Two members — Mask the Red and Doki — are exempt from all of this, and Mask the Red murders a wounded colleague.

**[R]** Two things are being bought at once. Honour across the whole cabal makes the enemy a society rather than a monster generator, which is what lets them recur without the audience demanding their extermination. Then the two exceptions matter enormously: because honour is the baseline, a dishonourable villain reads as genuinely frightening rather than as more of the same.

**[U]** If a villain faction is built on a code, spend one member's budget on someone who breaks it. That is where the horror goes.

## 8. Anyone can die, and the deaths are structural

**[S]** The series kills Taisou, Youshi, Professor Shizuma, Alberto, Ginrei and Genya. Several are principal cast. Two characters return from apparent death (Tetsugyu, Alberto) and the returns are staged as payoffs rather than reversals — Alberto has been clinging to Giant Robo unseen for several episodes.

**[R]** Each death does a job. Taisou's buys the escape of everyone else and triggers Tetsugyu's growth and Alberto's grief. Ginrei's completes a family tragedy and hands the antagonist his breakdown. None of them is a shock for its own sake. **[R]** The series is careful about which deaths it makes permanent: the ones that change a living character are permanent, and the ones that would only cost the audience a favourite are the ones that get reversed.

**[U]** A useful test before killing an NPC: name the living character the death changes. If there is not one, the death is decoration.

## 9. The truth arrives in four passes

**[S]** The Tragedy of Bashtarle is explained repeatedly and differently across the series. What it was, who caused it, why the survivors react as they do, and finally what the dead man was actually trying to do — each pass rewrites the previous one without contradicting it.

**[U]** Handled in full in [the-buried-atrocity.md](the-buried-atrocity.md). It is the most directly reusable structure in the series for our own buried-history material.

## 10. Fathers, children, and one question asked in episode one

**[S]** Dr. Kusama's dying question to his son — can happiness be obtained without sacrifice, can a new era be achieved without tragedy — is the spine of the whole series. Daisaku and Genya are explicit mirrors: both lost their fathers young, both inherited a legacy they did not fully understand, both fight to carry out a dead man's wish. The closing credits dedicate the series to fathers and their children.

**[R]** The question is load-bearing because it is never answered rhetorically — it is answered by what the characters do, and the ending is bittersweet rather than triumphant because the honest answer was "no, and we did it anyway".

**[U]** A campaign-level question of this kind is worth having: a single sentence the whole story is asking, which the table answers by playing rather than by debating. Whether ours has one is a matter for `design/campaign-arc.md` and the user, not for this shelf.

## 11. The real villain is poor communication

**[S]** Dr. von Vogler tried to warn the world that the Shizuma Drive was producing a poison that would eventually kill everything, and tried to fix it. He died before explaining, leaving his son a rigged attaché case and an instruction — "stop Shizuma" — with none of the reasoning. Genya spent the rest of his life killing people to carry out a wish he had misread. The heroes make a smaller version of the same mistake: the IPO seals Giant Robo away for legitimate reasons and delivers the decision so badly that it isolates Daisaku for two episodes.

**[R]** This is why the series is a tragedy rather than a fight. Nobody in the central conflict is actually wrong about what they want; they are wrong about what someone else meant. It is also a very forgiving engine, because it generates antagonists with real grievances who can be argued with.

**[U]** This sits extremely close to the v4 bible's own thesis that nobody in our story has ever done anything evil, only flinched and then built a life on the flinch. The two engines are compatible and should be checked against each other before either is extended.

## 12. Portable, and not portable

**Portable to a table, directly:** the ordinary protagonist among titans (§1); scarce mecha deployment (§2); the era held straight (§3); descriptive epithets and the honour code (§4, and [epithets-and-name-calling.md](epithets-and-name-calling.md)); sincerity over irony (§6); one dishonourable villain in an honourable faction (§7); the death test (§8); the four-pass reveal (§9); misunderstanding as the engine (§11).

**Not portable, or portable only with care:** the score (§5) does work at a table that a playlist cannot replicate, so do not rely on it to carry tone. The body count (§8) works in a finished seven-part story with an authored cast; in a campaign, permanent NPC death has to be earned against the table's attachment rather than scheduled. The series' pacing is uneven in ways its reputation politely ignores — an episode-long stretch where the plot is held up by a strategist refusing to explain himself would be intolerable at a table, where players would simply interrogate him.

**One caution.** The series' reputation is built on an ending the production never actually reached: the OVA was designed as the second-to-last chapter of a seven-arc story, and the rest was never made. Its shape is therefore not a model for a finished campaign. See [series-shape-and-unmade-arcs.md](series-shape-and-unmade-arcs.md).

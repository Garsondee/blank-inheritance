# Series Shape, Production, and the Six Unmade Arcs

*Part of the [Giant Robo research shelf](README.md). What the series' actual shape was, what it was meant to be, and what its titles and its failures teach about scoping a campaign. **This is not a decision document.** Claims are tagged **[S]** source, **[R]** reading, **[U]** suggested use.*

---

## 1. The production, briefly

**[S]** Seven episodes, each 40 to 60 minutes, released between 22 July 1992 and 25 January 1998. Intended to take three years; took six. The first four kept roughly to a six-month schedule, then the gaps widened, and there were **three years between episodes six and seven** while the budget recovered and the staff worked on other productions (*The Big O*, *Getter Robo Armageddon*, *Super Atragon*).

**[S]** The first episode's screenwriter, Eiichi Matsuyama, **died during production**; the credited writer name in the finished series is a composite of his name and his successors'.

**[S]** Three comedic *Ginrei* spin-off OVAs were made in the gaps, partly to keep interest alive and partly to raise money. They exceeded their budgets and made little.

**[S]** The series was well received abroad, sold poorly at home against its costs, and is now routinely listed among the medium's classics.

## 2. The seven episodes **[S]**

| # | Title | Length |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | The Black Attaché Case | 56 min |
| 2 | The Tragedy of Bashtarle | 44 min |
| 3 | The Magnetic Web Strategy | 39 min |
| 4 | The Twilight of the Super Heroes | 46 min |
| 5 | The Truth of Bashtarle | 46 min |
| 6 | Crime and Punishment | 49 min |
| 7 | The Grand Finale | 60 min |

**[R]** Note what the titles do. Two of them name the same event at two different depths — *The Tragedy of* and then *The Truth of* — which tells you the reveal structure before you have watched anything. One names an object. One names a plan. One names a condition of the cast. None of them is a mood.

## 3. The six arcs that were never made **[S]**

*The Day the Earth Stood Still* was written as the **second-to-last** chapter of a seven-part story. The others were titled and never produced:

1. **The Birth of Zangetsu the Midday**
2. **The Plan to Assassinate Daisaku — the Canary Penitentiary**
3. **The Boy of Three Days**
4. **The Greatest Battle in History — Marshal Kanshin versus Shokatsu Koumei**
5. **The Boy Detective, Kindaichi Shotaro, Appears!**
6. **The Siege of Babel** — the finale

**[R] These titles are a masterclass, and they are all doing the same thing: naming a specific person or a specific event, and promising something concrete.** Two name a matchup. One names a place of confinement. One names a duration. One announces an arrival with an exclamation mark. Not one of them describes an atmosphere.

**[U] The rule to take:** a chapter title should name the person, the place or the confrontation, not the feeling. "The Greatest Battle in History — X versus Y" tells a table exactly what they have been promised, which means the campaign can be held to it.

## 4. Starting at chapter six **[R]**

**[S]** Because the produced series is the penultimate arc, it opens on a cast that already has a decade of history: rivalries that began before the first frame, a dead member of the enemy council, a past operation everyone refers to and nobody explains, and an atrocity ten years cold.

**[R]** This is a deliberate technique and it works. The audience is dropped into a world in progress and trusts it, because the characters are not explaining themselves to each other.

**[U] For a campaign this is available and underused.** Begin with a history the party's own NPCs already share: one past operation everyone names and nobody details, one death that is still being argued about, one grudge that predates session one. The cost is that a table will ask, and the GM must have the answers written down — which is the difference between an implied history and a vague one.

## 5. What it cost, and the lesson **[R]**

The series never finished. Its reputation rests on an arc that was designed to be followed by a finale that does not exist. The consequences are visible throughout this shelf:

- **Zangetsu** is a fan favourite built entirely on a promise; his own arc was the first of the unmade six ([characters/zangetsu.md](characters/zangetsu.md)).
- **Big Fire** never wakes into a story ([characters/big-fire.md](characters/big-fire.md)).
- **Seven of the Big Nine** never appear ([characters/the-big-nine.md](characters/the-big-nine.md)).
- A **child on the enemy's side** was meant to become a friend of the protagonist, and the arc was compressed away ([characters/sunny-the-magician.md](characters/sunny-the-magician.md)).

**[U] The rule this shelf keeps repeating, stated once properly:** every mystery introduced is a debt. A campaign, unlike a cancelled OVA, will actually end — so write the answer down when the question is invented, and schedule the payoff in the arc plan. `design/objectives.md`'s accumulating log and `design/npc-social-intel.md`'s Facts-with-triggers are the project's existing machinery for exactly this, and a promise that is not in one of them is not tracked.

## 6. Two more transferable production facts **[U]**

- **The side story made during a gap.** The *Ginrei* specials exist because the main line stalled. A short, lighter, single-character session between arcs is a real tool for a campaign that loses momentum — with the caveat from [ham-and-sincerity.md](ham-and-sincerity.md) §3 that our tone rules put comedy in the main line rather than in a quarantine.
- **Scope honestly.** A production intending three years took six and did not finish. **[R]** The relevant comparison for us is the eight-chapter arc already sketched in `design/campaign-arc.md`: the question worth asking of any plan is not whether it is good but whether it can be finished at the rate the table actually plays.

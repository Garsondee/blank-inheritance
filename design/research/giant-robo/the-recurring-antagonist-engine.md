# The Recurring Antagonist Engine

*Part of the [Giant Robo research shelf](README.md). Written to answer a specific brief: **recurring villains who announce themselves, taunt the players, fight, and then leave using powers the party cannot yet counter — without the table ever feeling cheated.** This document covers what the OVA actually does, why it holds together, the four escape mechanisms it uses, and the table-level rules that separate a great recurring villain from a rigged fight. **This is not a decision document.** Every mechanical sketch in §7 is an option, not a ruling; none of it resolves anything in `data/campaign/open-questions.json`. Claims are tagged **[S]** source, **[R]** reading, **[U]** suggested use.*

---

## 1. Calibration: what the source actually does

Worth being exact, because the brief and the source overlap but are not identical.

**[S] What Giant Robo genuinely does.** Characters are addressed and introduced by epithet, constantly, and the epithet is the name: nobody says "Alberto", they say "Shockwave Alberto" (衝撃のアルベルト). Techniques are named and announced — the Big Bang Punch, the soul-chasing life-taking sword (追魂奪命剣), Issei's chanted incantation. Combatants declare their intentions to each other mid-charge, and in one sequence a declaration appears on screen in brush-written kanji while the music swells. Villains assert rank aloud in the middle of a fight: Alberto's last stand opens with him shouting at an enemy not to underestimate the Magnificent Ten. Grand entrances are standard — Alberto walks into the IPO's Beijing base alone and destroys the garrison; Doki's Blood Wind League rises out of a river.

**[S] What it does not do.** There is no gag where a villain shouts a name and a title card freezes. The taunting is not schoolyard; it is closer to a duellist's formality, with contempt underneath. And the series' villains do not escape *because* they are villains — they escape because the setting is full of people who can teleport, fly, walk out of shadows and survive a building collapsing on them, on both sides.

**[R]** That last point is the important one. **The escaping villain is not an exception to the world's rules; it is an ordinary consequence of them.** The heroes have the same class of ability and use it the same way. That is what stops the escapes reading as GM protection, and it is the first thing to copy.

## 2. The four escape mechanisms, and what each costs

**[S] a. The Implacable One — Ko-Enshaku.** A masked, armoured swordsman who never speaks, steps out of people's shadows, flies away, and survives everything: a building demolition, a suicide bombing at point-blank range by a named hero, being killed with bare hands. He reappears the next episode unmarked. Crucially, supplementary material states his weakness plainly: **the eyes.**

**[R]** The stated weakness is what makes him bearable. He is not "the GM says no"; he is a locked door with a keyhole the players have not found yet. The audience can hold the idea that this is solvable.

**[S] b. The Faked Death — Alberto.** Betrayed and apparently killed by his own side several episodes before the end, Alberto is revealed to have survived and spent the intervening time clinging to Giant Robo, unseen, waiting. He returns to settle a grudge, not to continue the plot.

**[R]** A survival the audience did not see is only satisfying when the reason for the silence is characterful. Alberto lay low because being murdered by an upstart was an insult he intended to repay in person. The return is a payoff for his personality, not a reversal of a death.

**[S] c. The Rota — the Magnificent Ten.** There are ten of them, each with a different power and a different temperament, and they take turns. Losing one does not end the threat; it changes which threat arrives next. The cabal is explicitly a leadership council, so the loss of a member is also an internal political event.

**[R]** This is the cheapest and most reliable of the four. The party gets to win permanently against a named individual, and the campaign threat is untouched. **A faction of named peers is a renewable antagonist that never needs anybody to survive anything implausible.**

**[S] d. The Staged Revival — Jujoji.** His stated ability is control over life, including his own: he recovers from death in stages. Two of the Ten appear convincingly dead in one episode and are fine in the next.

**[R]** This is the weakest of the four, and the series gets criticised for it. It works only when the mechanism is declared in advance as part of the character's power, which is the case for Jujoji and is not the case for the others.

**[S] e. The hero version — Kenji Murasame.** The Japanese material is precise about him in a way the English is not: he is not a man who *does not die* but a man who **cannot** die (「死なない」のではなく「死ねない」男), which is why he treats his own life carelessly, and why the one person who mattered to him was the one who understood that this was a burden.

**[R]** The same immortality given to a hero and framed as a cost becomes a tragedy rather than a cheat. **Whether an unkillable character is annoying or beloved is decided entirely by whether the story treats the immortality as a gift or a sentence.**

## 3. The Escape Clause — five rules

**[U]** These are the rules that keep a recurring escape from becoming a rigged fight. They are written as instructions.

1. **Disclose the mechanism the first time it is used, in the fiction.** The party watches him step into a shadow and vanish. They do not discover three sessions later that he had a get-out. `design/agency-and-player-choice.md` is blunt about why: an undisclosed authorial override is the one solution to the narrative paradox never to use. A disclosed, diegetic, in-world escape is a puzzle. A hidden one is cheating.
2. **State a condition under which the escape fails — to yourself, in writing, before the first appearance.** Ko-Enshaku's eyes. Fitzcarraldo, for all his power, ends up fused into a rock because someone teleported the whole building. The condition does not have to be discoverable yet. It has to exist, because that is the difference between a villain you are withholding and a villain you have not designed.
3. **Every appearance costs the villain something visible.** An eye, a lieutenant, a base, a secret, a piece of equipment, a working relationship inside their own faction. If the party fights the same villain four times and the villain is in exactly the same condition each time, the party is not in a story, they are in a loop.
4. **Every appearance gives the party something.** A name, a fact about the mechanism, an overheard line, a dropped object. Recurrence has to be how the players *learn* the counter, not merely the delivery system for a beating.
5. **Never let the escape undo damage the players actually did.** If they landed a hit, the hit happened; he leaves hurt. The escape removes the enemy from the field, not the consequences from the record.

**[R]** Rules 3 and 4 together are what convert repetition into progress. Without them, a recurring villain is a treadmill; with them, each fight is an instalment.

## 4. Arrival: announce, assert, act

**[S]** The pattern in the source is consistent. The villain arrives alone, into a place that ought to be safe. He announces himself, usually by epithet and by organisation rather than by intention. He demonstrates, immediately and disproportionately. Only then is there a conversation, and the conversation is conducted as though the outcome is already settled.

**[U] The three-beat entrance, for use at a table:**

- **Announce** — one line, containing the epithet and the affiliation, delivered to the room rather than to a person. "Shockwave Alberto. Big Fire. Nobody needs to be brave today."
- **Assert** — a single unanswerable demonstration that establishes the gap. Not a round of combat: one effect, narrated, that the party could not have stopped. A wall, a gate, a vehicle, a squad of NPC guards.
- **Act** — state the transaction. What he came for, what he will trade, what happens if refused.

**[U]** Then, and this matters: **let the players respond before the fight starts.** The theatrical entrance is a gift to them, not a cutscene at them. It exists to give them the information needed to decide whether to fight, stall, follow, or hand something over.

## 5. Calling the name, and the named technique

**[S]** Two separate devices, often confused.

- **The epithet as name.** Every significant character carries a descriptive title used as their name: Shockwave Alberto, Silent Chujo, Kenji the Immortal, Hanzui the Demon King of Chaos, Zangetsu the Midday, Yuki of the Gathering Dusk. The convention is borrowed from wuxia via Yokoyama's *Water Margin* adaptation, where every one of the 108 heroes has one. See [epithets-and-name-calling.md](epithets-and-name-calling.md).
- **The named technique.** A specific manoeuvre with a proper name, spoken at the moment of use. Chujo's Big Bang Punch. Kaei's soul-chasing life-taking sword, which turns one arrow into a rain of arrows. Fitzcarraldo's vacuum-break, delivered with a finger snap.

**[U] Rules for both at a table.** Give each recurring villain exactly one named technique, not a repertoire — the name is a signature, and signatures do not come in sets of five. Say the name at the moment of the roll, before the result is known, because saying it after the damage is narration and saying it before is a threat. Keep the name in the register of the setting: Giant Robo's names are plain compound nouns with weight, not sentences.

## 6. Thumbing the nose — what a good taunt does

**[R]** A taunt that only expresses contempt is the GM talking. A taunt is working when it does at least two of these four jobs:

1. **Reveals the villain's actual opinion of the world**, which is character rather than noise. Alberto is disappointed that the rank-and-file cannot fight him properly, and openly respects the one man who can — the contempt has a shape, and the shape is a code.
2. **Invites a specific response**, so the players have something to push against rather than an insult to absorb.
3. **Sets a price** — names something the villain wants, or something they will do next, which the party can now try to prevent.
4. **Costs the villain something later.** Contempt in episode two is what makes a defeat in episode six taste of anything.

**[U]** The best taunt in the source is Alberto's disappointment, because it is generous and insulting at the same time: he wants a better opponent, and saying so out loud tells the party exactly what would make him take them seriously. That is a taunt the players can answer by playing.

## 7. Mechanical sketches — options, not decisions

**[U]** All of the following are options for discussion. `design/design-layers.md` is the governing methodology: an encounter is authored as a defensible pure-SF2E encounter first, and only then does anything campaign-specific change what a combatant may do inside it. `design/ux-doctrine.md` §11 also applies — the shipped `gamesystems/sf2e/` already implements conditions, degrees of success, flanking and initiative, so none of that gets rebuilt here.

- **Withdrawal threshold, declared.** The villain leaves at a stated damage threshold or on a stated trigger, and the table is told the trigger exists the first time it fires. The fight is then genuinely winnable — the win condition is "drive them off", which the party achieves — rather than secretly unwinnable.
- **The escape as a real action.** The villain spends an action to leave, and it is interruptable by a stated counter (a readied action, a specific condition, a grapple). This is the version that respects the dice most, because the escape can fail.
- **The ledger.** Track, visibly, what each encounter with a recurring villain costs them and what it teaches the party. Shown to players, this is the single strongest defence against the treadmill feeling. Whether that belongs on the Objectives tracker (`design/objectives.md`) or the Character Card (`design/npc-social-intel.md`) is a UI question that is not settled here.
- **Never fudge.** A retreat written into the stat block is honest. A retreat improvised because the dice went badly for the villain is the thing the players will eventually notice and never forgive.

**[U] On the Character Card.** `design/npc-social-intel.md` already splits an NPC into permanent **Facts** (hidden until a reveal trigger fires, then known everywhere) and a live **Attitude** on SF2E's real five-step scale. A recurring villain is the ideal case for that structure: the escape mechanism is a Fact, the stated weakness is a Fact with a later trigger, and the taunt is what moves the Attitude. No new system is needed to run any of this.

## 8. Failure modes

**[R]** Each of these is visible somewhere in the source or its reception.

- **The villain who costs nothing.** The series takes real criticism for exactly this: two of the Ten appear dead and are simply fine later, with no explanation. Reception treats it as a flaw, not a feature.
- **The strategist who will not explain.** Koumei insists everything is going to plan and refuses to elaborate, at length, while hundreds of his own people die. In a finished film this is an unsettling mystery. **At a table it is unplayable**, because players will simply keep asking, and a GM answering "he won't say" four times has stopped running a scene.
- **The unhittable villain.** Ko-Enshaku works because he is terrifying and rare. If every enemy is implacable, the party stops trying, and a party that has stopped trying is no longer playing.
- **The escape that insults the roll.** Covered in §3, rule 5, because it is the one that does permanent damage to trust.
- **Recurrence without variation.** The Ten solve this by being ten different people. A single villain who arrives four times must arrive differently each time: with subordinates, with a hostage, with an offer, without violence.

## 9. Build checklist

**[U]** Before a recurring villain goes on the table:

1. Epithet, in the house register. One named technique.
2. One want, stated in a sentence, legible to the players within one encounter. (`design/quests-objectives-and-threads.md` — depth is free, wants are not.)
3. The escape mechanism, and the date it is first shown.
4. The condition under which the escape fails, written down now.
5. What appearance one costs them, and what it teaches the party.
6. What appearance two costs them, and what it teaches the party. If this is blank, they should only appear once.
7. The defeat that is available at the end, and what it takes.
8. One line they would say to a child, and one line they would say to an equal. That is the voice.

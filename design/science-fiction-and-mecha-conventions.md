# Science Fiction and Mecha Conventions

*Written 2026-09-13. One of eleven new reference documents on the fantasy and science-fiction writing-craft shelf, alongside the five narrative-craft documents written the same day ([narrative-theory-foundations.md](narrative-theory-foundations.md), [agency-and-player-choice.md](agency-and-player-choice.md), [ttrpg-campaign-and-session-craft.md](ttrpg-campaign-and-session-craft.md), [quests-objectives-and-threads.md](quests-objectives-and-threads.md), [pacing-and-the-responsive-campaign.md](pacing-and-the-responsive-campaign.md)). Two halves: science fiction as a literary mode (§1–§7), and the mecha genre's own grammar (§8–§15), which is separate craft with its own rules about scale, sortie, pilots, enemies and children. The second half gets equal weight because this campaign's register is a mecha register, not a general-SF one. Nothing here is a decision. It does not override `ideas/story/blank-inheritance-v4.md`, [campaign-arc.md](campaign-arc.md), or any entry in `data/campaign/open-questions.json`. Where the campaign already does the right thing this says so by name and file; where there is a gap it names the gap and stops.*

Claims are tagged: **[E]** empirical, **[T]** theory (a scholarly framework — vocabulary, not proof), **[C]** craft (practitioner consensus from shipped work, often righter than theory and never measured), **[Q]** contested. Nothing here is tagged [E], because none of it has been measured.

## 1. The novum, and cognitive estrangement

Darko Suvin's definition, from *Metamorphoses of Science Fiction* (1979): science fiction is the literature of **cognitive estrangement**, organised around a **novum** — a validated novelty whose consequences are worked out with enough rigour that the reader can reason about them. The estrangement half descends from Brecht and Shklovsky; the cognitive half is Suvin's insistence that the novelty be arguable rather than asserted. **[T]** It is contested and should be quoted as contested: prescriptive rather than descriptive, it excludes most space opera and nearly all media SF. **[T/Q]** Use "novum" as a working noun; do not use Suvin as a gate.

This campaign has **one novum and everything descends from it**. `ideas/story/dossier-first-impact.md` (rewritten 2026-09-24) derives it from one accident: a container of exotic fuel splits on a buried mitflit starship's cargo deck at Kortos, the rupture tears the ship's navigation and sensor system into the Watcher, and the founders' reaction becomes the Directorate.

**Before adding any new impossible thing, trace it back to the buried ships. If it does not descend from that accident it is a second novum, and a second novum costs far more than it looks.**

Named without resolving: magic is already a second novum. v4 §9 explains why the world looks like this — magic draining from Golarion, industry growing into the vacuum — but not how the two relate.

## 2. Extrapolation, speculation, and the one-big-lie discipline

**Extrapolation** runs something real forward. **Speculation** grants a premise that is not real and reasons from it. Most working SF speculates once and extrapolates everything downstream. The practitioner discipline is the **one big lie**: an audience accepts one impossibility without argument, then holds you to every consequence of it. **[C]** The cost of three or four unrelated lies is not implausibility — audiences are generous — it is that the ground rules stop being learnable, so no solution feels earned, because nobody could have predicted it and nobody can check it afterwards.

v4 §8 grants Sap one property — it is food; density is the effect, and visibility is what density looks like — then derives four consequences in a numbered list. `ideas/story/dossier-observer.md` §10 does the harder backwards version: four anomalies (no approach vector, immunity until the interdiction field breaks, small Claimants all falling the same direction, sizes from insect to behemoth) all explained by the one condensation mechanic, with nothing new introduced.

**When a new mystery needs an explanation, derive it from something already established rather than introducing a new substance, force, or faction. If the honest answer needs a new one, the mystery is mis-scoped — that is not a licence to add.**

## 3. The hard/soft spectrum, without the value judgement

The usual framing treats hard SF as rigorous and soft SF as lax, which is a value judgement smuggled into a taxonomy. **[C/Q]** The honest version is two independent kinds of rigour: **rigour about mechanism** (the physics is checkable) and **rigour about consequence** (given the premise, institutions, economies and people behave as they would actually behave). A work can be entirely soft about mechanism and extremely hard about implication.

This campaign is exactly that. The mechanisms are unexamined and should stay so — nobody needs to know how a nanoparticle cloud retains function. The consequences are tracked severely. **Mass is conserved**: every use of Sap permanently grows the Watcher, and the extra mass disperses back into the cloud and stays (v4 §8). **Nothing is ever killed**: Claimants are condensations, every victory is a dispersal, the war is unwinnable by attrition (v4 §4, §7).

**Consequence rigour is the kind this campaign has committed to, and the kind that can break at the table. When a player asks a mechanism question you have not decided, answer with a consequence you have — and never improvise a number the Mass Clock or the dispersal rule will later have to honour.**

## 4. Invented terminology: sparse, consistent, load-bearing

The test: **if a term could be replaced with an ordinary word without loss, it is decoration.** Decoration is not free — every invented word is a lookup, and a page with six of them reads as work rather than as a world. The second rule matters more at a table: **name the effect, not the mechanism.** A name encoding a correct mechanism was written by an author; a name encoding a plausible wrong guess was written by the people in the fiction. **[C]**

This campaign is strong on both. **The Interdiction Field** is its best term precisely because it is wrong — the Directorate's observational name for what `dossier-observer.md` §10 calls the manifestation envelope, so the gap between name and reality is itself a clue. **Claimants are announced by classification code, never named** (`data/antagonists/claimants/claimant-guidelines.json`) — an institution's voice doing escalation work for free, because a number is a series.

**Every invented term here should be a word an institution would actually have coined, and should ideally be wrong in a characterful way.**

Open, and the user's call: `data/campaign/open-questions.json` (which absorbed the naming placeholders on 2026-09-19) still carries the Watcher, the Gap and both excavation placeholders as open names. The city (Kurose) and the Directorate are settled.

## 5. The competence-and-wonder problem

SF audiences reward characters who understand their world. Explain too much and wonder collapses into a manual; explain too little and stakes collapse, because an audience that cannot assess a threat cannot fear it. The reliable resolution is to split the two: **make the cast fluent about the ordinary and baffled by the anomaly.** Competence spent on requisition procedure and which harbour gate floods at spring tide buys credibility at no cost to the mystery. **[C]**

The machinery already exists. [encyclopaedia-checks.md](encyclopaedia-checks.md) makes insight checks a standing pattern — always offered, grounded in real SF2E skills, with the rule that **failure never costs anything mechanical**, which lets a GM hand out world-fluency constantly without making ignorance a punishment. On the wonder side, `dossier-observer.md` §15's treated lens is the model: Perception DC 18 plus a hidden circumstance bonus of +0 to +8, with the standing instruction never to show the modifier. §16 extends that to the Mirror's lean and the Mass Clock's position: never confirm any of the three.

**Let the cast be expert about the machine and ignorant about the enemy. Institutional fluency is free and should be given away constantly; anomaly fluency is the plot and must be earned.**

## 6. Sufficiently advanced technology, and running SF on a fantasy engine

Clarke's Third Law is usually quoted as permission to hand-wave; the useful reading is the opposite. What separates technology from magic in a story is not what the fiction calls it but **whether the audience can predict it**. A rule the audience can reason with is technology; a rule only the author knows is magic, whatever vocabulary it wears. **[C]**

This matters because the campaign literally runs on a fantasy engine with SF trappings: the Foundry `sf2e` system is built off the pf2e codebase. The setting answers the resulting texture in fiction rather than pretending the question does not arise — v4 §9's draining-magic account, plus a social rule with real table utility: discussing mitflit technology carries the taboo this city applies to magic, **wise to conceal, vulgar to flaunt**.

**A rule the players can reason with is technology; a rule only the GM knows is magic. Keep the Frames in the first category and the Watcher in the second — and move things from the second to the first deliberately, on a schedule.**

The campaign already schedules that migration, which is the part most designs miss: v4 §15's comprehension rungs make the enemy progressively legible under a hard gate, the Mirror becomes visible partway through the campaign, and the Mass Clock becomes readable once the party understands the food model — **show it to them; dread is fine, ambush is not**.

## 7. Estrangement of the familiar

The genre's most durable power is not inventing the new; it is making the ordinary visible. Shklovsky's *ostranenie* names the general effect, and SF's version applies it to **institutional and social behaviour**, which is otherwise nearly impossible to see from inside. **[T/C]** A form, a rota, a safety drill and a school timetable are the hardest things in a reader's life to notice, and setting them beside an impossibility is the most reliable way to make them noticeable.

This campaign's best science-fictional material is already procedural. **The sirens**: the Directorate's own alert system is a scream directly into the Watcher's ear, so every drill escalates the attack it warns about (`dossier-observer.md` §14) — a civil-defence procedure converted into an SF mechanism, and the cleanest instance of this move in the repo.

**When a scene needs to feel science-fictional, make a procedure strange rather than making a machine loud.** The Frames are already loud; the requisition form is what the table has never seen before.

See [environmental-storytelling-and-place.md](environmental-storytelling-and-place.md) for this in physical space, and [japanese-narrative-conventions.md](japanese-narrative-conventions.md) for the cultural-form material.

## 8. Super robot and real robot

The genre's fundamental split, and the most useful single distinction in this document. **[C]**

The **super robot** is mythic and singular. It has a name rather than a designation, there is one of it, and it is essentially heroic — the machine itself is on the side of good, not merely wielded by it. Damage is dramatic rather than logistical, and attacks are announced by name: Yokoyama's *Tetsujin 28-go* (1956), *Giant Robo* (1967), *Mazinger Z* (1972), which added the decisive move of putting the pilot inside. The **real robot** is mass-produced hardware with model numbers, supply lines and a political constituency; pilots are soldiers, units are replaceable, attrition is the subject — *Gundam* (1979), *Votoms* (1983), *Patlabor* (1988). Mixing them carelessly produces one failure: a machine treated as irreplaceable in one scene and as materiel in the next, leaving the audience unable to price any risk.

This campaign uses both. The party starts on Gen 1, a century-and-a-half-old utility fleet of converted diggers and cranes that anyone can fly; a Gen 2 machine arrives much later, its seat shaped to one pilot's true form (`frame.schema.json`; `ideas/story/bible/LEDGER.md` #32–#33). Either way the machine is a PC-grade sheet rather than a monster stat block or upscaled gear (`design-bible.md`), and [campaign-arc.md](campaign-arc.md) gives each pilot their own repair crew, with an NPC-pilot roster thinning out until the six PCs are the only ones left.

**Play the Frame as a super robot and everything surrounding it as a real robot. The machine is singular and irreplaceable; the hangar, the crews, the requisitions and the other pilots are logistics — and that contrast is where most of the campaign's texture lives.**

## 9. The 1950s and 1960s inheritance — what makes the register work, what breaks it

This campaign names *Giant Robo*, so state the inheritance precisely. The 1967 tokusatsu series, and *Tetsujin 28-go* before it, established the boy-and-his-machine relationship: a child holds the control device, the machine is enormous and obedient, and the story is about whether the child is worthy of it. The 1990s OVA is the operatic restatement most people now mean by the name. **[C]**

Four things make the register work. **Characters state intentions and loyalties out loud** — v4 §24: *"Everyone means it. Nobody deflects. Grief is loud, loyalty is declared aloud, techniques are announced by name."* **Moral clarity with genuine cost** — the right thing is knowable and expensive; clarity without cost is children's television, cost without clarity is a different genre. **Scale treated with awe rather than horror.** **No irony** — nobody in frame stands outside the story and rates it. What breaks it, in order of likelihood: irony, then unrelieved grimness, then clarity loss.

**A joke that costs a character nothing is register. A joke that lets a character avoid meaning something is drift. Cut the second kind and keep the first in quantity.**

v4 §6 makes humour load-bearing rather than tolerated, naming five comic vectors to use freely: the stray, the mitflits, the Watcher's early incompetence (it fights a statue; it loses to a reflection), institutional absurdity, and the magic show. All are situational or characterological, and none requires a character to deflect, which is why §6 and §24 are consistent. The repo's guard against drift is the best-phrased tone rule in the project: Known Risk 5 says the material *wants* to curdle, and **if a run of sessions has contained no jokes, no warmth, and no small good days, that is a fault, not a mood** — a testable condition rather than an aspiration.

The *Water Margin* half contributes the roll-call, the epithets, and the outlaw band more righteous than the state it serves. Its practical import: **the Directorate never has to become villainous; it only has to remain exactly what it has been.**

## 10. The sortie ritual

Launch sequences, the hangar, the countdown, the call-and-response over comms, the naming of the machine. Repeated ceremony is a genre pleasure rather than filler, for three reasons. **[C]** **A fixed frame makes variation legible**: the third time the table hears the same launch call, one changed word carries a scene's worth of information — a missing voice, a different officer signing the release — and that only works because the ritual was boring twice first. **It converts a transition into an event**: a sortie of known length is a controlled breath, and is where anticipation is manufactured. **It distributes attention**: a fixed running order gives every player a line.

[theatre-of-the-mind-staging.md](theatre-of-the-mind-staging.md) §5 already defines three sting tiers — Quiet, Announced, Splash — under the rule that **the loud one only works if it is rare**, and §6 adds that a transition should mean something, with types bound to kinds of event rather than varied for novelty.

**Write one launch sequence, use the same words every time, and change exactly one thing when you want the table to notice something.**

Gap: nothing in the repo contains the actual words. The staging document specifies banners, splashes and the tier ladder; no authored sortie text exists in `data/episodes/` or `design/`.

## 11. Rendering scale in prose

The most practically useful section here, because this campaign runs theatre of the mind on a 10x grid and must produce enormity entirely in spoken description. The film techniques translate directly and share one principle: **giantness is never an adjective attached to the machine.** It is a relationship between the machine and something small. **[C]**

1. **Describe from the ground first.** The witness is the unit of scale. Open every Contact beat at street level with someone who is not in a Frame, then cut up. This is the campaign's actual situation, not a preference — Frames are *"slow, tough and near-blind,"* while some vehicles *"see extremely well"* (`frame.schema.json`).
2. **Damage the environment, not the machine.** The design bible's reflavour instruction is that a Strike is a building-sized fist and a spell is a kilometre-wide effect. A *miss* is therefore a warehouse. Narrate what every attack did to the city, failures included, and size establishes itself without being stated.
3. **Slow the machine and speed the people.** Weight reads as delay: a Frame's movement is announced as taking time, a civilian's is instant. Frames are deliberately very slow (`open-questions.json#vehicle-and-frame-rules-model`) — narrate that as the machine being late, not the pilot being slow.
4. **Use sound at two ranges.** What it sounds like inside the cockpit, and what it sounds like three streets away. The second is where the size lives.
5. **Count in city units, not feet.** Say "a block and a half," never "seventy-five feet."
6. **Give the number one referent, then stop.** One concrete comparison per fight — the harbour crane, the causeway, the school roof. A second dilutes the first.
7. **Let the environment react before the enemy does.** Displaced water, glass, birds, a street emptying. Reaction shots are the whole cinematic vocabulary for scale, and they are free in prose.

**Scale is rendered by what the machine does to the world and by who is standing close enough to be afraid of it. It is never rendered by adjectives attached to the machine.**

Gap: there is no general convention that every Contact should carry at least one ground-visible fact. That has not been decided.

## 12. The pilot-machine relationship

The genre's actual subject, underneath the fights. Four non-exclusive traditions. **[C]** The machine as **armour**, an extension of the pilot's body and will (*Mazinger Z*). As **burden**, a debt that costs to operate and will eventually take something (*Votoms*). As **inheritance**, belonging to a predecessor or an institution, with the pilot measured against whoever held it before — this campaign's title sits on this tradition, and `data/hangar/legacy-units.json` exists for the graveyard even while empty. As **another person**, where the machine has a point of view. The fourth is rarest and changes what every other mechanic means.

*(Cut 2026-09-20 and 2026-09-24: this campaign's application, which read every Frame as a living, suppressed person, with Resonance, Bleed and berserk as consent mechanics. Gen 1 Frames have no living core, the Gen 2 core is biomechanical mitflit technology rather than a living mitflit, and there is no berserk (`ideas/story/bible/LEDGER.md` #31, #32, #34). Removed from this document 2026-09-25.)*

## 13. What the enemy is for

**Monster-of-the-week is a scheduling device, not a lazy structure.** An enemy per episode lets every episode have a complete shape while a serial plot advances underneath. It fails only when the weekly enemy is uncorrelated with the serial plot, at which point the audience correctly learns to stop caring about fights. Escalation keeps the schedule readable: the audience must be able to say what changed since last time, in one sentence, unaided. **[C]**

The genre's other recurring move — **making the enemy sympathetic or mistaken rather than evil** — is tradition, not novelty, and this campaign belongs inside it rather than departing from it. *Gundam*'s Zeon are people with a cause; *Votoms* has a war with no villain in it; *Patlabor*'s antagonists are largely labour disputes; even *Evangelion*'s Angels are not malicious. A mecha antagonist who is simply evil is the exception in this genre, not the rule.

What is unusual here is **where the mistake sits**: the party's own victories are the training signal. `dossier-observer.md` §8 — it built a body to be polite in, was shot in fear by a police officer as almost anyone would have been, felt nothing, and did the only thing a sensor system can do: it found the pattern. *Manifest, and they attack.* Every fight against it is *"more data about fighting."* Two running decisions are settled: it is never played as evil, and its early voice is frantic and polite rather than gloating (`dossier-observer.md` §9).

**An escalation ladder is only satisfying if the table can state out loud what changed since last time. Write that one-line answer before the fight; if you cannot write it, the fight is not an escalation and should be re-scoped.** v4 §15's comprehension rungs supply the answers in order, under the hard gate that it cannot exploit a category the fiction has not established it understands.

The genre-known risk here is a reveal that deflates the sky; precedent says the deflation is real and the only fix is a second engine already running when it lands. The bible's Known Risk 4 used to name exactly that, and since `LEDGER.md` #31 reads the other way — *"The truth makes the sky worse, not better"* — because the Watcher stays the adversary once understood.

## 14. Collateral damage and civilian consequence

This is where mecha stories usually locate their real stakes, for a mechanical rather than a moral reason: **the machine's damage resets between episodes and the city's does not.** A fight priced in the Frame's HP bar has no memory; a fight priced in a district can still be felt four sessions later. **[C]** *Patlabor* builds a whole register out of the paperwork afterwards, and the genre's most-remembered images are usually aftermath rather than combat.

[campaign-arc.md](campaign-arc.md) supplies this campaign's arc version: the city takes real visible scars, and **victories should cost something real even when they are clean tactical wins.**

**Report the cost of every Contact in the city's own terms — a district, a bridge, a school year, a shift that will not be worked — before reporting it in the Frame's.**

A real tension, named rather than smoothed. v4 §6 says death is rare, meaningful and never horrific, with no casual casualties and no atrocity for texture; `campaign-arc.md` says the throughline is enduring rather than restoring. Both are correct and they pull against each other on any given Contact. The reconciliation the genre normally reaches for is that **damage lands on places and institutions rather than on named people**. That is not stated as a rule anywhere in this repo, and this document does not answer it.

## 15. The pilot's age problem

Child pilots are a genre staple carrying two opposite readings, both live in any work that uses them. **[C/Q]** The **early reading** (*Tetsujin 28-go*, *Giant Robo*) treats a child holding enormous power as a story about responsibility, and it is fundamentally hopeful: Daisaku is not the machine's victim, he is its conscience, and the drama is whether he will be worthy of it. The **critical reading** (*Evangelion*, and most work after 1995) treats a society that puts children in weapons as indicting itself; the drama is about the adults, and the child's competence is the accusation rather than the reassurance.

This campaign has the critical reading in its premise — an institution that puts its students into its machines — and the early reading in its register, deliberately. (The founding charter's painted-out paragraph, which this paragraph used to cite as the foundation, has been cut, LEDGER #41.)

The two are compatible, and what makes them compatible is **where the accusation points**. v4 §1: nobody in this campaign has ever done anything evil. The Directorate is not a villain; it is frightened people exercising one reflex for two hundred years, and v4 §24 is explicit that it never has to become villainous.

**Point the accusation at the procedure, never at a person in the room, and never at the players for playing it. The kids are not the campaign's evidence; they are the people the campaign is warm toward.**

Two practical consequences, both already in the design. **The small good days are load-bearing, not decoration** — a run of sessions with no school, no jokes and no ordinary life converts the register to the critical reading automatically, without anyone deciding to, which is Known Risk 5 arriving through a side door. **The answer to the accusation is an arc, not a rule** — [campaign-arc.md](campaign-arc.md)'s core theme is a group that finally succeeds in defining their own objectives, process and independence against a system that demands great sacrifice while never conceding that what they gave was enough.

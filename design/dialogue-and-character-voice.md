# Dialogue and Character Voice

*Written 2026-09-13. One of eleven documents extending the original five-part narrative and game-design reference series (see [narrative-theory-foundations.md](narrative-theory-foundations.md) for the series introduction and the tagging scheme). Nothing in the existing library covers how people talk. This one does: what a line of dialogue is doing, how subtext works, the levers for making two NPCs sound different, exposition delivered through people who already know it, how game dialogue systems differ, a conversation read as a scene, and — the part with the most practical value here — how to write NPC voice material a GM can improvise inside with six players waiting. **This is not a decision document.** It does not override the v4 bible, `design/campaign-arc.md`, or anything in `data/campaign/open-questions.json`. Where useful it is checked against what this campaign already builds, by file; where there is a gap it says so and stops.*

*Claims are tagged **[E]** empirical, **[T]** theory, **[C]** craft (practitioner consensus, often righter than theory and never measured), **[Q]** contested. Dialogue craft is overwhelmingly **[C]** — there is very little measurement here, and inflating craft consensus into empirical finding would be the wrong kind of confidence.*

---

## 1. Dialogue is a negotiation, and a line with no want underneath it is exposition in a costume

Characters are in a scene because each wants something, and at least one wants something *from the other*. Dialogue is the negotiation of that want. **[C]** Everything a line can do — characterize, inform, raise tension, land a joke — it does as a by-product of somebody trying to get something. Remove the want and the line reverts to its underlying purpose, transmitting information to the audience. That is what "exposition" means as a criticism: not that information was delivered, but that delivering it was the only thing happening.

This is [quests-objectives-and-threads.md](quests-objectives-and-threads.md) §1's principle at sentence scale. Doran and Parberry's generated fetch-chains and their hand-analyzed human-authored quest had *identical* recursive structure, and the only variable that changed was whether a want existed at every node **[E]** — hence that document's rule, **depth is free, wants are not.** Five topics with a want behind each is a scene; five topics without one is a menu of facts with a face attached.

**This campaign already gets it right, and not by accident.** The v4 bible's Cast (§2) states a want for every named character in one clause — Oda is "mission above the people carrying it"; Ume's "every kindness she wins is charged to the technician-day pool." Those are negotiating positions, not descriptions: each tells you what the character does when a player says something unplanned. It is also already a **schema field**. `data/schema/scene-brief.schema.json`'s `cast[].brief.wants` describes itself as *"The single most useful field — it decides what they do when the players surprise you,"* and its companion `opensWith` as *"Their agenda, not yours."* Even walk-ons comply: the Intake Medicals clerk wants *"For the forms to be filled in correctly. She has no other ambitions today."*

**Before writing a single line for an NPC, write the clause saying what they want out of this conversation. Any line that does not serve that clause is exposition wearing a costume, and the table will hear the costume.**

## 2. Subtext: characterization lives in the gap between what is said and what is meant

People rarely state their actual position. The framing worth having is Grice's: conversation runs on a cooperative principle and a set of maxims (be truthful, be adequately informative and no more, be relevant, be clear), and the interesting work happens when a speaker **flouts** one *conspicuously*, in a way the listener is meant to notice. **[T]** The flouting generates an implicature — meaning carried by the violation rather than the words. This names the mechanism precisely: **subtext is not vagueness, it is a legible violation.** Vagueness the table cannot detect as deliberate reads as the GM not having an answer.

The techniques, all **[C]**, all cheap: **answer a different question than the one asked** (the most reliable — what the audience learns is *which* question was preferred); **change the subject**, especially to something mundane, which reads as more evasive than a grand deflection; **over-explain**, since a character who gives four reasons has one; **the interrupted sentence**, including self-interruption, where what they were about to say is delivered by its absence; **the beat of silence**, the most economical of all because the table fills it in themselves; and **refusing to speak at all**, the strongest and hardest to use twice.

**Checked against this campaign — there is a real tension worth settling on the record.** The v4 bible's register notes (§24) say *"Everyone means it. Nobody deflects,"* and §6 adds *"Big feelings, said out loud. Loyalty declared rather than implied."* Read carelessly that forbids subtext, and it would if subtext were one thing. It isn't, and the resolution falls out of the cast as written: **in this campaign, subtext is about facts, never about feelings.** Shucks Mordant does both at once — warmth unhidden and stated in the plainest language in the cast, while his scene brief's `willNotDiscuss` entry records that on the institution's secret *"he is forbidden, he thinks the rule is stupid, and both show to anyone who reads him."* Nobu is that shape inverted: `nobu.md` locks him as *"harried, fast, overworked... talks in run-ons and half-finished sentences, interrupts himself"* and then rules, *"Never write him nervous, evasive, or cagey. He isn't hiding badly, he's hiding well."* Taka's canteen brief gives him a change-of-subject with a silence in it — *"Goes quiet for exactly one beat when something real comes up, then deflects straight back to noise"* — which is also why his deflection does not violate §24: he deflects about a memory, not a feeling.

The mitflits have an **authored cultural reason** for obliqueness rather than a personal one — v4 §9: *"they will not explain themselves... wise to conceal, vulgar to flaunt."* A reason like that beats a hundred evasive lines, because it generates them. And the campaign's strongest piece of dialogue produces none: the Thread Grid's captured mitflit who, *"walked past an open hangar, stops talking entirely and cannot be made to start again"* (`ideas/story/bible/species/mitflits.md`). Nothing is said; everything is delivered.

**Write what the character is willing to say out loud and keep what they actually mean in the note beside it. The gap between them is the characterization, and collapsing it into the spoken line destroys it. In this campaign specifically: withhold facts, never feelings.**

## 3. Voice differentiation: the levers, ranked, and a checklist for improvising at speed

Strongest first. **[C]** throughout; reasonable practitioners disagree about the middle three.

1. **What the character never says.** Purely subtractive, invisible as technique, and the most reliable differentiator there is — someone who never says "sorry," never uses a name, or never asks about anyone else stays distinguishable all campaign with no distinctive phrase at all.
2. **Where they retreat under pressure.** Bureaucratic, jokey, formal, silent, technical. This does the most work in a TTRPG specifically, because players apply pressure constantly and that is exactly when a GM needs a rule to fall back on.
3. **Sentence length and rhythm.** The fastest thing an audience perceives — someone who speaks in questions is a different person from someone who speaks in statements, before any content is chosen.
4. **Diction level.** Assignable to an institution rather than a person, which is what v4 §2 does in noting the Directorate's register "is audibly colder than the school's."
5. **How they handle silence.** A behaviour, not a line, so it survives paraphrase.
6. **What they call people.** Cheap, and it encodes a relationship rather than a personality.
7. **Verbal tics.** Last.

**Tics are the weakest lever and by a wide margin the most over-used. [C]** A tic is transplantable — it moves to any other character unchanged, a good diagnostic that it carries no characterization. It degrades into a catchphrase, which is a label the audience recognizes rather than a person they understand. And it is *costless to the character*, where every lever above it costs something: a refusal to say a word implies a reason, and the reason is the character. One or two per person as a mid-scene handhold is right; a cast differentiated only by tics sounds like one person doing voices.

**This campaign differentiates on mechanism, and says so.** The three adult voices of Chapter One are a deliberate contrast set stated in cause terms — `taka.md`: *"Shucks is warm because he has infinite time; Nobu is generous despite having none; Taka is warm because it costs him nothing to be."* The reason sets the rhythm: Shucks gives instructions as single words ("Arm. Light. Breathe.") and *"asks a question and then actually waits"*; Nobu *"never says one thing at a time"*; Taka *"talks at full volume regardless of what's being discussed."* Lever 2 is authored directly too — Shucks' brief specifies *"Pressure makes him bureaucratic rather than frightened,"* one clause covering every scene in which a player leans on him, including the unwritten ones. The bible files then do the part most prep skips: **negative specification**, a mandatory "Tone and register — what NOT to do" section per file whose entries are tests, not moods. `nobu.md`: *"If a line of his could be read aloud in Shucks' voice without sounding wrong, it's the wrong line for Nobu."* A GM can apply that one second after improvising a line.

**One constraint follows from the thesis.** The v4 bible opens *"Nobody in this campaign has ever done anything evil."* That forbids a whole class of voice — the villain who explains their philosophy, the functionary cruel for texture. Every voice must be playable from the inside as a reasonable person, needing a *position* rather than a motive. A character who must stay defensible cannot be differentiated by being unpleasant, so the levers above are all there is.

**The improvisation checklist,** for an NPC invented because a player asked to talk to someone: **(1)** What do they want from this conversation? One clause. **(2)** Long sentences or short? Pick one and hold it. **(3)** What is the one thing they will not say, and why not? The *why* keeps the refusal in voice. **(4)** What do they do with a pause — fill it or let it sit? **(5)** What do they call the party? **(6)** Where do they go when leaned on? None of that requires inventing a backstory, and none of it is a tic. For honorifics and Japanese address terms — levers 4 and 6 simultaneously — see [japanese-narrative-conventions.md](japanese-narrative-conventions.md); for sentence-level craft generally, [prose-craft-and-naming.md](prose-craft-and-naming.md).

**Differentiate on rhythm, on what a character refuses to say, and on where they retreat under pressure. Reach for a verbal tic last, and never let one be the only thing separating two people.**

## 4. Exposition: getting information to the audience through people who already know it

The structural problem is permanent: the audience needs information every character in the scene already has, so anyone stating it plainly is talking for the audience's benefit and the scene stops being a negotiation (§1). The real techniques, all **[C]**:

- **The newcomer.** Someone who genuinely doesn't know asks. Honest, easy, self-limiting — it works exactly as long as the newcomer is plausibly new, and campaigns routinely keep using it months past that.
- **Disagreement between two informed characters.** The strongest available, and not close: two people who both know the facts and dispute their meaning deliver the facts *and* characterize both speakers *and* make the information contestable rather than authoritative — evidence to weigh instead of a briefing to absorb.
- **Reluctant disclosure.** The speaker pays something to say it, and the cost keeps it a negotiation.
- **Information under time pressure.** Compression is forced, and the listener's attention becomes diegetic rather than assumed.
- **The wrong version, corrected.** A confident incorrect account is more interesting than a correct one, and the correction lands harder than a first statement would.

**This campaign has promoted the strongest of these to the level of premise.** v4 §13 states the conference is *"reconstructed... through contradictory testimony... every one of them holds a piece and colours it with their own stake."* Then the line that makes it more than technique: *"This is the theme running as a game mechanic — a story about who gets believed, played by deciding who to believe."*

The Watcher/Observer split (v4 §2) is the compressed version. Two speakers use different words for one entity — *"The Watcher — what the frightened city calls it"* against *"The Observer — what the mitflits call it. Plain, benign, technical. A job title, not a malevolence"* — and the bible states the payload: *"The gap between these two words is the misunderstanding, and should function as an in-fiction clue."* (Since LEDGER #31 it adds that "benign" describes the word, not what the thing has become.) No character explains anything; the information rides on vocabulary choice. It becomes noticeable the moment someone hears a mitflit say "the Observer" in that flat technical way and notices nobody in the room is frightened. Exposition delivered by tone of voice, which is the highest form of this.

Reluctant disclosure is authored at field level (§7): Shucks' refusal carries information — there is a rule, he dislikes it, the thing behind it is institutional — without delivering content, and his brief records its shape as *"he deflects about the INSTITUTION'S secret, never about the player's."* The newcomer technique is available structurally rather than by contrivance, since every PC is new and was summoned and induction is chargen (`design/induction.md`) — a real asset with a shelf life measured in sessions, worth spending deliberately in Chapter One and worth noticing the day it stops being plausible.

**Prefer disagreement to explanation. Two informed characters disputing the meaning of a fact deliver the fact, characterize both of them, and hand the table something to weigh — the only exposition technique that gets stronger the more the audience already knows.**

## 5. Dialogue systems: trees, topic lists, keywords, and state

Four families, with what each affords and costs. **[C]** throughout.

**Branching trees.** Each choice leads to authored content and further choices. Affords precise dramatic control — timing, escalation, a line that lands because of the exact line before it. Costs combinatorially: content grows with the product of choices rather than their sum, most is never seen, and new information cannot be added without re-authoring the paths around it.

**Topic lists (hub-and-spoke).** The conversation is a hub; each topic is a spoke that delivers content and returns to it. Affords unbounded scale, additive authoring, and player-controlled pacing. Costs dramatic shape: hub-and-spoke has no arc by construction, because every spoke returns to the same state. Run purely as a topic list, a conversation is an interview, and interviews do not build.

**Keyword systems.** Topics as a vocabulary shared across NPCs (the Ultima and Morrowind lineage), where a word learned from one person can be raised with another. Affords discovery and cross-referencing. Costs coherence: the failure mode is every NPC having an opinion on every keyword, flattening the world into one voice with different portraits.

**Stateful conversation.** The same topic returns different content depending on world state, prior topics, or disposition. Affords memory and the felt sense of being *known* by the fiction, the most valued quality in this whole area. Costs an explicit state model plus per-state authoring.

**Why a topic list scales better than a tree for a long campaign.** A tree's authoring cost is per-path and its content is consumed once; a topic list's cost is per-topic and additive, and topics accumulate across arbitrarily many sessions without invalidating each other. In a campaign whose length and order are unknown — and where a GM is a live improvisation layer a tree cannot accommodate anyway — additive beats multiplicative. [agency-and-player-choice.md](agency-and-player-choice.md) §5 supplies the rest: Fendt et al. found players given choices plus immediate *acknowledgement* reported no significantly different sense of agency than players given genuine branching **[E]**, and that document's synthesis is to *"buy acknowledgement cheaply and constantly; buy real branching... only at the handful of places the campaign is actually built to diverge at."* Applied here: **a topic list plus a stateful acknowledgement layer delivers most of the felt responsiveness of a branching tree at additive rather than multiplicative cost.** That document's **[Q]** caveat applies harder to dialogue than anything else, because dialogue is where players probe most deliberately and compare notes most precisely.

**The topic-list system is already designed here, and its best feature is one most shipped systems lack.** v4 §13, in the Signals Liaison entry (a ground role, since cut; the mechanism stands on its own): *"each NPC carries a numbered topic list; when the players raise a subject, the GM ticks it and delivers that version. Ticked topics are recorded automatically and inform later sessions — including, usefully, what the party has never thought to ask."*

**The untickered topics are data too, and that is the genuinely original part.** Conventional systems record what was said; this one records the negative space, which answers questions nothing else can — which threads the party has no working model of, which NPC they have decided is not worth asking, which fact has sat available for four sessions untouched. That is a starvation detector for the Thread Grid and a direct input to pacing ([pacing-and-the-responsive-campaign.md](pacing-and-the-responsive-campaign.md)); given §13's framing of the campaign as a story about who gets believed, it is also a running measure of whose account the party has never sought.

Four existing pieces are one system, worth stating in one place since they are documented separately: **topics per scene** live in `scene-brief.schema.json`'s `cast[].brief.willDiscuss[]` (with `willNotDiscuss[]` and `coercible[]`); **topics per campaign** are v4 §13's numbered per-NPC list and its tick record; **state** is `npc.schema.json`'s `socialProfile` (`attitude` plus `facts[]`); and the **live tool** is `ideas/entries/dialogue-management-panel.json`, status `in-progress`.

**Gaps, named plainly.** The tick record is designed and not built — there is no recorder and no consumer for the negative-space read. And `socialProfile` exists with a full `facts[]`/`attitude` shape, but as of 2026-09-25 only 13 of the 42 files in `data/npcs/` populate it, so the stateful layer is still theoretical for most of the cast.

**Author a long campaign's dialogue as a topic list, not a tree, because topics are additive and paths are not — and build the record of which topics were never raised deliberately, not as a by-product.**

## 6. A conversation is a scene, and a scene that ends where it started did not happen

The screenwriting formulation transfers intact: a scene has an **entry state**, a **turn**, and an **exit state**. **[C]** If nothing is different at the end, the material may be enjoyable but it is texture rather than structure, and it will not be remembered — which [narrative-theory-foundations.md](narrative-theory-foundations.md) §2 supports empirically, since events off the causal chain are forgotten regardless of how well written. **[E]** What can turn, in rough order of how often it should: **information** (the default, weakest alone), **attitude**, **a decision**, **a relationship**, **a cost** — a favour, a secret, credibility, an opportunity now closed.

**Two of those are already persistent mechanics here.** `design/npc-social-intel.md` §1 defines **Facts** as *"permanent, discrete, revealed one at a time... Once revealed, a Fact never re-hides. It's knowledge, not a mood,"* and **Attitude** as *"a live, moving state, using SF2E's own real five-step scale rather than an invented one: Hostile → Unfriendly → Indifferent → Friendly → Helpful,"* kept between scenes *"instead of the GM holding it in their head."* The split is exactly right: *"you can know exactly how to manipulate someone who currently hates you."*

**The counterweight, which this campaign also states.** Not every conversation is a scene, and requiring every one to turn would strangle the ordinary talking that makes a cast feel inhabited. `npc-social-intel.md` §3: *"Most of a scene with a recurring NPC is just talking — nobody invokes a Fact, nobody rolls Request or Coerce. Nothing reveals, nothing shifts, nothing logs,"* and therefore *"the reveal control and the leverage buttons must never be the only way to interact with an NPC's card."* The rule above governs conversations a GM has decided to **run as scenes**. It is not a tax on chatting.

**The best-specified turn in the campaign is Nozomi's.** `ideas/story/bible/characters/nozomi.md` gives an entry state (*"a begrudging, already-debriefed witness"*), both exit states, and the reason they differ: *"The skill is in the asking. A conversation that doesn't 'ruffle her feathers' gets somewhere; one that pushes or treats her like a suspect makes her go quiet and stop cooperating."* The failure exit is worse than the entry state — *"she clams up, the party gets nothing, maybe burns the chance for anyone else to try"* — which is what makes it a scene rather than a gate. The file flags its own gap honestly: *"a DC and skill set for it isn't written yet."* Still open, and it is the difference between a well-specified scene and a runnable one.

**Before running a conversation as a scene, name what will be different at the end — and name the version where it goes badly, because a conversation with one possible exit state is a delivery mechanism with a face on it.**

## 7. Writing NPC dialogue material a GM can actually use live

The finding is blunt: **a script is close to useless at the table, because the players will not say the cue line.** Read-aloud text works for a scene's opening image, where nobody has spoken and there is no cue to miss. It fails for everything after, because dialogue prepared as a sequence assumes a sequence, and the fourth thing a player says is never what the fourth line was written to answer. What a GM needs is not a recording but a **generator** — enough structure to produce a line in this character's voice in answer to a question nobody anticipated. (Genuinely contested: practitioners split on read-aloud text in published adventures, one camp holding it gives inexperienced GMs a floor and the other that it trains them to deliver material rather than run situations. **[Q]** This takes a position only about *dialogue*, not scene-opening description, where the case for prepared text is much stronger.)

The generic craft recommendation is **a want, a register, two or three lines of actual sample speech, and what this person will not say.** This campaign's version is already built, and it is better. `data/schema/scene-brief.schema.json`'s `cast[].brief` carries `wants`, `opensWith`, `willDiscuss[]` (topic plus *"what they actually say or concede here"*), `willNotDiscuss[]`, `coercible[]`, and a `voice` object of `register`, `tics` and `samples`.

Three of those fields are the improvement on the generic recipe. **`willNotDiscuss` requires a `why`**, described in the schema as *"what lets you improvise the refusal in their voice instead of just saying no"* — a hard line turned from a wall into a generator. **`coercible` as a separate array** encodes that "won't say" and "won't say yet" are different states, which is the difference between an NPC who is a lock and one who is a negotiation (§1); it also records *what kind* of pressure works, and a `challengeRef` when getting it is a real roll. And **`opensWith`** solves the live problem of a scene where the players say nothing useful for thirty seconds. One smaller detail is worth copying wherever else this project builds voice material: the `cast` array's description says *"a brief for three lines of foreman is still worth thirty seconds of authoring"* — proven by the canteen scene, where two texture students with one want and one sample line each are distinguishable from each other and from Taka.

**On the samples themselves:** three to six, spread by *function* rather than being the character's best lines — an opener, a refusal, a moment of warmth, one under pressure. Shucks' six do exactly this, from an instruction (*"Arm. Other arm. Look at the light, not at me."*) through a deflection that is also a kindness (*"It's a screen. Screens flag things. You're fine."*) to the question carrying the scene's whole emotional content (*"If you come in here unconscious, what do I need to know that I wouldn't be able to see?"*). A GM who has read those six can answer a seventh question in his voice, which is the only test that matters. The other half is §3's **negative specification**: positive notes tell a GM what to aim at, negative ones tell them what they just did wrong, which is the feedback that actually arrives during play.

**Gap, named and stopped.** As of 2026-09-25, 27 scene briefs in `data/scene-briefs/` and ten character files in `ideas/story/bible/characters/`, against 42 files in `data/npcs/`. The machinery is well-designed and demonstrably works; coverage is thin. That is a content gap, not a design gap.

**Prep a voice, not a script: a want, an opener, a register, samples spread across function, the hard lines with their reasons, and at least one note on what this character must never sound like. If a GM can answer an unanticipated question in the character's voice after reading it, the brief works; if they can only read out what is written, it does not.**

## 8. The one place this repo's plain-prose rule inverts — its complement, not an exception

This repo has a standing hard rule about GM-facing prose: **plain instructional sentences for anything read at the table; no aphorism; state the instruction outright.** A GM scanning a brief mid-scene cannot afford to decode a clever sentence, and an aphorism is a sentence that requires decoding by design. Every instruction in this document obeys that rule, including the bolded ones.

**Character dialogue is the one place where the opposite is true, and it is the rule's complement.** Characters may be oblique, evasive, allusive and aphoristic precisely because people are, and §2 is the whole argument for why that is where characterization lives. The two do not conflict because they govern different objects: **the frame is plain; the picture is not.**

- **Anything a GM reads in order to know what to do is an instruction. Write it plainly.** Wants, notes on what a topic concedes, the reason behind a hard line, the tone warnings, the "what NOT to do" tests.
- **Anything a GM reads in order to know how someone sounds is speech, and may be as oblique as the person is.** Sample lines, openers, the exact words of a refusal.

The scene-brief schema already draws this line **by field**, the cleanest possible place: `wants`, `willDiscuss.note`, `willNotDiscuss.why` and `coercible.how` are instructions and read as plain prose; `opensWith` and `voice.samples` are speech and do not. The schema even carries the typographic consequence, requiring the panel to set `samples` *"in a different face from everything around it, because a line you might actually say out loud is not the same kind of text as a note about them."* Nothing needs deciding here — it needs noticing, because the rule is carried as a standing working instruction, applied consistently across `data/scene-briefs/` and `ideas/story/bible/`, and written down in no design document. This is the first one that states it.

Two failure modes follow. **An instruction written as an aphorism** is unusable at speed, because the GM must interpret it in the one moment they have no capacity to: "kindness is a currency here" is a bad note; "every accommodation she wins costs technician-days from the repair pool, and she knows it" is the same idea as an instruction, and it tells you what to say next. **Dialogue written as an instruction** produces a character who states their own function — §1's exposition-in-a-costume at its purest. An NPC saying "I'm the one who decides what gets repaired" is a brief leaking through a mouth; the line a person in that job would say is about the repair.

One clarification, because the two axes are easy to conflate: **plainness is not the opposite of obliqueness.** A character can speak in the plainest possible language and still be entirely oblique about what they choose to be plain *about*. Shucks is the proof — single-word instructions, the least ornamented speech in the cast, and the most thoroughly withholding character in Chapter One. Obliqueness is a property of what is selected for saying, not of how ornate the saying is, which is also the version of subtext that survives improvisation at a table.

**Write every GM instruction as a sentence that can be acted on without interpretation, and every line of character speech as something a person would actually say. When a line is doing both jobs, it is in the wrong field.**

## 9. The pressure valve: not every fact should have to arrive through a mouth

If every important fact must reach the table through an NPC's speech, NPCs must become explainers, and §2's obliqueness and §4's preference for disagreement both become luxuries the campaign cannot pay for. A non-dialogue delivery route buys them back.

`design/encyclopaedia-checks.md` is this campaign's version — Disco Elysium-style optional insight checks, offered proactively, doing three jobs: *"Background... Explain the unusual... Set up future advantage."* The rule that makes it safe to use constantly is stated there: **"failure never costs anything mechanical."** The consequence it draws is itself voice craft, aimed at the party's own internal monologue rather than an NPC: *"A wrong-but-plausible belief on a bad roll is fine — often more interesting than a flat 'you don't know.'"* A wrong belief is a characterized answer, doing what §4's "wrong version, corrected" does with the party supplying the wrong version themselves. The same logic runs through the Character Card's reveal triggers (`npc-social-intel.md` §4), where a Fact can be unlocked by *"Sense Motive to read them cold"*: that is how **subtext becomes text without the NPC having to say it** — the character stays oblique, the player still gets the read, and the read was earned rather than handed over. **[C]**

**Give the table a non-dialogue route to every fact that matters, so no NPC is ever forced to become an explainer to keep the plot moving.**

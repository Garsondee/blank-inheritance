# TTRPG Campaign and Session Craft

*Written 2026-09-13. Third of five narrative/game-design reference documents (see [narrative-theory-foundations.md](narrative-theory-foundations.md) for the series introduction). Where the first two documents cover how narrative works cognitively and how player choice works structurally, this one is table-tested GM craft — decades of published, practiced technique for running exactly the kind of long, branching, high-stakes campaign this one is. Most of it validates decisions this campaign has already made independently; a few sections flag genuine risk this project's own "Known Risks" (v4 bible §22) already names, with a specific theoretical name attached so it's easier to watch for.*

---

## 1. A shared vocabulary for what different people want from a session (used descriptively only)

Ron Edwards's **GNS theory** proposes that play tends to organize around one of three creative agendas: **gamism** (step on up — skill, competition, tactical accomplishment), **narrativism** (story now — addressing a theme through character choice), and **simulationism** (the right to dream — exploration and internal consistency valued for their own sake). **[Q — influential, empirically weak, sociologically contentious in its home community]** Use the words descriptively, to notice what a given scene or a given player is actually oriented toward, never prescriptively, and never at the table itself.

The one genuinely durable contribution from the same tradition is **the Impossible Thing Before Breakfast**: the incoherent proposition that the GM authors the story *and* the players determine what their characters do — both can't be fully true at once, and every design resolves the tension somewhere. **[T]** This campaign's resolution is already explicit and correctly stated in [agency-and-player-choice.md](agency-and-player-choice.md): the GM authors the *space of what could happen* (the threads, the escalation curve, the peril conditions), the table authors *what actually happens* within it (which bifurcation points fire, who lives, what the stray becomes). Naming this clearly is useful the one time it actually comes up — when a scene feels like it's fighting itself, it's often because the GM and the moment have quietly slid into believing both halves of the Impossible Thing are true simultaneously (a fixed outcome *and* real player agency over the same event), and the fix is deciding, for that specific event, which half is actually in force.

## 2. "Prep situations, not plots" — and this campaign already does it

Justin Alexander's most durable formulation, and the single most-adopted principle in contemporary GM practice: **a plot is a sequence of events you expect to happen; a situation is a set of circumstances that plays out differently depending on what the players do.** **[C]** The corollary — prep toys, not journeys — is the operational form: give the table objects, people, and pressures with their own logic, not a script.

The Thread Grid (v4 bible §17) is explicitly built this way, and says so: "**A working reference, not a script. Cells are beats, not scenes.**" A beat is a situation (Chapter 6's "core revealed; the sit-in" is a state of the world, not a scripted sequence of dialogue); a scene is what a table does with that situation on the night. This is precisely Alexander's distinction, arrived at independently, and it's worth protecting deliberately under pressure — the failure mode this principle guards against is a GM (or a piece of prep) quietly hardening a beat into a script between prep and table, at which point the first player action that doesn't fit the imagined script either gets railroaded past or breaks the session.

**Node-based scenario design** — the companion technique — treats a scenario as nodes (places, people, organizations, events) connected by *leads*: pieces of information that point from one node toward another, discoverable by any means the table comes up with, in any order. **[C]** This campaign's clue web (the graffiti, the second witness, the photograph, the stencil pattern in Chapter 4) is a lead-based node structure already; the "the party works out *where it's looking* before *what it is*" line (v4 bible §17) is a direct description of node-based investigation working as intended — the nodes (the sites) get found before the connecting fact (the mechanism) does, which is the natural order leads produce and script-based mystery design usually gets backwards.

## 3. The Three Clue Rule — and where this campaign should audit itself for it

Alexander's second major contribution: **for any conclusion the plot depends on, provide at least three independent ways to reach it.** **[C]** The reasoning is redundancy under realistic table conditions — players will miss one clue and misinterpret a second, and a third source is what stops a single missed detail from stalling the whole plot. The **inversion** matters just as much: given any single clue, ask what conclusions a clever table could draw from *just that one*, and be ready for them to get there faster than planned.

This is worth an explicit, deliberate audit against the current design, because the campaign's evidence is currently spread thoughtfully but not obviously counted:

- **"The party learns the mechanism, not the truth"** thread (Cult, §5–7 of the Chapter Grid) has several independent lines converging — Cicada's own declaration (§5), the sceptics' forty years of independent investigation (§7, previously unconnected to the party), Hori's direct testimony (§7), the recovered revenge list (§7). That's well past three, which is exactly right for the campaign's central mystery.
- **The column's existence** (found in Chapter 6) is worth checking the same way: is it independently reachable via the heist-loop friction becoming visible (§11) and via at least two more routes, or does it currently depend on one specific player making one specific check on one specific night? If it's the latter, that's the single point of failure the rule exists to eliminate — not because the design is wrong, but because it's worth a two-minute check rather than an assumption.
- **The inversion, applied to the graffiti (Chapter 2)**: a pre-dated tag is a strong, deliberately anomalous clue, and per the inversion a sharp table might reach "the Cult can predict or is coordinated with the attacks" well before Chapter 6's intended reveal. That's not a flaw — arriving early at a true partial conclusion is the reward the anomaly-before-question convention is designed to produce (see [narrative-theory-foundations.md](narrative-theory-foundations.md) §4) — but it's worth having a ready answer for a table that gets there in session three rather than session six, rather than being caught flat-footed by a smart guess landing early.

## 4. Fronts and clocks — the closest existing vocabulary for the Escalation Curve and the Mass Clock

**Apocalypse World's fronts** and **Dungeon World's** refinement give a standard anatomy for offscreen, ongoing threats: a **front** bundles related dangers around a shared **impulse** (its single motivating drive if left unopposed); each danger advances through **grim portents** toward an **impending doom**; **progress clocks** (the mechanism popularized by *Blades in the Dark*) are segmented tracks, advanced only by real fictional events, that make offscreen change visible and inevitable-feeling without being predetermined in *when* it lands. **[C]**

Translated directly onto this campaign's own structure:

| Fronts vocabulary | This campaign's equivalent |
|---|---|
| Front | The Watcher's whole arc |
| Impulse | Observe, multiply and expand (`ideas/story/bible/LEDGER.md` #31) |
| Danger / grim portent | Each comprehension rung (v4 bible §15's table: what exists → what moves is alive → Frames matter → …) |
| Impending doom | The runaway — its children feed it, and it no longer needs any human, Manako included, to supply it (`ideas/story/dossier-observer.md` §16) |
| Progress clock | The Mass Clock (§15) — explicitly "not a timer… advanced by what happens rather than by how many sessions pass" |

This translation is worth having for one specific, practical reason: the fronts literature has hard-won, specific guidance about **how many fronts a campaign can carry at once** — roughly three is the usual working ceiling before a table can't hold the threats in mind, and the v4 bible's own **Known Risk #1** ("Six players, twelve sheets, six threads, three flashback layers, a side-plot engine, a procedural Contact system, a dossier metagame, and an AI antagonist reading logs… the apparatus collapsing under its own weight") is independently naming the same ceiling from the other direction. The fronts tradition's specific fix for "too many fronts" is worth knowing precisely because it's already the fix Known Risk #1 gestures at without naming it: **not every thread needs equal weight in every chapter.** The Thread Grid already does this (School is "live in every chapter" by explicit design choice, while Founders appears mainly as flashbacks at specific points) — the fronts framing just gives a name to *why* uneven weighting across sessions is the correct answer rather than a compromise: a table can track six fronts total across a campaign far more easily than it can track six fronts all advancing in every single session.

One more piece of clock-specific craft worth stating precisely, because it's easy to violate by accident: **a clock only advances on a real fictional event that causes it, never on a schedule.** The Mass Clock is already built this way ("Pace is entirely yours; the clock only moves when someone moves it"), and it's worth protecting this explicitly against the natural GM instinct, under time pressure, to nudge a clock forward "because it's been a few sessions" — the moment a clock advances without an in-fiction cause, it stops being a clock and becomes a countdown timer, which is a different, weaker tool that the design deliberately isn't using.

## 5. Fail-forward and the Peril Roster's actual mechanism

**Conflict resolution** (as opposed to task resolution) asks "did the *larger stakes* resolve," not "did this one small action succeed" — and the **fail-forward** principle built on top of it (from *Burning Wheel*, *Powered by the Apocalypse*, *Forged in the Dark*) holds that a failure should change the fictional situation, never simply repeat it or produce nothing. **[C]** A roll that just fails and changes nothing is a stall; a roll that fails and moves the story somewhere new — at a cost — is what keeps momentum.

**The Peril Roster (v4 bible §21) is fail-forward design applied to whole named NPCs across a campaign, not just to single rolls, and it's worth recognizing as the same principle at a larger grain.** "Nobody on this list is scheduled to die. Each has a trigger condition and a save condition, and the outcome belongs to play" is exactly the conflict-resolution move: the stakes (will this named person come through?) are what's actually being resolved, and *how* it resolves — badly, well, or somewhere costly in between — is play's job, never a pre-written outcome. The explicit rule "manufactured jeopardy is fine and encouraged… what is not fine is deciding the outcome in advance" is fail-forward's core discipline stated almost word for word.

This is worth extending consciously to any *new* named NPC introduced mid-campaign: give them a trigger (what puts them in real danger) and a save condition (what specific, achievable thing the table can do about it) at the moment they're created, rather than deciding in advance whether they live. That's cheaper to improvise than it sounds, because it only requires answering one question — "what would have to be true, in the fiction, for this person to come through this" — and it guarantees the NPC's fate is always fail-forward rather than pre-scripted.

## 6. The railroading debate, resolved for this campaign already — and one term worth having

The hobby's long argument over **railroading** (forcing a fixed sequence regardless of choice), **illusionism** (secretly ensuring outcomes while players believe they're choosing freely), and **participationism** (the same, but disclosed and consented to) comes down to one honest distinction: illusionism works only until it's detected, and detection is catastrophic because it retroactively erases every choice the table thought it had made. **[C/Q]** The **quantum ogre** — the encounter that's behind whichever door gets opened — is the canonical illusionism example.

This campaign's Bifurcation Points table (v4 bible §20) is the disclosed, honest version done right, and it's worth being explicit about why: each fork states **both** branches' consequences in advance, in the design document, where they're checkable — "Find Nozomi early? If yes: Cicada's argument arrives from a trusted source before the party has any defence against it. If no: she surfaces in Ch7 among the schism, harder to reach." Both branches are real, different, and pre-considered. That's the opposite of a quantum ogre: nothing behind either door was decided *after* the players chose which door to open.

The one term worth adding to this project's working vocabulary: **the quantum beat** — any piece of prepared content (a side plot, a scene, an NPC reaction) that would land identically regardless of what the party actually did leading up to it. It's the small-scale version of the quantum ogre, and it's worth a quick self-check on any new scene before running it: does this scene's content depend on anything specific the party has done, learned, or chosen — or would it play out exactly the same either way? A scene that fails this check isn't dishonest the way a quantum ogre is, but it is a missed opportunity for the acknowledgement-and-inflection cheapness discussed in [agency-and-player-choice.md](agency-and-player-choice.md) §5 — a small addition (one line referencing what actually happened) usually converts it for free.

## 7. Prep philosophy — "seeds, unremarked" as an existing implementation of a named technique

The Lazy Dungeon Master tradition's central invention is **secrets and clues**: short (a sentence or two), relevant facts, deliberately written **abstracted from the place they're discovered** — the GM decides *what* is true and lets play decide *how* the table finds out. **[C]** This is exactly the "**Seeds, unremarked**" convention already used throughout the Chapter Grid (v4 bible §19 — "no approach vector; contact time-stamped before the alert," "the exam timetable continues unchanged") — each is a short, portable fact, not tied to a specific discovery method, ready to surface whenever play naturally reaches for it.

The broader prep-philosophy consensus across this tradition, Alexander's node-based design, and the fronts tradition converges on one sentence: **prepare things that are useful regardless of what the players do — situations, secrets, NPCs with wants, pressures that advance on their own — never sequences.** Everything about this campaign's structure that keeps content reachable from multiple angles (the Thread Grid's beats-not-scenes discipline, the seeds-unremarked convention) is an implementation of this principle; anything that would hard-wire a specific order onto a scene is in tension with it, and that tension is worth noticing consciously any time new prep starts to read like a script rather than a situation.

## 8. Campaign shapes — relevant background for the open overworld question

The hobby has a handful of named large-scale campaign shapes worth knowing as options, not as a recommendation for any specific one: **the adventure path** (a linear chain of pre-authored scenarios — momentum, but brittle to deviation), **the sandbox** (situations and factions placed in a space with no fixed plot — highly player-driven, needs active offscreen pressure like a fronts system to avoid feeling inert), **the pointcrawl** (nodes and known connecting routes rather than open terrain — closest structurally to a node graph), and **West Marches** (an open table where players themselves declare a destination before a session so the GM can prep the specific region needed, rather than the whole map at once). **[C]**

This is directly relevant background for the overworld/signup design (first sketched in a session-flow draft retired 2026-09-25, now settled as the downtime segment mechanic in `ux-doctrine.md` §3): the "players sign up for an encounter, pick map pins" flow is structurally closest to a **pointcrawl** (a map of discrete, connected points of interest) with a **West Marches-style commitment step** (players declare where they're going before the GM commits full prep to that location) layered on top. Naming the closest existing shapes doesn't answer that document's four open scope questions, but it does mean the design isn't starting from nothing — the pointcrawl tradition in particular has existing, tested answers for "what happens when players don't all pick the same destination" (parallel small-group scenes, exactly what the flow document is already describing) that are worth consulting once the scope questions get real answers.

## 9. Player types, and the Time Economy's menu as coverage

Robin Laws's practitioner taxonomy of player motivations — the Power Gamer, the Butt-Kicker, the Tactician, the Specialist, the Method Actor, the Storyteller, the Casual Gamer — remains the most useful shorthand in the hobby for the plain fact that **a real table contains several different kinds of engagement at once**, and a campaign that serves only one type is failing most of the room most of the time. **[C]**

The Time Economy's downtime menu (`config.json`: Frame Modification, Repair Advocacy, Investigation, Analysis Duty, Rest — the design bible's Sync Training and Anchor Time went with Resonance and Anchors on 2026-09-20) already reads as deliberate coverage across these types without naming them as such — Frame Modification serves the tactician/power-gamer instinct, Investigation serves the specialist and storyteller, Analysis Duty serves the tactician wanting to manage risk numerically. Worth checking consciously any time the menu gets revised: does the change still give every type of player at the table something that's specifically for them, or does it quietly narrow toward serving only the types whose preferences are loudest in the room that week.

## 10. A note on safety tools, given this campaign's actual content

This campaign's material is intense by design: an institution's use of children played straight as "legal, not criminal" horror, forced imprisonment of a survivor, grief carried for decades, disfigurement, and a slow-burn thesis that entire lives get built on top of a single moment of ordinary human failure. None of that needs softening — the tone notes (v4 bible §6, §24) are explicit that the intended register is warm and operatic specifically *because* the material underneath is heavy, and that's a defensible, well-considered choice.

Standard, low-overhead practice for material at this weight, worth having explicitly in place rather than assumed: **a brief session-zero-style conversation about content boundaries** (what the table wants signaled rather than played out) and **a simple in-session signal** for "pause or redirect this, no explanation needed" (a spoken word, a hand gesture, whatever the table finds least awkward). **[C]** This is process, not content — it changes nothing about what the campaign is about, only gives the table a low-friction way to say "not like that" if something ever lands differently than intended. Given how much of this campaign's design already anticipates and handles heavy material carefully (the Peril Roster's explicit "nobody scheduled to die," the Mercy option built into every major confrontation per v4 bible §7), a lightweight version of this is likely already the table's practice; naming it here is just making sure it's a deliberate agreement rather than an assumption.

# Mechanics as Meaning — what a rule argues

*Written 2026-09-13. Part of the narrative and game-design reference series begun with [narrative-theory-foundations.md](narrative-theory-foundations.md), which carries the series introduction. The existing library covers how stories work, how player choice works, and how a GM runs and paces material, but never asks what the rules themselves say. This document covers that: systems as arguments, the gap between a stated theme and a rewarded behaviour, when a mechanic should feel like the thing it models, what giving something a number tells a table, and how to test whether a rule carries meaning or only carries numbers. Its nearest sibling on the other side is [rpg-systems-design.md](rpg-systems-design.md), which covers how to build a rule that works; this covers what a working rule ends up meaning. **This is not a decision document.** Nothing here overrides `ideas/story/blank-inheritance-v4.md`, `design/campaign-arc.md`, or any entry in `data/campaign/open-questions.json`. Where it touches a tracked open question it says so and stops.*

*Claims are tagged: **[E]** empirical, **[T]** theory (a framework — vocabulary, not proof), **[C]** craft (practitioner consensus, often righter than theory and never measured), **[Q]** contested, weak, or extrapolated beyond what was tested.*

---

## 1. Procedural rhetoric — a rule is a claim about how the world works

Ian Bogost's *Persuasive Games* (2007) names the subject of this document: **procedural rhetoric**, persuasion through rule-based representation rather than through words or images. **[T]** A system does not describe a claim about the world, it enacts one, and the player learns the claim by operating the system. His worked examples are advocacy games whose rules encode an economic argument — every profitable path requires a small cruelty, so the player derives the argument by playing rather than by being told.

Carry the criticism alongside it. Miguel Sicart's "Against Procedurality" (*Game Studies*, 2011) argues that procedural rhetoric over-credits the rules and under-credits play: meaning is made by players in the act of playing, not delivered intact by a system. **[Q]** Both are right. Rules do argue; players argue back.

Every rule answers a question about the setting whether or not the designer meant it to. **Before writing a rule, state in one plain sentence the claim about the world it enacts. If you cannot state it, the rule is decoration — and it will still be making some claim you did not choose.**

Checked against this campaign: `data/campaign/config.json`'s `twoTracksPrinciple` is already a claim in that form — *"the difference between a well-synced and poorly-synced pilot is what they're allowed to do, not how good they are at doing it."* That is not a balance note. It is an argument about what sync is: a permission granted by the machine, not a skill the child possesses. The mechanic and the claim were written together, which is the condition this section asks for.

## 2. MDA — build mechanics, meet aesthetics, reason in both directions

Hunicke, LeBlanc and Zubek's MDA framework (AAAI workshop, 2004) splits a game into **mechanics** (the rules as authored), **dynamics** (the behaviour those rules produce at run time), and **aesthetics** (the experience the player has). **[T]** Its load-bearing observation is directional: the designer builds mechanics and hopes for aesthetics, while the player meets aesthetics and only ever infers the mechanics. **The designer therefore has to reason in the direction they do not build in** — name the target experience first, predict the dynamic that would produce it, then author the mechanic that produces that dynamic.

Its eight-item aesthetics taxonomy is ad hoc and the clean three-layer separation is widely argued with. **[Q]** Treat MDA as diagnostic vocabulary, not a form to fill in. The diagnosis is the part worth keeping: a bad aesthetic over a healthy dynamic is a framing problem, a bad dynamic over sound mechanics is an emergent-behaviour problem that more rules usually worsen, and only the third case is fixed by editing rules.

Checked against this campaign: `design/design-layers.md` is a dynamics-diagnosis instrument built without the vocabulary. Its stated reason for layering is **attribution of failure** — *"building everything at once destroys your ability to diagnose"* — and its rule that a problem appearing after a layer goes on makes *"the layer the suspect — never the foundation"* turns the MDA question into something answerable by construction rather than by argument.

## 3. Ludonarrative dissonance, stated precisely — and its inverse

Clint Hocking coined the term in a 2007 blog post about *BioShock*. **[T/Q]** His argument is more specific than the phrase's later use suggests, and the loose version is useless.

The game offers two contracts that cannot both be honoured. The **ludic contract** — what the reward structure teaches — is that pursuing self-interest is how you succeed: the power economy pays out for harvesting, and the system's shape endorses the setting's objectivist philosophy. The **narrative contract** — what the story requires — is that the player repeatedly acts against self-interest on another character's behalf, and the plot's critique lands only if that selflessness is real. The two argue opposite positions on the same question, and the game never acknowledges the conflict, so it undermines both halves. The dissonance is not that the story and the gameplay *feel different*. It is that they make **contradictory claims about the same proposition.**

The criticism the term has attracted is substantial and largely fair. **[Q]** It gets applied to any tonal mismatch at all — protagonist body counts, health restored by eating, a serious plot in a game with a jump button — most of which are ordinary abstraction rather than contradiction, and players do not experience abstraction as a broken promise. It gets used as a verdict rather than an observation. And it ignores that dissonance is sometimes deliberate, with the friction as the subject. **Use the term only where you can name the proposition both halves argue about and state both claims; otherwise what you have is a tonal mismatch, and those are cheap to fix.**

The inverse gets discussed far less and is more useful. A system is in **ludonarrative harmony** when the behaviour it rewards is the behaviour the story says is correct, so that a player optimising purely for mechanical advantage arrives at the protagonist the fiction wanted anyway. **[C]** Flavour is what a rule is called; harmony is what a rule pays for. **Test any single rule with three questions: what behaviour does it pay for (follow the reward, not the name); what would a player who only wanted to win do repeatedly because of it; and is that person recognisable as a protagonist of the story being told?** If no, the rule argues against the story, and the rule wins — rules are executed every session and themes are stated occasionally.

Checked against this campaign, the test passes cleanly on the central emotional loop, and `design/resonance-and-bleed.md` §4 already worked out why: **there is no safe play.** Engaging with an Anchor risks violating it and losing Resonance; declining engagement charges Bleed through Suppression; pushing to compensate charges Bleed faster. It pays for emotional engagement; a pure optimiser engages emotionally and manages the cost; that person is exactly the protagonist of a story about children who have to spend themselves to be useful. That loop was arrived at independently of the theory and it is the strongest piece of harmony in the design.

## 4. The Mirror — the campaign's thesis compiled into a rule

This is the sharpest worked example of procedural rhetoric in the repository, and the library should say so plainly.

The thesis, `ideas/story/blank-inheritance-v4.md` §1: **everything in this setting becomes what it was mistaken for.** The antagonist, §4: the Watcher is wreckage rather than an invader, *"a semi-sentient cloud with no will of its own worth the name — and it conforms to the strongest coherent signal it receives."* Its history is that thesis already executed once — it built a body in good faith, a frightened police officer shot it, and *"it found the pattern. Manifest, and they attack."* The consequence, same section: *"Fighting it teaches it that fighting is correct. The players are not opposing the engine. They are half of it."*

The Mirror (§6) turns the premise into a procedure: a merciful party teaches it that manifesting need not mean attacking and it begins producing things that observe rather than things that kill; a brutal party teaches the opposite and it obliges. `design/strategy-layer.md` §4 gives that its operating form at each debrief — **what** the Observer does comes from its Comprehension rung, **how** from the Mirror, **how big** from Mass.

Three things make this textbook rather than gestural. **The claim is computed, not asserted** — nobody has to tell the table that treating a thing as a monster produces a monster, because the table produces the evidence itself and the number that reads their behaviour is the number that shapes what comes back. **The argument is falsifiable in play** — a procedural argument the player cannot test is an assertion in a different font, and here the players can change their behaviour deliberately and watch the output change, which makes the eventual realisation a discovery rather than a lecture. **It does not require the antagonist to have a plan** — `design/strategy-layer.md` §8 states the constraint directly, that the Observer's turn is a function of three numbers and *"it has no intent, and the moment it appears to have one, the campaign's thesis breaks."* That prohibition protects the argument from the likeliest way it would be spoiled at the table.

[agency-and-player-choice.md](agency-and-player-choice.md) §1 covers the Mirror from the agency side and is not repeated here. What this document adds is about content rather than structure: **the Mirror is not a mechanic that hosts the theme, it is a mechanic that states it.** Protect it on those grounds specifically, separately from its tonal usefulness.

The Mass Clock (§15) applies the same technique to the deadline. It rises with every use of Sap *including the party's own*, and counts down not to defeat but to the expiry of an option — at sufficient mass the cloud self-organises and *"the option of correcting the Observer expires."* A timer that ends the campaign argues that survival is the stake. This one argues that mercy is time-limited, which is the claim the campaign is actually making.

## 5. The live question: "nothing is ever killed" against a hit-point system

Examine this honestly rather than resolving it. It is the campaign's most exposed seam.

The fiction-level claim, `ideas/story/blank-inheritance-v4.md` §4: Claimants are condensations of the cloud, *"nothing is ever killed… Every victory is a dispersal. The war is unwinnable by attrition."* §7 extends it — no corpses on the enemy side by construction, the Watcher was never a kill target, and every major confrontation *"offers kill / capture / destroy / protect as visible, supported options, and the adventure adjusts."* The mechanical reality: SF2E combat is a procedure for reducing a number to zero, and the campaign runs it unmodified by design (`design/design-layers.md`, Layer 0).

**Where this is genuine harmony, and it is more than a reskin.** The fiction supplies a real referent for every part of the mechanic instead of renaming the output. A Claimant's HP is how much matter is held in coherence at that site; zero is the envelope failing; the particles going back is why there is no body. `design/scale-and-chassis.md` §8 does the same on the friendly side with an actual mechanical difference rather than a label: damage caps a machine's actions where it never caps a kid's, because *"it is a machine coming apart, not a person enduring,"* and the track ends at Hulked rather than dead. The fiction is not laid over the arithmetic; it explains the arithmetic.

**Where the dissonance is live, and it is about payment rather than description.** §3's test asks what a rule pays for, and advancement pays for winning fights. `design/campaign-arc.md` states progression as milestone-based, gated by *"a significant defeat of a Claimant, followed by enough downtime for the pilots' Frames to actually be refit/upgraded,"* with upgrade points earned from downtime activities and from winning combat at both scales. Nothing in that reward structure currently distinguishes *how* a Claimant was put down. If a careful dispersal and a maximum-force dispersal clear the board identically, bank the same progress and cost the same time, the system has stated that they are the same act — and it states this every session, while the thesis is stated occasionally.

The campaign is not naive about this and already carries two counterweights: the Mirror reads restraint, and `design/strategy-layer.md` §7 supplies the other directly — *"Prevention beats reaction — Mass falls for things stopped, not things won."* That is exactly the shape of counter-incentive this problem needs.

What is unresolved, stated plainly and left there: the Mirror's measurement is specified in the bible's §6 as a mercy/lethality ratio tracked *from session logs*, and nothing in `data/campaign/config.json` defines what makes an engagement count as mercy; §7's four-option menu is a fiction-level promise with no mechanical difference attached to the options. **Neither is currently listed in `data/campaign/open-questions.json` in any form** — the Bleed and Resonance questions are tracked there; this is not. That is the gap, and it is not resolved here.

**If two outcomes pay the same, the system has said they are the same, and no amount of fiction laid over the top will be heard.**

## 6. Incentive design — a game is about what it pays for

The most common failure in this area is not a contradiction as sharp as §3's. It is a gap: a stated theme that nothing rewards, sitting next to a rewarded behaviour nobody chose as the theme. **[C]** The maxim usually credited to Soren Johnson — given the chance, players will optimise the fun out of a game — is the practitioner form, and its real content is that optimisation is a force to be routed rather than a player defect to be scolded. **[C]** Two consequences follow and they are separate. **What you reward is your actual theme**, not what the pitch says: if the only thing that reliably advances characters is combat, the campaign is about combat. And **warmth and slowness lose by default**, because anything merely permitted competes for table time against anything that pays.

Checked against this campaign, `design/strategy-layer.md`'s BONDS track is a correct application including its reasoning: the bible says the reward is bonds rather than survival or victory, and the response is that *"if that is the reward, it should be a number, or it will be the first thing dropped when a session runs long."* Its stated purpose is enforcement against tone drift toward grimness, so that *"a chapter that generated no Bonds contained no warmth, and that is now visible on the board rather than a thing the GM has to notice."* That is incentive design protecting a register rather than balancing a fight, which is the harder and less common use. The Technician-Days pool in the same section argues about institutions instead, by making one finite weekly pool fund Frame repairs, Frame modifications and every accommodation won for the kids — *"three things competing for one finite resource, one of which is being kind to children."* The scarcity is the argument, and it lands without anyone editorialising about the Directorate.

The gap here is tracked rather than open. `ratio-publication` decides whether the six Resonance percentages are read aloud and ranked weekly, which changes what the incentive argues: from "open up and you will be more capable" to "perform openness for an institution that grades it publicly." `design/resonance-and-bleed.md` §2 calls the published version the cruellest and best use of the mechanic. It remains `status: open` and is not decided here.

## 7. Mechanics as metaphor, and what a number signals

A mechanic is a metaphor when the *procedure* has the shape of the thing it models, so that executing the procedure teaches the fiction. **[C]** The canonical tabletop examples are physical: *Dread* resolves actions by pulling from a Jenga tower, so tension is literally accumulating instability and the game ends when it falls; *Ten Candles* extinguishes a real candle as hope is spent; Blades in the Dark's clocks make approaching danger a visible arc filling in front of the players. In each case the player's hands perform the theme.

Three mechanics here already do this well:

- **Resonance as permission rather than bonus** (`config.json`, `twoTracksPrinciple`). `design/resonance-and-bleed.md` §2 names why it works — advancement becomes emotional rather than numerical, *"you unlock yourself, not your gear."*
- **Damage capping actions on machines but never on kids** (`design/scale-and-chassis.md` §8). A Degraded Frame is capped at 2 actions and a Critical one at 1 regardless of band, while a level-1 kid keeps every action until they drop. Machines and people break differently as a matter of procedure, not description.
- **Bleed learned by testing** (`config.json`, `bleed.visibilityMechanism`). The number is neither shown passively nor hidden — a discretionary slice buys a harmonics evaluation that reveals it and can improve it. The metaphor and the setting's 1950s instrument idiom agree: you go and get a reading, because readings are things you go and get.

The limit is real. **Mechanising a thing makes it a resource, and a resource will be optimised.** An Anchor is a relationship the player wrote; attaching a Resonance delta to affirming or violating it converts that relationship into a lever, and a rational player will start choosing scenes for the delta. `design/resonance-and-bleed.md` §3 caught the sharpest version in Suppression, whose trigger requires the GM to judge *why* a player did something and penalise the motive, and proposed judging pattern rather than intent instead (`suppression-intent-vs-pattern`, `status: open`, not resolved here).

That is the general signal a number sends: it tells the table the thing matters, makes it comparable to other numbered things, and invites optimisation. **[C]** The usual corollary is that anything you do not want optimised should not have a number — right often enough to be a default, and wrong often enough to need its condition stated. §6 endorsed numbering warmth precisely because an unnumbered reward gets dropped when time runs short. Both instructions are correct, and what decides between them is which failure the thing is more exposed to. **Number a thing when its likely failure is neglect; leave it unnumbered when its likely failure is gaming; decide which of the two it is first.**

Warmth fails by neglect, so it gets a track. A Claimant's threat fails by nobody being able to price it, so it gets a stat block. Humour fails by being forced, so it gets none — `ideas/story/blank-inheritance-v4.md` §6 calls humour load-bearing rather than tolerated and lists the comic vectors (the stray, the mitflits, the Watcher's early incompetence, institutional absurdity, the magic show) without attaching a single number to any of them. That is the correct call. One live case sits exactly on the line: `design/npc-social-intel.md` puts each NPC's Attitude on SF2E's real five-step Hostile-to-Helpful scale, and `npc-attitude-player-visibility` asks whether players should ever see the current step. The scale exists either way; showing it converts a read-the-room judgement into a number to be moved. It is `status: open`.

## 8. Penalties cost risk, never choice

`design/campaign-arc.md` records a standing principle issued 2026-09-06: **a drawback should shrink the menu as little as possible and grow the stakes instead.** Its own statement is precise — a permanent slice tax *"makes a character have fewer things to ever choose between, forever — a flat tax with no play in it,"* while reduced personnel-scale HP *"leaves every choice on the table exactly as it was, and makes some of those choices matter more than they used to."*

Read as procedural rhetoric, the two shapes make different claims about the character. **A permanent menu reduction argues that the character is lesser. A raised-stakes penalty argues that the character is in danger.** This campaign wants the second claim, which is why this is worth keeping as a design law rather than a preference.

It sits inside a wider practitioner consensus. Failure design has moved away from outcomes that remove the player's turn and toward outcomes that complicate the situation — the "fail forward" practice associated with Burning Wheel and carried through the Powered by the Apocalypse family, where a miss produces a new problem rather than a stalled action. **[C]** Lost turns and lost options are consistently the least popular punishments in both video and tabletop design, because they punish by removing participation, which is the one thing the player came for. **[C]** SF2E agrees: its conditions are largely graded states a character keeps acting through, which is why `design/downtime-activities.md` §1 can build the whole Exhaustion track out of real ones (Fatigued, then Slowed 1, then Stupefied 1) rather than a bespoke lockout.

The campaign has run this check on itself and recorded the result: Resonance's action bands, Frame damage's action caps and the Exhaustion track are all dynamic, recoverable states rather than permanent reductions, and the one place the wrong shape had shipped — the Day 1 pilot's permanent slice loss — was replaced with reduced personnel-scale HP rather than rescaled (`day1-pilot-slice-penalty-scaling`, `status: resolved`).

One honest tension, named rather than smoothed. Dead Weight sync does reduce the menu: 2 actions and no reaction, per `config.json`. The principle survives because that state is recoverable and is explicitly a failure state rather than a chargen tax — which identifies the real boundary the law draws. It is not "never reduce options." It is **never reduce options permanently, and never as a fixed property of who a character is.** The exact size of the Day 1 pilot's HP reduction remains open (`day1-pilot-hp-reduction-amount`).

## 9. The removability test, and the second thing it tells you

`design/design-layers.md` contributes a test this literature mostly handles by intuition, and contributes it in falsifiable form: **a layer is a real overlay only if you can delete it and still have a playable game.** If you cannot, it is load-bearing, and it belongs further down the stack where it gets validated earlier and more carefully. That document runs the test on its own systems and publishes the results — Resonance and Bleed both pass, and the scale interface deliberately fails, because deleting it leaves a level-1 kid rolling against DC 32. Its nearest external relative is subtractive design as practised commercially: establish what can be removed before adding, because removal is the only reliable test of necessity. **[C]**

The addition this document makes is a second reading of the same test. **Removability tells you whether a layer is structurally load-bearing. Run it again asking what the layer *argues*, and it tells you whether the layer is rhetorically load-bearing, which is a different property.** Delete a system and ask what claim about the world disappeared from the table. If nothing changed in what the game says, the layer is mechanically real and rhetorically empty, and it is costing table time for no meaning. Deleting Bleed removes the claim that using yourself up has a price — a real loss, even though nothing structural collapses.

Deleting the Mirror removes the campaign's thesis outright, because the Mirror is the only system that argues it. **The Mirror appears in no layer of `design/design-layers.md`'s table** — Layer 3 lists Resonance, Bleed, Anchors and Suppression. By the removability test as written it is not an overlay: `ideas/story/blank-inheritance-v4.md` §1 and §4 stop being demonstrable without it. Whether that means it belongs in the layer table, and at which layer, is a real question about the sequencing commitment and is not answered here.

# Narrative Theory Foundations

*Written 2026-09-13. The first of five reference documents on narrative and game design craft — academic narratology, ludology, and TTRPG-specific practice, gathered because this campaign's own design (the v4 bible, the threads, the objectives system) was already reinventing a lot of it independently and it's worth having the theory alongside the instinct. This document covers narrative science proper: how stories work in a reader's or player's head, independent of any game system. The other four are [agency-and-player-choice.md](agency-and-player-choice.md) (choice, storylets, why branching is expensive and acknowledgement is cheap), [ttrpg-campaign-and-session-craft.md](ttrpg-campaign-and-session-craft.md) (table-tested GM technique — fronts, clocks, the Three Clue Rule, prep philosophy), [quests-objectives-and-threads.md](quests-objectives-and-threads.md) (what makes a quest feel authored rather than generated), and [pacing-and-the-responsive-campaign.md](pacing-and-the-responsive-campaign.md) (session and arc rhythm, delayed consequence, the "secret sauce" synthesis). None of this is a decision document — nothing here overrides `campaign-arc.md`, the v4 bible, or any open question in `data/campaign/open-questions.json`. Where it's useful, it's cited against what this campaign already does; where a live design question would benefit from it, it says so without answering it.*

Every claim below is tagged: **[E]** empirical (peer-reviewed or at least tested), **[T]** theory (a scholarly framework — useful vocabulary, not proof), **[C]** craft (practitioner consensus from thousands of tables or shipped games, often righter than theory and never measured).

## 1. Two layers, and why a "compressed novel" is the correct shape for a story bible

Every serious account of narrative since the Russian Formalists splits story into two layers: **fabula** (the events, in causal-chronological order — what actually happened, in order, including everything the audience never sees directly) and **syuzhet** (the discourse — the order, pace, and framing in which it's actually delivered). **[T]**

The v4 bible is written almost entirely in fabula: §18's timeline lays out two hundred years in chronological order, independent of when or whether the party ever learns any of it. The Chapter Grid (§17) and the chapters themselves (§19) are the syuzhet layer — the order of *telling*, which routinely runs backwards through the fabula (Flashback One in Chapter 3 shows something from 200 years ago; the party learns the column exists in Chapter 6 though it's been running for years).

This is exactly correct, and it's worth naming why: **a GM who has fully internalized the fabula can improvise any syuzhet order the table's actual choices demand.** A GM who has only memorized a syuzhet (a fixed telling order) breaks the moment the party asks a question out of the planned sequence. The reason the v4 bible reads as "dense" rather than "a script" is that it's fabula-first — Manako's fall (§10) is nine causally-linked steps that happened in a fixed order regardless of when the party learns about step 3 versus step 7.

The knob this gives a GM, precisely: **order, duration, frequency, mood, and voice** are the only five things discourse actually controls (Genette's formulation). Concretely — order is what a flashback or a foreshadowing note does; duration is how much table-time a thing gets (a summary line versus a full scene); frequency is how often something is told versus how often it happened (the memorial nobody wants, mentioned once, versus the exam timetable continuing "unchanged" across every chapter — a frequency joke); mood is whose eyes a scene is shown through; voice is who's narrating from where. When a scene isn't landing, it's worth asking which of these five knobs is actually wrong, rather than assuming the fabula itself needs to change.

## 2. Causality is the skeleton, and it is measurable

The single most load-bearing empirical result in narrative psychology: Trabasso and van den Broek's **causal network model**. **[E]** Story events fall into six clause categories (Setting, Event, Internal Response, Goal, Attempt, Outcome) linked by causal relations, and two purely structural properties — **membership in the causal chain** from opening to outcome, and **number of causal connections** — predict, with substantial explained variance, which events readers recall immediately, which they recall after a delay, which they include in a summary, and which they rate as important. Events off the chain are forgotten regardless of how vividly they were written.

This has one blunt, useful consequence for prep: **importance is a property of wiring, not of prose quality.** A scene you polish for an hour but that connects to nothing before or after it will be forgotten by the table faster than a plain scene that three later scenes depend on. The v4 bible's own "Ties" section (§2) is, structurally, a causal-connectivity map before any scene exists — Hori↔Oda, Bobbin↔Anabala, Nobu↔Manako are all pre-declared causal links, which means any scene touching one of these relationships inherits connectivity for free. When inventing a new scene at the table, the fastest way to make it land is to route it through an existing tie rather than introducing a fresh, unconnected one.

The corollary that matters for **The Watcher's dossier chain** specifically (§10, "the dossiers... read in sequence they drift"): a sequence of documents is memorable exactly to the degree each one is causally connected to the reveal it's building toward, not to the degree each one is individually well-written. The current design — ex-thugs → cult-susceptible → a chemist → a lawyer → a school governor → a classmate — already is a causal chain (escalating social proximity to the party), which is why it should land. A dossier that broke that escalation (say, a random tangent between the lawyer and the governor) would cost more than its own scene; it would blur the chain's connectivity and make the whole sequence less memorable, not just that one document.

## 3. The five things a table is actually tracking

The **event-indexing model** (Zwaan, Langston & Graesser; extended by Zwaan & Radvansky) holds that comprehenders track ongoing events along **five dimensions simultaneously**: time, space, protagonist/entity, causality, and intentionality (goals). **[E]** Integrating a new piece of information is cheap when it shares indices with what came just before; it's expensive — measurably, in reading-time studies — when an index breaks (a new time, a new place, an unexplained new goal, all at once).

This is the empirical basis for a design instinct the v4 bible already applies without naming it: **the Thread Grid's currency-and-question framing (§16) is an index-labeling system.** Each thread — War, Observed, Cult, Mitflits, Founders, School — carries its own currency (tactical stakes, dread, investigation, found family, complicity, small stakes) and its own question. A scene that clearly belongs to one thread is a scene where four of the five indices (space, entity, causality, intentionality) are already primed by everything else that thread has established; a scene that jumps between threads without a bridge is asking the table to re-index everything at once, which is exactly the discontinuity the model predicts costs comprehension.

The practical GM-facing version: **when a scene feels confusing at the table and you can't tell why, check which index broke.** Did the location change without a transition? Did a new NPC arrive with no stated relationship to anyone present (entity)? Did something happen with no stated cause (causality)? Did a character act with no legible want (intentionality — see [quests-objectives-and-threads.md](quests-objectives-and-threads.md) §1 on this specifically)? Confusion at the table is almost always one of these four, not "the players weren't paying attention."

## 4. Suspense, curiosity, and surprise — the only three engines of narrative interest

Sternberg's account, later tested empirically by Brewer and Lichtenstein, holds that narrative interest reduces to exactly three structures, distinguished by where the audience's knowledge gap sits relative to the events: **[T/E]**

- **Suspense — prospection.** The cause is known, the outcome isn't. *What will happen?*
- **Curiosity — retrospection.** The effect is visible, the cause isn't. *What happened, or why?*
- **Surprise — recognition.** A gap was hidden entirely, then sprung. *That's what it was.*

The same underlying events, restructured to change which of these three the audience experiences, reliably change how much they're liked — this is a tested, not merely theorized, finding.

This maps with unusual precision onto the v4 bible's own stated principle, "**give them the anomaly before the question**" (§16, applied to the Observed and Cult threads): that instruction is a direct implementation of **curiosity as retrospection**. Chapter 2's pre-dated graffiti, the mitflit at the wreckage, the "unexplained new goal" cases above — all of these are effects shown before causes are available, which is precisely the structure that produces sustained curiosity rather than confusion, *provided the table can tell the difference between "I don't understand yet" and "nothing here is trackable."* The five-dimension check in §3 above is exactly the tool for telling those two states apart while writing a scene.

**Surprise is the only one of the three that cannot be sustained** — it costs a setup and pays once, and it degrades on replay or re-explanation. This is worth stating plainly against the campaign's own **Known Risk #3** ("Almost everything good is currently a secret. Chapters 1–2 must work as good mecha episodes and good school drama with none of the reveals helping"): that risk is really the observation that a campaign cannot run on surprise as its primary engine for two chapters, because surprise has no fuel once spent. What Chapters 1–2 are actually running on, correctly, is **suspense** (a Claimant fight with visible stakes, no reveal required) layered with early, small **curiosity** seeds (the graffiti, the anomaly-before-question convention) — surprise is being held in reserve for specific, single-use moments (the Envoy's true nature, the Watcher's benign core) rather than asked to carry the whole opening.

## 5. Gaps, and why partial knowledge is more motivating than none

Loewenstein's information-gap theory: curiosity is aversive arousal from a gap between what's known and what's wanted, and — the counterintuitive part — it *increases* as the gap narrows, not as it widens. **[E]** Three-quarters of an answer is more motivating than a locked door. The Zeigarnik effect (unfinished tasks recalled more readily than finished ones) is related but smaller and more conditional than its popular reputation suggests — treat "unfinished business stays live" as roughly true, not as a lever to pull hard. **[E, with the caveat noted]**

For a campaign built on six parallel threads (§16, v4 bible) that are explicitly *not* meant to resolve one per chapter, this is direct support for the structure as designed: a thread should never sit at zero information for long, and it pays off more as it gets closer to resolution, not merely by existing. The Mass Clock (§15) is a literal, mechanical instantiation of a narrowing gap — a party that can *read the clock* (which the bible specifies happens once they understand the food model, Chapters 5–6) is a party experiencing Loewenstein's curiosity directly, as a number they can watch tighten. This is also the argument for the bible's own instruction to "show it to them. Dread is fine; ambush is not" (§15) — an ambush is a gap sprung with no narrowing phase, which forfeits the whole mechanism.

## 6. Structuralism: what to keep, what to leave alone

**Greimas's actantial model** is the one piece of structuralism worth actively using. **[T]** Six roles on three axes — desire (**Subject → Object**), knowledge (**Sender → Object → Receiver**), power (**Helper / Opponent** around the Subject) — and it is a *relationship* schema, not a sequence, which is exactly why it survives a non-linear, multi-thread campaign where sequence keeps changing but relationships don't.

The v4 bible's "Ties" section (§2) is already, functionally, an actantial map, and naming it as one makes it easier to extend consistently when a new NPC needs a place in the web. Nobu, for instance, resolves cleanly onto the schema: Subject (Nobu) → Object (absolution for his silence) via a Sender (his own guilt) with Helper (the column, laundered funding) and — devastatingly, once revealed — an unwitting Opponent in his own smuggling contacts, whose scheduling turns his "sacred, spontaneous acts of witness" into "somebody else's Tuesday shift schedule" (§10). Any new NPC introduced at the table can be given a fast, legible place in the story by answering just those three questions (what do they want, from whom, and who helps or blocks them) rather than by inventing a full backstory on the spot.

**What to leave alone.** Propp's 31 functions are an ancestor of trope libraries and worth knowing exist, but the *sequence* claim (that stories march through them in a fixed order) is overfitted to one folklore corpus and shouldn't be imported as a template. **Freytag's pyramid** (exposition → rising action → climax → falling action → catastrophe) is routinely misapplied to interactive, multi-session work; a campaign's real tension curve is a repeated build/release sawtooth under a slowly rising floor, not one triangle (see [pacing-and-the-responsive-campaign.md](pacing-and-the-responsive-campaign.md) §3 for the working replacement). **The monomyth** (Campbell/Vogler) assumes a single protagonist on a fixed schedule of inner transformation — this campaign runs six protagonists with six independently-paced arcs (the six threads, the Peril Roster's per-character save conditions), and the monomyth's *sequence* would actively fight that structure. Mine it, if at all, only for individual situations (a threshold, a mentor, an ordeal), never as a spine.

## 7. Scripts and schemas — why genre convention is free compression

Schank and Abelson's **scripts** — stereotyped event sequences a comprehender fills gaps with by default (the restaurant script: you're seated, you order, you eat, you pay) — explain why genre convention is a *compression technology*, not a crutch. **[E/T]** Saying "a magic show" transmits an entire installed set of expectations (patter, spectacle, a mildly disreputable but socially tolerated pastime) in two words; every departure from that installed script is then legible as *meaning* rather than noise.

This is precisely why "**Giant Robo by way of Water Margin**" (§6, register) works as a two-phrase pitch rather than a paragraph: it's loading two scripts (operatic mecha sincerity; an outlaw band more righteous than the state it serves) that the table can draw on immediately, and every scene that plays *against* type — the Watcher losing to its own reflection, the exam timetable continuing through an alien invasion — is legible as comedy or pathos specifically because a script was installed first for it to depart from. A campaign that skipped establishing the register early would have no baseline for those beats to subvert.

The corollary for antagonist design: the Watcher's arc (§4) — "it found the pattern. *Manifest, and they attack*" — is literally a script being *learned* rather than assumed, in-fiction, which is why its behavior can visibly change (the Mirror, §6) without breaking believability. It isn't acting inconsistently; it's updating which script it's running, exactly the way a person's behavior updates as their expectations of a situation change.

## 8. Retrospective coherence — audiences build the story themselves

The least comfortable and most useful finding in this document: **audiences construct causal coherence the material didn't actually contain.** People readily infer intention, causality, and theme from sequences that had none, and the same faculty that produces conspiracy thinking produces the pleasure of figuring out a plot. James Ryan's *curationist* thesis for emergent narrative makes the design point directly: what separates a good emergent-story system from a bad one is mostly whether *someone bothered to select and frame the output.* **[T]**

Two consequences, both already reflected in how this campaign is built:

**The GM is the curator, and that's where narrative actually happens.** Automated or improvised material that arrives without a human selection step reads as noise no matter how well any individual line is written. The v4 bible's §20 feedback loop — "the GM writes up what landed and what didn't after each session… drives the next batch of side plots" — is exactly this discipline in practice: the campaign isn't generating content and hoping it coheres, it's curating what actually happened into what comes next.

**Design for inference, not for delivery.** Two adjacent facts that the party discovers separately — the pre-dated graffiti and the second witness's unprompted corroboration (§17, Chapter 2 and 4) — will be *connected by the players themselves* into a working theory, and that act of connecting is where the pleasure lives. Handing them the connection directly ("the Cult predicted the attack") would remove exactly the thing that makes discovering it worthwhile. The current design already withholds the connective tissue and lets separate facts accumulate — that restraint is the single highest-leverage thing to protect when improvising new clues at the table: give a fact, not a conclusion, and trust the table to do the actual narrative work of connecting it.

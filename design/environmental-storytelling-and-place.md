# Environmental Storytelling and Place

*Written 2026-09-13. Part of the narrative-craft reference series that began with [narrative-theory-foundations.md](narrative-theory-foundations.md). The siblings there cover narrative cognition, choice, GM craft, quest design and pacing — none cover **place**, which does enormous work in this campaign: a real Kobe-derived map, a gazetteer of ranked locations, a fortress island whose architecture is the plot's own history in physical form. This covers Jenkins's four narrative-architecture modes, space as evidence rather than exposition, why players remember rooms better than revelations, spatial rhythm as pacing, storytelling through absence and wear, the lived-in-world detail problem, and — at greatest length, because it is what this table will actually use — how all of it translates for a GM describing a place in prose rather than building it geometrically. **This is not a decision document.** Nothing here overrides `ideas/story/blank-inheritance-v4.md`, `design/campaign-arc.md`, or any entry in `data/campaign/open-questions.json`. Where a claim meets a live open question, it says so and stops.*

*Claims are tagged as in the siblings: **[E]** empirical (tested), **[T]** theory (a scholarly framework — vocabulary, not proof), **[C]** craft (practitioner consensus, often righter than theory and never measured), **[Q]** contested or weak.*

---

## 1. Jenkins's four modes, and the four this campaign already runs

Henry Jenkins's **"Game Design as Narrative Architecture"** (2004) reframed a stale argument about whether games tell stories: designers are not authors of plots but **architects of spaces in which narrative experiences occur.** The four modes are not alternatives — one place can run all four. **[T]**

- **Evocative spaces** borrow meaning from existing genre or cultural memory. The audience arrives already carrying what the place means.
- **Enacted narrative** is story *staged* in space, where the shape of the place shapes the event.
- **Embedded narrative** is information *deposited* in the environment. The story already happened; the space is what is left of it.
- **Emergent narrative** is space built so stories arise in it that nobody authored.

**The rule: name which mode a location is doing before writing a word of it, because the four want completely different prose, and it is the mode rather than the length that decides whether a description lands.** An evocative space wants two words; an embedded one wants specifics with no gloss on them.

**Evocative** is `ideas/story/bible/locations/central-fortification.md`, which states its reference outright — "our universe's equivalent of NERV HQ" — then names its deliberate divergences: NERV's Geofront is a hollow sphere, this is solid rock cut into, and the tone stays Giant Robo, not Evangelion. An evocative space invites gap-filling, and some of those gaps are Ingram's, which is what the same file's guardrail protects: **do not connect the rock to the buried ship or the crater without asking.**

**Enacted** is a deleted draft's climax, kept in `ideas/story/bible/locations/the-gnoll-statue.md` as an idea rather than a plan: a Claimant fights the founder's statue for ninety seconds, then fights its own reflection in the water. That scene cannot be relocated — the comedy, the pathos and the reveal are all produced by geometry. **Embedded** is `data/locations/deep-storage.json`, almost entirely deposited evidence: a lift floor-indicator that skips several numbers without explanation, corridors of numbered doors mostly unmarked, some floors lit and swept and others that "plainly haven't had a visitor in years." Nothing is explained; the building is the record. **Emergent** is `data/locations/main-school-complex.json`'s `consequences` field — anything that happens to a cohort there "moves through every family on the island within the week." Not a plot, but a property of the space that will manufacture plots without an author.

## 2. Evidence, not exposition

The craft predates games; Don Carson's 2000 write-up of Disney Imagineering practice is the usual origin citation, and its core instruction is that the space must carry the story because no narrator is available. **[C]** Games split it into two lineages that fail differently: the **BioShock** lineage puts recordings in the world, a voice explaining what happened here, while the **Gone Home** lineage puts only objects and arrangement there and requires inference. **Dark Souls** goes furthest, into item descriptions and architecture, accepting that many players never assemble it. **[C]**

Which lineage is better is not the useful question. This is: **a space that TELLS has a narrator hiding in it, and a space that is EVIDENCE does not.** The moment a location contains something whose only function is to explain the location, players stop reading and start listening, and every other detail drops in value — the room has admitted it will explain itself if waited out.

**The rule: give the table the artefact, never the conclusion drawn from it.** This is the spatial form of the restraint [narrative-theory-foundations.md](narrative-theory-foundations.md) §8 identifies as the highest-leverage thing to protect when improvising: design for inference, not delivery.

`ideas/story/bible/locations/the-gnoll-statue.md` is why the pattern is worth trusting here. It describes a weathered gnoll posed on guard, sculpted with real effort toward dignity, with a face that would read as foaming at the mouth if the stone were flesh — then instructs, in bold, that **whether that effort succeeds is never resolved in narration and never by an NPC.** A passing NPC may shrug; a setting-wide consensus is forbidden, because the ambiguity is the point. The plaque was first written as worn past reading, with one word, *Hyuck*, as the reward for a real effort; since LEDGER #27 it is legible and simply reads HYUCK, unglossed. The reading mechanism is built and correctly bounded — [`encyclopaedia-checks.md`](encyclopaedia-checks.md)'s optional insight checks, where **failure never costs anything mechanical** and a wrong-but-plausible belief is a fine outcome, itself embedded narrative.

One genuine risk, flagged and left open. `the-gnoll-statue.md` records an unreconciled contradiction: Chapter One §4 calls the statue weathered stone, while the deleted Episode Eight draft and the v4 grid's (since demoted) image of a Frame kneeling in the shallows "still holding the statue's bronze hand" call it bronze. Evidence-based description is exactly the technique that makes an inconsistency expensive, because players are invited to read closely. The file already says to pick one before the statue is used in play, and it is not this document's to pick.

## 3. The memory palace — why place outlasts plot

The **method of loci** is among the best-supported memory techniques there is: information attached to locations along a familiar route is recalled far better than the same information as a list, and training novices produces large, durable gains and measurable changes in memory-network connectivity (Dresler et al., 2017). **[E]** Jenkins himself reaches for the memory-palace image when describing embedded narrative. The useful direction for a GM: **a table will remember where something happened long after forgetting what it meant.** They will not remember which chapter the column was found in. They will remember the causeway.

**The rule: if a fact has to survive the campaign, attach it to a place the party will stand in more than once — and describe that place the same way every time.** A location described richly but differently on each visit is three locations, and the fact hangs on none of them.

This is empirical support for something `design/location-depth.md` already requires on other grounds. Its depth-3 `standing` rung mandates two fields nothing below it has — `scene.history` and `scene.consequences` — justified there as continuity rather than déjà vu, with the failure stated plainly: "A standing location that is identical on the fifth visit is a backdrop, not a location." Its requirement that scene- and standing-depth locations own **their own artwork and their own playlist**, per location and stable, is the multimodal version of the same hook, better read as memory engineering than as polish.

Space is also one of the five indices in the event-indexing model in [narrative-theory-foundations.md](narrative-theory-foundations.md) §3 — comprehension is measurably cheaper when new information shares a spatial index with what came before. **[E]** The related **doorway effect** (crossing a threshold degrading recall) holds in its original laboratory paradigm but replicates unevenly elsewhere; treat it as suggestive, not as a lever. **[E/Q]**

## 4. Absence, wear, and repair

Three of the highest-yield things a place can carry are not objects. **What is missing** — a gap in an otherwise complete row, a number skipped on a panel, a record that stops. **What has been repaired** — the scar of a past failure, which also dates it: something broke, somebody cared enough to fix it, and this is how much they could afford. **What has been worn smooth** — a frequency count, saying how many people have done this and for how long, which no sign in the building would admit. All three are embedded narrative and all three are cheap, because each is one clause implying a duration. **[C]**

**The rule: for any institution with something to hide, write one absence, one repair and one worn surface before writing any of its secrets — the secret should be inferable from the maintenance record before anyone finds a document about it.**

This matters here more than in most campaigns, because the Directorate has things buried: whatever it digs up goes into deep storage and is never spoken of again (LEDGER #40). An organisation in that state does not leave a confession lying around. It leaves maintenance.

`data/locations/deep-storage.json` is the reference implementation. Absence: the lift indicator skipping numbers, and a `gmOnly` note that at least one manifest was never filed at any clearance level at all — "a different and worse problem than a redacted one." Wear and its opposite: floors lit and swept, floors dark until someone finds the switch. Even the siting is an inference about institutional behaviour — putting it under a working cargo apron rather than inside the Directorate's own complex was deliberate, because "nobody audits a warehouse's basement as carefully as they audit a garrison." `data/locations/main-school-complex.json` carries the best repair detail on the map: two institutions merged into one block when the island ran out of room to expand sideways, the join still visible in third-floor brickwork where the floor levels do not line up and **a half-flight of stairs corrects the difference.** A player who trips on those four steps has learned the building's history without being told any of it.

The two strongest absences are deliberately unfilled and neither is available to resolve. `ideas/story/bible/locations/the-rock.md` has nine circular silo doors opening vertically onto cells sized for something Frame-scale, and instructs in bold: **do not decide what's in the nine cells** — with an ordinary carpark serving Central HQ beside them, which the file says should read as juxtaposition, not horror. And v4 bible §5 establishes that somewhere is a room full of what Shucks' detached right hand has written across two centuries: the only unbroken honest record of anything. Where that room is, and what reading it costs, is not established.

## 5. Spatial rhythm is pacing

Level designers have a working vocabulary for tension that does not use plot. **[C]** **Sightlines** determine what a player knows before committing — a long sightline converts an ambush into a decision. **Thresholds** are where the register changes, the cheapest signal that the next space runs on different rules. **Compression and release** is the fundamental rhythm: a tight passage that forfeits information and freedom, opening into a space that returns both. **The breather room** is a space designed to contain nothing, so the next thing can register.

Kevin Lynch's *The Image of the City* (1960) is the closest thing to an empirical foundation here. From residents' sketch maps and interviews he found people build mental images of cities out of five element types — **paths, edges, districts, nodes and landmarks** — and that a city is *legible* to the degree those are distinct. **[E for the finding; C for the prescriptions drawn from it.]** The Kaplans' preference work adds an independent pair: people prefer scenes high in **legibility** (you could find your way) and in **mystery** (going further would reveal more). **[E]** Appleton's prospect-and-refuge thesis is often quoted alongside these and is far weaker evidentially — worth knowing the phrase, not worth designing against. **[Q]**

**The rule: a breather space must be authored on purpose, with its own description, not left to be whatever happens between two fights.**

That is the same instruction [pacing-and-the-responsive-campaign.md](pacing-and-the-responsive-campaign.md) §3 derives from the sawtooth model, where *relax* is a designed state and Known Risk #5 (tone drift toward grimness) is what happens when it stops being scheduled. The spatial version is easier to check in prep: **look at a session's planned locations and ask which one is the room where nothing happens.** If everything on the list has something at stake, the session has no release phase whatever the beat sheet says.

The geography is built for this rhythm. V4 bible §5 gives the city a harbour, a cliff-top old town, a smokestack waterfront, and a causeway out to the sea-fort — distinct districts with hard edges, Lynch's legibility condition met by construction, and in the causeway the best compression-and-release object available: a corridor with no cover and total sightlines both ways, which is what Chapter Six's assault is fought along in the v4 bible's grid (§19, since demoted). That grid runs the same rhythm through one location deliberately. The Main School Complex is an ordinary Tuesday in Chapter One; a memorial assembly nobody wants, with the exam timetable continuing unchanged, in Chapter Two; the front line itself in Chapter Four; a public shelter in Chapter Seven; a graduation in Chapter Eight. One space carries the whole tension curve by changing what it is for, and §3's memory-palace effect is what pays for it.

## 6. The lived-in world problem

Barthes's **reality effect** (1968) names the detail that signifies nothing: the barometer that is not a symbol, advances nothing, and exists purely to assert that this is a real room. **[T]** His point was not that such details are waste — they do their job *by being inconsequential*, and a world in which every object matters reads as a set, not a world.

The failure mode is therefore not texture. It is **texture that has displaced consequence**: five vivid details, an evocative proprietor, a smell, a piece of history, and no answer to "what do the players do here." `design/location-depth.md` already makes this checkable at depth 2 — a scene-depth location "needs what a scene needs: things to **do**, not just things to look at" — and bluntly: "If a location cannot answer 'what do the players actually *do* here', it is a depth-1 sketch with ambitions, not a depth-2 scene."

**The rule: detail is rationed by how often the party will be in the room, not by how interesting the room is to write.**

That ratio is the whole content of the depth ladder: four rungs (`named`, `sketch`, `scene`, `standing`), each with required fields *and* a word ceiling — 70 words at depth 0, 250 at depth 1, 900 at depth 2, uncapped at depth 3 — on the stated grounds that a depth-0 entry "is finished when it is short" and writing more is "not generosity, it is misallocation." Two things about it beat the hobby's norm: the ceilings were **retuned by measurement, not taste** (an initial 40-word depth-0 ceiling flagged 19 of the first 36 real entries, so the ceiling was judged wrong rather than the entries), and **depth is declared before it is written, never inferred from what exists**, which makes it a commission rather than a description.

[`location-rank.md`](location-rank.md) adds the axis depth cannot express, how much *process* a place earns, and the two are explicitly allowed to disagree: "A place seen once that the whole plot turns on is **rank 1, depth sketch**." Rank 1 carries a standing rule that outranks anything in this document: no narrative decision about a pivotal location is made alone, and Claude brings a draft and waits. Central Fortification and The Rock are the two rank-1 locations as of 2026-09-12.

The **footprint gate** aims the same discipline at a failure word counts cannot catch — a declaration wrong because the building is too small to hold what is claimed for it. Floor area caps the ladder (under 30 m² reads as a kiosk; 1,500 m² and up as a complex you can get lost in), with one override: a plan narrower than 6 m past a 3:1 aspect ratio is a wall or a jetty whatever its area, because the 334 m × 4.3 m baggage inspection line has 1,430 m² and nowhere inside it to stand six people in a circle. Like the word ceilings it advises and never blocks.

## 7. Theatre of the mind — all of the above, in prose

This campaign runs mostly without battlemaps. [`theatre-of-the-mind-staging.md`](theatre-of-the-mind-staging.md) settles what a scene *looks like* on screen; what follows is the other half, what the GM says out loud, written as instructions because this is the section most likely to be used at a table.

**The rule: describe three things on entry, no more — and hold a fourth in reserve to answer the first question.** Three is practitioner consensus and consistent with what is known about working memory; a fourth and fifth detail do not add, they overwrite. **[C]** The reserve matters as much as the limit: a GM who spends everything on the opening has nothing left when a player leans in, and leaning in is the behaviour the technique exists to produce.

**Pick the three by function, not by vividness.** One orients — the shape of the space and where the exits are, Lynch's legibility. One is evidence, per §2 and §4: an absence, a repair, a worn surface. One is an affordance: something a player could touch, climb, open or read. Three atmospheric details make a mood, and a mood cannot be acted on.

**Lead with a sense other than sight.** Sight is the sense players supply for themselves; smell and temperature are the ones they cannot. Deep Storage opens its below-ground text exactly this way — the air runs cold and smells of oil and old paper — and that clause does more than a paragraph about the corridor would.

**Describe in the order a person arrives, from the door outward, and give the space one landmark to orbit.** Arrival order hands the table the spatial index from [narrative-theory-foundations.md](narrative-theory-foundations.md) §3 in the cheapest possible form. The landmark solves the other half: theatre of the mind fails when positions are relative to nothing, and it fails silently, with everyone playing in a different room. One named fixture anyone can state their position against is enough, and costs one sentence.

**On a return visit, describe the delta only.** The table already holds the room, and re-describing it in new words overwrites the version they have. `scene.consequences` exists to supply exactly this delta: Deep Storage's says that if the party was caught taking something unsigned, the next visit brings more frequent inventories, a second lock on the lift, and a custodian who stops answering questions he used to answer for free.

**Say what the place lets you do before anyone asks.** Affordances are invisible here by construction — there is no map to scan — so an unstated option does not exist. The depth ladder's requirement of two `scene.activities` at depth 2 and three at depth 3 is, read at the table, a list of things to say out loud on arrival. Deep Storage lists four, one of them explicitly "playing the setting itself — numbered doors, patchy lighting, decades of unsorted paperwork — as a real obstacle."

**Keep a location's vocabulary fixed.** Choose the three or four words that belong to a place and reuse those exact words every time. Bronze or stone. Shed or warehouse. Corridor or hall. The gnoll statue file records what happens otherwise: two passages written months apart made the same statue two different materials, and it is now an open question rather than a description. Synonym variation reads as good prose and behaves as new information.

**Two scales means two descriptions of the same place, and they should not sound alike.** Personnel scale and Frame scale have a prose consequence that is easy to miss: a courtyard is a room at personnel scale and a foothold at Frame scale. Interior detail, surfaces and smells for the kids; approach, footing, sightline and what will take the weight for the Frames. `design/ux-doctrine.md` §7b already makes this split in the interface — **Frame scale is GLASS, personnel scale is PAPER** — and the narration should wear the same two costumes.

**Let a place be funny.** The register is Giant Robo, not Evangelion, and §6 states that humour is load-bearing rather than tolerated, naming institutional absurdity as a built-in comic vector. Environmental comedy lives in exactly the details recommended above — an exam timetable that continues through an alien invasion because cancelling it would require authority nobody has; a carpark beside nine cells sized for something enormous. A room can deliver a joke and a threat in the same three details.

**With nothing prepared, describe purpose, then age, then maintenance.** What the building is for, roughly how old it looks, how well it is being kept. Those three answers generate a serviceable improvised location and — because maintenance is §4's inference channel — one that stays consistent with whatever the institution turns out to be hiding, without deciding anything on the spot.

## 8. The tooling is already an environmental-storytelling instrument

`tools/city-map/` renders a real OpenStreetMap-plus-GSI-DEM map of the actual city, and campaign edits are applied as an append-only operation log rather than hand-drawn. [`map-generation.md`](map-generation.md) §5 argues for that in terms that are exactly an argument about believability: real city data is geometry obeying rules nobody wrote down, a hand-placed road looks wrong because it violates rules the viewer cannot articulate but can absolutely see, so the tool measures those rules from the imported data — class-adjacency, junction spacing, curve radius, setback, frontage width — and requires every generative edit to satisfy them. Building **orientation** relative to the fronting road is called out as "the single most visible tell of generated content," and the invariants checked after each operation apply the battle simulator's instinct to terrain: reject the illegal move rather than trust the author. That is a machine against one specific failure — **a city that stops reading as a place the moment the campaign starts changing it** — and the campaign's own chapters rewrite the map without being allowed to break it.

**The rule: a location's geometry is campaign material, not production metadata — check the real footprint before describing a space, because the map can contradict the prose and it is usually the prose that is wrong.** `tools/location-sync/` makes that checkable: it catalogues locations against the map, enforces the depth contract, and computes the footprint gate, so Central Fortification is not "very large" but 142,224 m², by a wide margin the largest structure drawn so far. The gate exists because of a session in which fifty-one adopted footprints from 24 m² to 16,479 m² were all offered the same blank note field and the same invitation to write, and one of them was a bicycle store with nothing in the tool to say so.

One risk worth naming and not resolving. `general-hospital.json` and `main-school-complex.json` are both adopted from real Kobe footprints whose real-world identities sit in research tags, with explicit instructions that neither may surface in player-facing text. Both entries currently observe that wall well. The risk is structural rather than present: the more locations are adopted this way, the more research tags exist that must never be read aloud, and nothing enforces that boundary the way `core/depth.py` enforces the word ceilings. Whether it needs a check belongs to whoever owns the gazetteer, not to this document.

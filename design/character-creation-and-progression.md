# Character Creation and Progression as Design

*Written 2026-09-13. One of eleven reference documents expanding the narrative-craft library opened by [narrative-theory-foundations.md](narrative-theory-foundations.md), [agency-and-player-choice.md](agency-and-player-choice.md), [ttrpg-campaign-and-session-craft.md](ttrpg-campaign-and-session-craft.md), [quests-objectives-and-threads.md](quests-objectives-and-threads.md) and [pacing-and-the-responsive-campaign.md](pacing-and-the-responsive-campaign.md). This one covers the first thing that happens at a table and the thing that happens between every session after it: how characters get made, what a creation process teaches players about the game, what levelling is for, what a departure costs a campaign, and — at length, because it is this campaign's real situation and the published literature barely touches it — what changes when one player runs two characters at two scales. Not a decision document. Nothing here overrides the v4 bible, [campaign-arc.md](campaign-arc.md), or any entry in `data/campaign/open-questions.json`; live questions are named and left open. A hard constraint observed throughout: the six kids' and Frames' names, classes and Anchors are collaborative work with the project owner, and nothing below proposes any of them.*

Every claim is tagged: **[E]** empirical (measured, usually in another medium — the transfer is stated), **[T]** theory, **[C]** craft (practitioner consensus from thousands of tables and shipped games, often righter than theory and never measured), **[Q]** contested.

## 1. Character creation is the first act of play, not preparation for it

The order in which a system asks its questions is its first statement about what the game is about, and players believe it. **[C]** D&D asks for a class and six ability scores, and the table correctly concludes the game is about capability under conflict. Apocalypse World asks how well you already know each other before it asks what you can do. Burning Wheel asks for three Beliefs and then tells the GM to attack them. Traveller asks what you did for four years at a time and, in its earliest edition, whether you survived it — creation with an outcome the player does not control is already play.

**The rule: whatever a creation process asks for first is what the table will believe the campaign values, regardless of what the pitch document says.** Asking for a combat build first says something different from asking who you owe money to, and no amount of tone-setting prose in session one overwrites it.

This campaign takes the strongest available position, recorded rather than implied. `design/induction.md` §4 opens: *"Character creation, run in fiction, as a scene."* The in-fiction intake and the out-of-fiction chargen are the same act. The v4 bible reached it from the other side — §3 says *"the mandatory DNA screen at character creation is the Directorate checking a city's children for exactly this and calling it a health check."* The screen that sorts the party onto a Frame-capable track is both a scene in Act I and the moment the player's ancestry choice matters mechanically.

Two consequences do work no later scene can. The **terminal lockdown** (`induction.md` §4): a non-terrestrial ancestry trips a screen, the form stops and asks with bureaucratic patience how the character went undetected, on that player's screen alone so the reveal stays theirs — and *"the Directorate now has a record, which is the founding sin operating exactly as designed."* The institutional flaw at the campaign's centre is demonstrated during chargen, before any NPC explains it. The **medical** (§3): Shucks Mordant already knows what each candidate is, so *"there is no check to pass and no way to hide it, and that is the point of the scene rather than a problem with it."*

**One thing to flag and not resolve.** `design/induction-terminal.md` §4 puts the eight narrative questions on Page 8 of 9, after ancestry, background, class, boosts and portrait, and states that *"nothing is required — leaving every box blank and continuing is a real, supported path."* Optional is defensible alone; last is defensible alone. The combination deserves a decision, because the campaign's highest-leverage input (§3) sits where a tired player is likeliest to skip it, and skipping is explicitly supported.

## 2. The authored/blank-slate spectrum, and what each end buys

Creation processes sit on one axis, and the trade along it is consistent. **[C]** **Pre-generated characters** give maximum prep leverage and cost ownership: the player performs someone else's character, and the gap widens over a long campaign. **Lifepath systems** (Traveller, Burning Wheel, Cyberpunk, WFRP careers) generate a history as a by-product of mechanical choices, so the player ends with a past they did not plan and therefore find interesting; they cost table time and can produce a character the player did not want. **Point-buy and class-and-level** give complete mechanical ownership and no narrative prompt, and the cost is silent — a player who volunteers no hook leaves the GM nothing to schedule and the system never notices. **Zero-session collaborative creation** gives the highest ownership and the best cohesion, because characters are made in one room and can reference each other, and costs a session everyone has to attend.

[E, transferred] The IKEA effect (Norton, Mochon & Ariely) — people value what they assembled themselves above identical pre-made objects — is the measured version of why players defend characters they wrote. Measured on furniture and origami; the direction transfers, the magnitude is unknown.

**The rule: authored characters buy GM leverage, player-authored characters buy ownership, and no process gets both at full strength — so decide which one the table is short of and buy that.**

SF2E is class-and-level, so the correct fix is a lifepath-shaped overlay bolted on top, which is what the eight intake questions are. `induction-terminal.md` §4 shows the hybrid built: Pages 2–5 are real SF2E mechanics (all 51 ancestries, all 154 backgrounds, all 6 classes, four free boosts, resolved against real compendium data), Page 8 is the bible's question set verbatim.

**The roster's actual state, checked rather than assumed.** `data/characters/kids/` and `data/characters/frames/` each hold six JSON files dated 2026-08-22. They are drafts — one Frame file's `notes` opens *"DRAFT — proposed 2026-08-22 at Ingram's direct request, open to full revision"* and grounds its numbers in GM Core's real level-12 tables. Both directory READMEs still say none of the six exist, so **the READMEs are stale relative to their own directories** and anyone consulting them gets a wrong answer about project state. What they say about *policy* remains correct and is repeated in CLAUDE.md: those files are a proposal in pregen shape, not the roster, and the roster is the user's to make with the players.

The same dial is a live question. `frame-class-assignment` (open) asks whether a Frame's class is a psych-profile *verdict* the kid did not choose or a player selection — the verdict characterises the institution, the selection keeps the build with the player. Its notes record a larger answer that does not settle it: Frame builds, the machine included, are not fixed at creation and can be renegotiated in-fiction across the campaign.

## 3. The sheet as a content request — flags, bonds, beliefs and drives

A family of mechanisms looks like characterisation prompts and is not: Burning Wheel's Beliefs and Instincts, Dungeon World's Bonds, Apocalypse World's Hx, Fate's aspects and compels, 13th Age's One Unique Thing, Blades in the Dark's vice and friends/rivals list, and — in the PF2E/SF2E line this campaign runs on — the background, which attaches mechanical grants to a narrative claim about your past.

What they do is solve the GM's hardest problem, which is never inventing content but inventing content *this specific table wants*. **A flag is a message from a player to the GM saying: put this in the game.** The sheet is a request form. **[C]**

Four corollaries, all consensus and none measured **[C]**: a flag must be **specific enough to schedule** ("I want to be a hero" is not one, "my sister works for the people we just arrested" is); it must be **answerable within a few sessions** or it is decoration both parties quietly forget; **unused flags teach players to stop writing them**, because a form collected and never cashed trains the table that it was a formality; and **the GM owes an answer, not obedience** — a flag says what to put in, never what happens once it is in.

### The intake quiz, worked through

v4 bible §20 lists eight questions for session zero and states that *"every answer becomes an Anchor, a side plot seed, or a vulnerability. Written down, kept, and mined for the whole campaign."* `induction-terminal.md` §1 reproduces them and adds *"do not invent new ones."* Read as a flag system, it is unusually well built:

1. **Who do you love, and what would you never tell them?** A bond and a secret in one sentence. The craft is the pairing — the secret is attached to a person, so the scene exists the moment those two are in a room. "What is your secret" alone produces backstory nobody can schedule.
2. **What are you good at that has nothing to do with piloting?** The only question with a direct mechanical destination: the bible annotates it as ground-role affinity, landing on `data/campaign/ground-roles.json`'s five roles. It also states a value out loud — competence outside the headline activity is worth writing down.
3. **What did you want to be, before the draft notice?** A counterfactual life. Sacrifice is legible only against what was given up, and this supplies the measure.
4. **Who in this city owes you something? Who do you owe?** The most quest-generative of the eight: an obligation arrives with a second party and a deadline already attached.
5. **What is the smallest thing that would make today a good day?** The bible calls it *"the cheapest, most reliable emotional lever in the campaign."* The structural point is rarer — this is a **reward** flag, and almost every published system in this family collects only trouble. Beliefs get attacked, aspects get compelled, and nothing else asks the player how to be kind cheaply. Against a tone directive of Giant Robo warmth, and §20's line that solo side plots are *"where the campaign earns the right to hurt later,"* that is load-bearing rather than soft.
6. **What do you not want the Directorate to know about you?** A vulnerability aimed at the campaign's own institution, whose founding sin is information suppression. It interlocks with two systems designed separately: `induction.md` §2's disguise policy, advising that alien origin stay with close friends and *"presented as protection, and it is partly sincere"*; and §4's lockdown, which may already have filed the answer.
7. **Name someone who thinks you are better than you are.** An external witness — structurally a Belief pointed backwards. It hands the GM a character whose disappointment costs something, the cheapest non-violent stake available.
8. **What do you find funny?** The only question addressed to the player rather than the character. It is a register request, and against v4 §22's Known Risk #5 — tone drift, where *"no jokes, no warmth, and no small good days... is a fault, not a mood"* — a per-player register channel is worth more than it looks.

Notice what the eight do not ask: nothing about combat capability, nothing about what the character is good at in a fight, nothing about ambition expressed as power. Per §1, that ordering is the campaign's thesis statement.

**One schema decision deserves specific praise.** `data/schema/kid.schema.json` keeps `intake` (with `banal` and `questions` arrays) separate from `anchors`, capped at three and typed person/belief/need, and says why: *"raw answers, not yet distilled into the capped-at-3 Anchors, which stay collaborative work with the real player."* **Keep the raw content request and the mechanical hook in separate fields, because the cap on the hook must never delete the request.** Most systems in this family get that wrong — they collect three Beliefs and discard everything the player said while writing them.

### Three gaps, named and left open

- **The answers cannot currently reach the people who need them.** `induction-terminal.md` §6 point 5: the terminal can capture the eight answers into the Actor, but *"the harvest/inbound sync mechanism this depends on does not exist yet"* — issue #140's own audit found only a docstring. The best player-to-GM channel in the design has no delivery pipe at the far end.
- **Nothing marks an answer as used.** `kid.schema.json`'s `intake.questions` entries hold an id and an answer and no status; there is no cadence for revisiting them, and §20's feedback loop runs off the GM's post-session notes instead. Every flag system's failure mode is flags never cashed, and the data cannot say which those are.
- **Two of the eight carry premises the campaign has since overturned.** Q3 asks about *"before the draft notice"* and Q4 who *"in this city"* owes you — but `induction.md` §1, settled 2026-09-09, states the party are *"volunteers, not conscripts"* and that *"every PC is new to the city."* Both still have honest readings (an acceptance letter for a notice; the city they came from, or obligations about to be acquired), and the terminal ships the text verbatim by instruction. A wording collision between two documents each internally right; which way it resolves is the user's call.

Read alongside [agency-and-player-choice.md](agency-and-player-choice.md) §6, which analyses the same quiz as storylet seeding — what the answers become once they enter the content graph. This section is about the channel itself.

## 4. Why is this party together

Six players make six characters and then have to explain why those six travel together. The standard solutions **[C]**: a **shared origin**; a **shared obligation**; **pre-existing relationships mapped at the table** (Dungeon World Bonds, Apocalypse World Hx, Blades' crew sheet); or a **shared asset everyone co-owns** (Ars Magica's covenant, a ship, a crew).

**The rule: solve cohesion before the first session or spend the first three sessions solving it live — those are the only two options, and only one of them is a decision.**

This campaign solves it three times over, and the third way is the interesting one. **Shared origin**: everyone is new and everyone was summoned (`induction.md` §1), so nobody is the local who must be talked into caring. **Shared obligation**: they volunteered, were screened, and were streamed onto one course together — the dormitory beat is named in the document itself (§5, Act I step 6), *"six unfamiliar beds and somebody has already taken the good one. This is where they stop being six players and start being a party."* **A shared secret they work out themselves**: Act I step 14 is *"they compare notes. Everyone in this section came back irregular"* — awarded as an accomplishment and described as *"the first thing they work out without being told."* **A cohesion fact the party derives is worth more than one they are assigned**, because deriving it is the first thing they did as a group.

What the campaign deliberately cannot do is map pre-existing PC-to-PC relationships, because six strangers cannot have Bonds. That is a real cost: all six relationships must be built in play, and Act I carries the whole load. The hedge is §20's **Open** side plot type — *"the whole group can attend... these build the found family, which is the campaign's actual reward."* The risk is compression. If Act I is shortened for pace, the party reaches Chapter Two as six colleagues with a shared employer, and the theme the campaign names as its own reward has nothing under it yet. The eight questions ask only about people outside the party, which follows from the premise rather than being an oversight, and `induction-terminal.md` §1 forbids adding questions — so this is not a gap to patch, but a consequence to hold in view when scheduling Act I.

## 5. What levelling is actually for — three jobs that get confused

Levelling does three separable things, and most disappointment with a progression system comes from a designer delivering one while a player expected another. **[C]**

1. **Capability.** The numbers rise. This feels good briefly and, in a bounded-accuracy system, mostly keeps pace with the opposition.
2. **Options.** The menu lengthens. This is the one that changes play, because it changes what the player decides between on their turn.
3. **An arc marker.** A statement in the fiction that this character is not who they were. What players remember years later, and the job systems most often forget entirely.

**The rule: a level-up that raises numbers without adding a decision is felt as maintenance, not progress.** SF2E gives both kinds; the levels that land at the table are the feat levels.

[E, transferred] Self-determination theory work on digital games (Ryan, Rigby & Przybylski) finds need satisfaction — autonomy and competence especially — predicts enjoyment better than content does. Options feed autonomy, capability feeds competence, and a track delivering only one under-serves a measured need. Measured in short digital sessions, not campaigns; a direction, not a number.

The campaign's main track is milestone, and `campaign-arc.md` states the gate: *"a significant defeat of a Claimant, followed by enough downtime for the pilots' Frames to actually be refit/upgraded. Leveling is earned by surviving and recovering, not just by fighting."* **That gate is the arc-marker function made mechanical**, the rarest of the three to get right — tying advancement to a recovery period guarantees the fiction always has somewhere to put the change, instead of a level arriving mid-corridor because a counter filled.

The second track is better still. `data/campaign/config.json`'s `twoTracksPrinciple`: *"Neither ever produces a numeric combat bonus larger than +2 — the difference between a well-synced and poorly-synced pilot is what they're allowed to do, not how good they are at doing it."* That is job 2 chosen over job 1 in the campaign's own words, and `frame.schema.json`'s `abilityBands` is the machinery — one full level-12 statblock, sorted into four Resonance bands, with the instruction that *"a Dead Weight Frame should still be competent; sort generously rather than dumping everything into the top band."*

**The band-spread finding, connected and not reopened.** `design/resonance-and-bleed.md` §2 ran arithmetic nobody had run: total actions across a fight are 7 / 12 / 24 / 40 from Dead Weight to Absolute — *"a wider gap than four character levels in SF2E — expressed on one track, inside one tier, between two players at the same table."* `resonance-band-spread` is marked **resolved** (2026-09-06), addressed by the action-economy change rather than by capping who deploys together: with three actions from Nominal upward the normal operating range compresses from 3.3x to 2.2x, while Dead Weight to Absolute stays 5.7x and is now correct, because Dead Weight is explicitly the failure state. The theory worth attaching, without reopening it: **a track that varies how many actions a player takes is not varying capability, it is varying participation**, which is a far more expensive currency than a bonus. That is why the split is the right shape — a failure state may cost participation, a normal operating range must not. What it leaves live is not arithmetic but table experience: what a Dead Weight session feels like to sit through. The adjacent open question is `ratio-publication`, which decides whether that state is private or read aloud and ranked.

**The penalty corollary, settled here already.** `campaign-arc.md`'s principle — penalties cost risk, never choice — produced a clean worked example. The Day 1 pilot's disadvantage was a permanent loss of one discretionary slice per day, a straight menu reduction. `day1-pilot-slice-penalty-scaling` resolved it not by rescaling but by changing shape: reduced starting Hit Points at personnel scale, which *"costs nothing in a Frame (full Resonance, full actions, full slice choice); everything the moment they are not in one."* **Progression should add options; penalties should not remove them.** The same rule read in both directions.

## 6. Trap options, and the narrative cost of a bad choice

The mechanical side of build space — dominant options, dead options, the maths of a choice that looks equal and is not — belongs to [rpg-systems-design.md](rpg-systems-design.md). What that document will not cover is the narrative cost, which is the expensive part at a table.

A player who discovers at level 6 that their level-1 choice was a trap has three exits, all costly. **Retcon**, and the character's history changes for a reason outside the fiction. **Rebuild**, and the character changes who they are with no story cause. **Keep playing**, and the character's defining trait is a mistake the *player* made, not one the character made. **[C]**

**The rule: the cost of a trap option is not the lost damage, it is that the character's defining choice stops belonging to the character.**

Three mitigations exist in practice **[C]**: publish the trap at the point of choice; allow retraining and give it a fiction, so a rebuild is a scene rather than an apology; or design so nothing is a trap, which is expensive and rarely achieved. This campaign has the two usable ones. **Publishing at the point of choice** is built into the induction terminal — every mechanical page carries an out-of-character help layer in a mechanical register, Page 5's reading *"this permanently raises one of six numbers that drive almost every roll you make,"* which the document calls the single most consequential page. **A fiction for rebuilding** is recorded in `frame-class-assignment`'s notes: Frame builds, the machine included, can be swapped or upgraded across the campaign through an in-universe correspondence and negotiation system, balanced case by case. That costs the story nothing, because the in-world requisition process *is* the retraining rule.

**One gap, named and left.** The campaign invents a build decision SF2E does not have and nothing validates: the `abilityBands` sort. Which of a Frame's level-12 abilities sit in Dead Weight, Attuned, Resonant or Absolute is a real choice with real consequences; `config.json` says it happens *"at chargen"*; `frame.schema.json` gives one instruction about generosity; and the repo does not state **who makes the sort** — player, GM, or whatever process decides class — or what a bad sort costs. A badly sorted Frame is a trap option discovered several sessions later, with all three bad exits above and no published guidance for spotting it in advance.

## 7. Death, retirement, and replacement

When a PC leaves a campaign, three things break, in ascending order of expense. **[C]** The **threads attached to that character** stop, and those flags are content already scheduled. The **party composition** changes and a role is empty. And the **replacement faces an entry problem**: a new character arrives with no shared history, and the table's accumulated investment does not transfer. The standard answers: let the replacement inherit the thread (a sibling, partner, heir, student); build a recruitment fiction into the premise so arrivals need no explanation; or take PC death off the table deliberately and put the stakes elsewhere.

**The rule: decide before session one whether a PC can die, because the answer determines where the campaign is allowed to put its stakes.**

This campaign has decided, in data rather than prose, and took the third option. `config.json`'s `frameEconomy.permanentLoss`: *"PC death is never the cost mechanism at either scale."* The cost lands on machines — a Frame that reaches Hulked can stay Hulked, *"permanent loss of a personal Frame is on the table from the start (foreshadow it early, so when it happens it reads as the campaign keeping a promise, not GM cruelty),"* and the same now applies to vehicles. The NPC side is symmetrical: the Peril Roster (v4 §21) gives eight named NPCs a trigger and a save condition and states *"nobody on this list is scheduled to die... manufactured jeopardy is fine and encouraged... what is not fine is deciding the outcome in advance."* Jeopardy sits with the NPCs, where a campaign can afford to spend it. The recruitment fiction comes free: `induction.md` §1 has several hundred candidates screened every semester and a flagged group streamed onto the track together, so a replacement PC needs no special explanation.

**The gap.** The most common real reason a PC leaves a six-player campaign is that a *player* leaves the table, and nothing in the repo covers what then happens to that character's Frame, ground role, Anchors, intake answers or threads. This campaign is unusually exposed, because one departure removes two PCs, a Contact rotation seat and a ground-role lens at once. Naming it, not solving it.

## 8. The dual-character problem, part one: attention and identity

This is the campaign's actual design situation: **six players, each running a level-1 kid and a level-12 Frame simultaneously.** Published design literature barely covers it. The nearest analogues **[C]**: **Ars Magica's troupe play**, where one player runs a magus, a companion and grogs at wildly different tiers and the answer is rotation plus a covenant that persists across all of them; **Lancer**, a small pilot sheet and a large mech sheet as deliberately different subsystems; **older mecha and vehicle games**, where the machine is equipment and the character is the only character; and **Blades in the Dark's crew sheet**, a second sheet everybody co-owns. Structurally this campaign is nearest Lancer, and differs decisively: here the Frame is neither equipment nor a shared asset.

### (a) Attention economy — which sheet is live

**The rule: at any moment exactly one of a player's two sheets should be the one being rolled, and the table should be able to tell which without asking.**

The rules layer answers this by construction. `design/scale-and-chassis.md` §2 states the principle — *"A personnel-scale number never appears on the same side of a comparison as a Frame-scale number"* — and §3 permits exactly three conversions: **be crew** (inside a chassis, the chassis's numbers roll, and you are not a target); **a Ground Role check at your own level's DC granting a Frame-scale effect** (you roll DC 15 because you are level 1, the effect lands at level 12); and **a clock** in place of a save you mathematically cannot make. Even while a kid acts into a Frame-scale fight, the kid's own sheet rolls, at kid DCs. One sheet is live at a time because nothing else is legal on the map.

The one deliberate crossing is the Push: *"this is the one place the level-1 sheet does direct, real work inside a level-12 scene"* (`config.json`). A single named, documented exception is good design; what to watch is exceptions accumulating, because two more and the principle stops being one. The interface layer then answers the same question independently — `design/ux-doctrine.md` §7b makes **Frame scale GLASS and personnel scale PAPER**, two registers already in the stylesheet, chosen partly so the scales *"cannot appear together."* The player sees which sheet is live before anyone narrates it. **Two independent layers enforcing the same separation is the right amount of redundancy for the campaign's hardest structural problem.**

### (b) Identity — is the Frame a character or equipment

**The rule: whether a machine is a character is decided by what the system lets it want, not by how much prose is written about it.**

The repo answers in three places, with three emphases, and the differences matter.

**The data says character.** `data/schema/kid.schema.json` and `data/schema/frame.schema.json` both `allOf`-reference the same `common/sf2e-common.schema.json#/$defs/pcStatblock`, and frame.schema.json's own description reads: *"A player's mech: a full, standard SF2E PC... pick a class, build it exactly as any PC of the same level — reflavored at the fiction layer."* A Frame is not a vehicle stat block here; vehicles exist separately in `scale-and-chassis.md` as objects with Hardness and a Broken Threshold. **Spending a full PC sheet on the machine is a design statement, and the strongest one available in this repo's own vocabulary.**

**The fiction says more than character.** v4 bible §3: each Frame is *"a biomechanical core at full mitflit scale — a living, motivating body — inside a terrestrial steel exoskeleton"*; the cavity *"is not a fixed shape somebody measured. It is a response"*; autonomous mode fails only because the communication technology it needs is a *language* nobody but the mitflits has; direct control *"is a violation"*; **Bleed *"is resistance. The pilot is suppressing a person. The person pushes back"***; and berserk is *"uncontrolled mercy"* — the core winning, dropping its weapon, refusing to finish something already beaten. On that reading the player runs two PCs and, inside one of them, an NPC they are suppressing.

**The interface says seats.** `ux-doctrine.md` §12 records: *"Kids and Frames are interchangeable seats, not different characters."* That line is answering the attention-economy question — which console is live, and how to keep both seats attractive when Frames are strong and slow and vehicles fast and fragile — not the identity question. Worth knowing which question it answers, because it is the one sentence in the repo that, quoted alone, contradicts both the schema and the bible.

**The gap.** If a Frame contains a person, there is nowhere to write that person down. `frame.schema.json` has `concept` for reflavouring, plus `abilityBands`, `damageState`, `repairLog` and `customizations` — and no field for the core's own wants, history or attitude, even though `npc.schema.json` gained a `socialProfile` for exactly that kind of tracking (`design/npc-social-intel.md`). Meanwhile berserk is the core acting on its own preferences, and `config.json`'s narration rule says *"do not narrate this from the cockpit — the player doesn't get to watch it happen to them."* The campaign's most distinctive fictional claim about its Frames has no data representation. Naming it and stopping: the six cores are exactly the kind of thing this repo forbids inventing on the user's behalf.

## 9. The dual-character problem, part two: coupling, the consolation prize, and partial loss

### (c) Progression coupling

Three general options for advancing two linked sheets **[C]**: **lockstep** (one advancement event moves both — simple, and it quietly makes the second sheet feel like a stat block rather than a character); **independent** (two economies, maximum expressiveness, and the encounter maths breaks the moment they diverge far); and **coupled-with-drift** (independent earning inside a bounded divergence).

**The rule: couple two sheets loosely enough that investment shows and tightly enough that the maths survives, and write the bound down as a number.**

This campaign chose the third and wrote the number. `config.json`'s `frameEconomy.upgradePoints`: points earned from downtime activities *and* from winning combat at both scales; the pool **individual per pilot** rather than shared, *"because each pilot has their own repair crew"*; combat awards guaranteeing a floor so *"a Frame can never be stranded at level 12 by a player who spends their downtime elsewhere"*; and `levelDrift` a *"hard cap of ONE level either way from the pilot's own level."* Over-investment is punished through existing systems rather than a new rule: hangar slices are slices not spent resting, so Exhaustion climbs, Bleed goes unmanaged for want of a Harmonics Evaluation, and untouched Anchors invite Suppression. The specific thing that is good: **the drift band makes the difference between two pilots characterising rather than merely numerical.** A player who lives in the hangar ends with a better Frame and worse rest, both visible in play, without the divergence becoming an encounter-maths problem or a dominant strategy. That is the entire job of a coupling rule, done.

Two things to hold in view. `design/design-layers.md` puts the campaign economy at Layer 2 and Resonance/Bleed/Anchors at Layer 3, so coupling must be validated *before* Resonance is allowed to change what a pilot may do — worth stating because at the table the two feel like one system and are not. And `frame-upgrade-economy` is still open on the real parameters. The number of clocks one player carries — kid level, Frame level, upgrade points, technician-days, repair-crew standing, Resonance, Bleed, Exhaustion, four slices a day — is exactly where v4 §22's Known Risk #1 lands: *"the threat is not a weak story — it is the apparatus collapsing under its own weight. Decide which two subsystems get cut first under pressure."* A progression system a player cannot hold in their head stops being experienced as progression.

### (d) The consolation prize

Any session that foregrounds one scale risks making the other sheet feel like the point, and the player on it a spectator with paperwork. Here it is structural rather than occasional: the opening arc fields one Frame. `induction.md` §5, Act V: *"one Frame launches, five ground roles live."* Five of six players spend the chapter's climax at personnel scale while one is in a giant robot. The bible names the risk in its own words and answers it — v4 §13: *"each ground role owns a permanent piece of the mystery, which fixes the risk of non-piloting kids feeling like a consolation prize."*

**The rule, stated generally: a secondary role stops being a consolation prize the moment it exclusively owns something the primary role cannot get — and information is the cheapest such thing to give it.**

§13 does exactly that, with plot-critical discoveries rather than flavour, none of them available from a cockpit. Umbilical Control is *"the one who finds the column"*, because whoever manages municipal power load notices power going somewhere unaccounted for. Evacuation Marshal *"spots the demonstration/heist timing correlation."* Signals Liaison is *"the one who hears a mitflit say the Observer in that flat technical way and notices nobody in the room is frightened."* Structural Command learns apertures open where the ground has been watched; Sensor Operator first notices where attention pools before things appear there. `ground-roles.json` then makes the roles mechanically real rather than compensatory: Sensor Operator's successes are true facts the pilot can act on, Umbilical Control converts effort into a saved internal-power round — the exact resource the Resonance bands meter — Structural Command changes the map mid-fight, and Evacuation Marshal's failures become next episode's Resonance penalties. The strongest single item is Signals Liaison, which *"reduces Bleed, or holds a slipping Resonance band"* and is *"strongest when they share an Anchor with their pilot"* — a ground player's own relationship doing load-bearing mechanical work inside someone else's fight.

**One tension to flag, because three files say different things.** v4 §13 says each ground role owns a **permanent** piece of the mystery. `ground-roles.json` says *"rotate roles fight to fight. Nobody should play the same one twice in a row once the roster settles."* And v4 §20's second intake question produces a per-kid *ground-role affinity*. If the lens is permanent to the **role**, rotation moves the discoveries between players and no player owns a lens, which weakens the consolation-prize fix the lens was written to deliver. If the lens is permanent to the **kid**, rotation contradicts it and so does the affinity question. Both are workable and they are different designs; `kid.schema.json`'s `groundRoleHistory` array exists specifically to enforce rotation, so the data currently backs rotation. Naming it and stopping.

### (e) Partial loss — the sub-problem the structure creates by itself

A Hulked Frame removes one of a player's two PCs and leaves the other standing. That is the only PC-scale loss the campaign permits (§7), and **the dual-PC structure is what makes it survivable**: that player still has a kid, a ground role, a rotation seat and every thread attached to them. Worth stating as an argument *for* the structure rather than a cost of it — running two PCs is what lets this campaign put a permanent PC-scale loss on the table without removing anybody from the session. `permanent-hulk-commitment` (open) asks whether at least one such loss is a planned beat or left to emerge, which is §7's retirement question in Frame clothing.

The design move worth copying out of this campaign into anything else with two sheets is the Day 1 pilot's resolved disadvantage: reduced starting HP at personnel scale, which *"costs nothing in a Frame... everything the moment they are not in one."* **The best dual-character mechanics put the cause on one sheet and the cost on the other**, because that is the only kind of rule that makes a player care about both sheets at once.

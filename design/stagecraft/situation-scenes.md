# Situation scenes: the third kind

*Drafted 2026-10-01 at Ingram's request. **A proposal**: the method and the tools wait for her calls (§10).
Companions: [`scene-standard.md`](scene-standard.md) (group F is this document's checklist) and
[`stage.md`](stage.md) (the zones and blueprint a situation scene is played on). The GM's view is mocked up
as plate 10 in [`lab/`](lab/).*

---

## 0. In short

A **situation scene** is a problem with no script: a goal, some places, people who want things, and pressure. The
table decides how to tackle it. To make that runnable in theatre of the mind:

1. **Prepare the situation, not a plot**: a map of places, people with wants, the things that can be found, and a
   clock (§4).
2. **Prepare for the likely moves**: for every place, what happens if the table talks, looks, uses what they know,
   goes elsewhere, sneaks, tricks, forces, fixes, waits, splits up, helps each other or walks away. Each answer is
   one line with its roll and its four outcomes (§4.5). This is preparation, never prediction (§3).
3. **Make the rest cheap**: when the table does something nobody prepared for, the GM has a one-press way to price
   it, play it and record it (§6), and Claude writes it in afterwards (§7).

At the table, the Runner shows a situation scene as **a map first**: where the party is, what is there, and the
prepared moves for that place, filtered by the kind of move a player just described (§6).

---

## 1. The brief, in Ingram's words

> "I want you to give some thought about how it might be possible to aid the GM in creating good theatre of the mind
> scenes that need more complexity to work story wise. Some scenes can be simple and benefit from being shorter and
> simpler. Some scenes are designed to be flexible, open ended and because they have no solid goal they become great
> places for RP. I need your help with the third kind, a non-combat scene where the descriptions and the unfolding of
> the narrative has to happen by giving the players options for what to do or perhaps even better trying to predict
> what the players might do and plan for all the most likely cases."

---

## 2. Three kinds of scene, and why the third is hard

| | Short | Open | **Situation** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Something has to happen | yes | no | **yes** |
| The table chooses how | within one job | entirely | **entirely** |
| The GM prepares | the beats | a menu | **a map, people, pressure and the likely moves** |
| Example | The Walk Down | a haunt | The Errand, Intake Medicals |

Short scenes are carried by their beats; open scenes by their deck and their regulars (`haunts.md`). A situation scene
has neither crutch. In theatre of the mind the GM has to hold, live: where everyone is, what each place contains,
what each person wants and will do, how much time is left, which clues have been found and how else they could be,
and what any move the table invents should cost. All while describing. That is too much to hold in one head under
pressure, so the scene has to hold it.

---

## 3. Prepare for moves; never predict the table

Ingram's standing rule: never write what the table feels, concludes or will do. A likely-moves list honours it
because of how it is written:

- **Every entry begins "If they…"**: a possibility, never a forecast. "If they lie about why they're here", not
  "they will try to lie".
- **It prepares responses, it doesn't steer.** Nothing in it pushes the table toward an approach
  (`feedback_dont_prescribe_approach`): it makes sure that whichever approach they take, the GM has an answer.
- **Feelings stay out.** It says what the place and the people do. It never says how the table reacts.

The rest of the craft is already in the library and is applied, not re-invented: prep situations, not plots;
the Three Clue Rule; clocks that only move for a reason in the fiction; failing forward; beat reciprocity (people ask
as well as give); and the pacing sawtooth (a situation scene still needs its quiet beats).

## 4. What a situation scene contains

### 4.1 The problem

- **Goal**: what the party is trying to do, in one sentence.
- **The floor**: what is true however it goes, so the story continues. ("They come back with the docket satisfied,
  one way or another.")
- **The ceiling**: what an excellent run earns. ("They win it properly, and one of them leaves with a friend.")
- **The clock**, if there is pressure: a visible clock (a subsystem with a pool, as the Runner already shows), each
  tick tied to a cause in the fiction (a move between places, a failed check, a person losing patience), and its
  cost visible before it bites (Law 19).
- **End states**: at least two, each with what carries forward.

### 4.2 The map

Every **place** is a zone on the stage, a room on the blueprint, or a subscene (`stage.md` §8, §9). For each:

- **On arrival**: up to three sentences, a sense other than sight first, a fact not a conclusion.
- **Who is here**, and **what is here** (things that can be looked at, taken, used).
- **Look closer**: the checks this place offers (Seek, Investigate, Recall Knowledge, a lore), each with four
  outcomes.
- **Hidden**: what is here but unseen until found, and how.
- **Ways on**: doors and exits, and what each costs (time, a check, a locked gate).

### 4.3 The people

For each person: where they are, and **where they go as the clock moves** (people are not furniture); what they want;
**what they ask of the party**; their conversation map (attitude, what they say freely, on a check, never, and five
to eight likely questions answered in their voice, `scene-standard.md` SR-C3); and how they react to the moves
most likely to involve them.

### 4.4 The discoveries

Everything the scene needs the table to learn, and the optional things it rewards them for learning. **Each needed
discovery has at least three routes**: different places, different people or different skills, so no single failed
roll or unvisited room can stall the scene. The Runner tracks which have been found, and how many routes remain for
the rest.

### 4.5 The likely moves

The heart of it. For **every place**, the twelve **move families** below are each answered with one or more prepared
responses, or marked "doesn't apply here". The families are fixed, so nothing gets forgotten and the Runner can filter
by them; the examples are SF2E's own actions.

| Family | Examples of what the table might do | SF2E |
|---|---|---|
| **Talk** | ask, persuade, charm, bargain, threaten | Request, Make an Impression, Coerce, Gather Information |
| **Look** | search the place, watch someone, read the room | Seek, Search, Investigate, Sense Motive |
| **Know** | "do I know anything about…" | Recall Knowledge, lores |
| **Go** | move to another place; follow someone | moving between zones or rooms; Follow the Expert |
| **Sneak** | get in unseen, listen at a door, hide | Avoid Notice, Sneak, Hide |
| **Trick** | lie, pose as someone, distract, palm something | Lie, Impersonate, Create a Diversion, Thievery |
| **Force** | break in, push past, start a fight | Force Open, Shove, a fight (`fight` block) |
| **Fix or use** | operate, repair, hack, use kit or magic | Crafting, Computers, a spell |
| **Wait** | watch and see what happens, let time pass | the clock moves; people move |
| **Split up** | cover more ground | groups in different places (`stage.md` §8.5) |
| **Help** | help each other, follow the one who knows | Aid, Follow the Expert |
| **Leave** | walk away from the goal | the floor, and what it costs |

Each **prepared response** is one line the GM can scan, with detail one press away:

- **If they…** the move, in plain words.
- **Roll**: the action or skill and the DC (from the level table).
- **Four outcomes**: critical success, success, failure, critical failure, each changing something.
- **What changes**: a place opens, the clock ticks, an attitude moves, a discovery is made, someone comes looking.
- **How common**: common, plausible or long shot. This only orders the list so the likeliest are on top.
- **Keywords**, so the Runner's search finds it when the player says it differently ("bribe" finds the response
  written as "offer him money").

### 4.6 The valves

- **If the table stalls**: a temptation (one sentence to say out loud), someone who comes to *them* with an ask, or
  the clock forcing the issue.
- **If they ignore the goal**: what that costs, and the floor.
- **If they split up**: what each group can find, so nobody waits with nothing to do.
- **If they do something nobody prepared for**: §6's unprepared-move key.

## 5. How Claude writes one: the workshop

1. **Floor and ceiling first.** Then the goal and the end states.
2. **Draw the map.** Name every place; draw the zones on the picture, and a blueprint if it is a building. Render the
   stage proof.
3. **Put people in places.** Wants, an ask each, where they go as time passes.
4. **Plant the discoveries,** three routes each. Draw the discovery graph: every needed discovery reachable three
   ways.
5. **The move census.** For every place, go through the twelve families and write the likely responses, most common
   first; mark the families that don't apply.
6. **The clock.** Every tick gets a cause; the cost is shown before it bites.
7. **The dead-end sweep.** Every response leads back into the map or to an end state (a graph check).
8. **Simulated tables.** Run the scene against the persona roster (`scene-simulation-testing.md`: the CASTER, the
   BRUTE, the RULES-LAWYER, the LATERAL THINKER, the QUIET ONE, the CHAOS AGENT), with predictions written first.
   Every move that found nothing prepared becomes a response or a valve.
9. **The re-read and the Standard report** (`scene-standard.md`).
10. **Ingram's read**, with the stage proof beside the text.

## 6. At the table: what the GM gets

*Mockup: plate 10.*

The Scene Runner shows a situation scene differently from a scripted one, because the GM's question is different:
not "what's next?" but "where are they, and what happens if they do that?"

- **The header**: the shape (Situation), the clock, and the floor one press away.
- **The map**: the stage's zones or the blueprint, small, with the party's positions. Click a place to open its panel.
- **The place panel**: the arrival line (to read out), who is here, what is here, the look-closer checks (they roll
  from here and record themselves, as beats already do).
- **The moves panel**: twelve family chips. When a player says what they're doing, the GM taps the family and sees
  the prepared responses for the party's place, likeliest first; or types a word into the search. Each response is
  one line; a tap opens its four outcomes and its roll.
- **Discoveries**: found, and routes left for the rest. When a needed discovery is down to its last route, it shows
  amber: time to lean on a valve.
- **The unprepared move**: one key, **"Not prepared"**. A tiny panel asks three things, each one press: what kind of
  move it was (the family), how hard it is (easy, standard, hard: the level-based DC is filled in), and how it came out
  (the degree, from the roll). A few words of what they tried are optional. It closes and logs. Nothing about it can
  hold up the session.
- **The one-press rule** holds throughout: it fits on screen, and the only thing that scrolls is one read-only list.
- **An end goal, not built: the Draft** (`draft.md`). In an action scene the GM could draw a series of draft cards, face
  up or face down, for the players to pick from, and the table would play what the card they pick says. It is the
  moves and valves above, made into cards.

## 7. After the session

- **The harvest** brings home the moves used, the unprepared moves and their outcomes, the discoveries found and by
  which route, and the time spent, with the zones and arrangements saved during play.
- **Claude adds the unprepared moves** to the scene if it will be played again (a haunt, a returning place), and to
  the next scenes' census.
- **A table profile**: plain counts of how this table has approached problems (how often they talk first, sneak,
  split up, or try the lateral thing), kept as evidence and used only to decide **what to prepare more of**. It never
  scripts what they will do, and nothing in a scene ever cites it as a forecast.

## 8. A worked example: The Errand, as a situation

*Illustration of the shape only, drawn when The Errand was an old draft (`canon/plan/chapter-01.md`, Scene 7). It has
since been rebuilt in canon (`canon/scenes/episode-c1e1--the-errand.json`), and the sketch below no longer matches it:
the scene's instrument maker is a *she*, not a *he*, and the scene has no apprentice. Where the two differ, the canon
scene is the newer. None of this is canon.*

- **Goal**: get an impossible requisition docket satisfied before nineteen-hundred.
- **Floor**: they come back with it satisfied, one way or another. **Ceiling**: they win it properly, and one of them
  leaves with a friend.
- **Clock**: "Due back by 1900" in six ticks; a tick for each move between places, and one for each critical failure.
- **Places** (zones on the street, plate 08): the instrument workshop (door, a subscene), the shop window (spot),
  the tea house (door, a subscene), the alley (door, a subscene), the way back (exit).
- **Discovery: who can read the docket**, three routes: the storekeeper, if they get him talking (Diplomacy); a card
  in the shop window (Seek); a passer-by who has seen it before (Gather Information).
- **A few prepared moves at the workshop**: *If they demand he fills it as written* (Coerce): he's unfriendly to
  bullying, and on a failure he sends them to the wrong shop, one tick. *If they read the docket closely* (Society or
  a bureaucratic lore): on a success they see what's wrong with it; on a critical success, why someone wrote it that
  way. *If they split up to try several shops*: two groups, each with a lead, and the clock ticks once for both.
- **Valve**: if they stall in the workshop, the storekeeper's apprentice asks them to carry a parcel to the tea house.

## 9. The data, sketched

check, a subsystem for the clock), plus one new block:

```jsonc
  "clock": { "subsystemRef": "…", "ticks": [{ "cause": "a move between places" }] },
  "ends": [{ "id": "…", "when": "…", "carries": "…" }],
  "places": [{ "id": "…", "zoneRef": "…", "arrival": "…", "here": ["…"], "lookCloser": ["<checkRef>"], "hidden": [], "ways": [] }],

}
```

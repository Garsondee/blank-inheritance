# Japanese Narrative Conventions and Setting Register

*Written 2026-09-13. Part of the narrative-craft reference series that began with [narrative-theory-foundations.md](narrative-theory-foundations.md). The earlier documents cover how stories work in a reader's head, how choice works, how a session is run, how a quest is shaped and how a campaign is paced; none cover the fact that this campaign is set in a Japanese city. Not nominally: the map is rendered from real OpenStreetMap and GSI Japan elevation data of the Kobe area (`design/map-generation.md` §2–3), the interface stamps documents with real Japanese classification markings (`design/visual-identity.md` §1b), and the register sits on a locked 1950s Japan tone pivot (`design/campaign-arc.md`). Covered here: kishōtenketsu, jo-ha-kyū, ma, mono no aware, the adjacent aesthetic vocabulary, honorifics and register, naming, Shōwa texture, the school story, giri and ninjō, and the difference between using a culture's forms and wearing them. **This is not a decision document.** Nothing here overrides `ideas/story/blank-inheritance-v4.md`, `ideas/story/bible/`, `design/campaign-arc.md`, or any entry in `data/campaign/open-questions.json`. Where a claim meets a live open question or an unbuilt system, it says so and stops.*

*Claims are tagged: **[E]** empirical or plainly documented, **[T]** theory, **[C]** craft (practitioner consensus, never measured), **[Q]** contested. Aesthetic concepts are [T] or [C]. Japanese terms appear in romaji with kanji on first use, romaji thereafter.*

## 1. Kishōtenketsu (起承転結) — the structure that does not require a villain

Four parts: **ki** (起) introduces, **shō** (承) develops without complicating, **ten** (転) turns by bringing in something the first two parts did not prepare you for, and **ketsu** (結) reconciles, retroactively changing what came before. Chinese in origin, absorbed into Japanese composition teaching, used in school essays and four-panel manga alike. **[T]**, **[E]** on the origin.

**The load is carried by the turn, not by opposition** — interest comes from the audience being handed a third thing and then shown how it belongs, not from a force meeting a counter-force. Two corrections to the version circulating in English game-writing: it is not "a story with no conflict," only one where conflict is not the organising engine, and its reach beyond composition teaching is contested rather than settled. **[Q]** on both.

**Checked against this campaign.** The v4 bible's thesis (§1) is that nobody in it has ever done anything evil — every catastrophe is an ordinary person flinching and then building a life on that moment — so a structure that produces interest without an antagonist matches the premise rather than decorating it. The Watcher is the existing case: §24's landing is "it was never stupid. It was new, and it was drowning, and nobody had ever spoken to it." A third fact arrives and two chapters mean something different.

**When drafting: a third beat of "and then someone opposes them" is a conflict scene, which is fine and this campaign has plenty; a third beat of "and then they learn something that was always true" is a turn, and it carries a reveal without anyone needing to be wrong on purpose.** Hold the *ten* as a fact rather than a scheduled beat ([narrative-theory-foundations.md](narrative-theory-foundations.md) §1), since a table can reach it early or refuse it.

## 2. Jo-ha-kyū (序破急) — tempo, and the fact that it nests

Three movements: **jo** (序), a slow deliberate opening; **ha** (破, "break"), development that accelerates; **kyū** (急), a rapid conclusion. It originates in gagaku court music, was formalised as a principle of performance by Zeami Motokiyo (c. 1363–1443) in his noh treatises, and was then applied across other Japanese arts. **[E]** on the origin, **[T]** on the general principle. Its distinctive property is nesting — the same tempo governs a whole programme, one play, one scene and a single gesture — because it prescribes rate, not plot.

**Against [pacing-and-the-responsive-campaign.md](pacing-and-the-responsive-campaign.md) §3 these are complementary models, not rivals.** The sawtooth says where the highs and lows sit across a campaign and insists the relax phase is designed rather than left over; jo-ha-kyū says what shape one unit has from start to finish. What it adds is useful because the common table failure is its exact inverse: GMs open quickly, impatient for the content, then close slowly, tidying up. **A scene, a session and a chapter should each end faster than they began** — which agrees with that document's §4 on peak–end, where the ending carries a disproportionate share of what gets remembered.

**Checked against this campaign.** Units already exist at four scales: the four-quarter day (`data/campaign/config.json`'s `timeEconomy`), the session, the chapter, and the campaign's twenty chapters in five Books (`ideas/story/blank-inheritance-twenty-chapters.md`, a flexible draft).

## 3. Ma (間) — the interval is content

**Ma** (間) is the character for interval, used for gaps in space and time alike: the bay between two pillars, the silence between two notes, the pause held before a movement. It is not absence but a shaped, timed interval that gives form to what stands either side of it. **[T]** That it is an everyday competence shows in ordinary vocabulary: *ma ga warui* (間が悪い) means badly timed or awkward, and *manuke* (間抜け), literally "missing the ma," means a fool. **[E]** **[Q]:** much of the English account of ma as a unified philosophy of space was packaged for foreign audiences in the twentieth century by architects exhibiting abroad.

**Silence and stillness are things you deliver, not gaps in delivery.** A scene that never stops moving has no weight, because weight comes from the audience having a moment in which to feel what just happened. After a reveal, stop talking and do not fill it; cut a scene one beat before its obvious end; put the interval before a hard choice rather than after it. One caveat: pure silence at a table, especially over voice, reads as a technical fault and someone will ask if you dropped out, so describe the room continuing to be still — the tide on the harbour wall, a fan turning — and the stillness becomes plainly content.

**Checked against this campaign.** `design/encyclopaedia-checks.md` describes optional Recall Knowledge offered proactively, where "failure never costs anything mechanical," and says a scene with no `insight` check should be the exception. An offered check needs an unhurried moment to be offered in — **the pause and the optional check are the same design move seen from two sides.** One ma is already authored: §6's first Claimant fights the founder's statue "for ninety seconds with total commitment."

## 4. Mono no aware (物の哀れ) — the register this campaign is actually asking for

**Mono no aware** (物の哀れ) is usually rendered "the pathos of things," and more accurately names a capacity: the ability to be moved by things, and the gentle sadness of recognising that they pass. Motoori Norinaga (1730–1801) established it as a critical term, arguing that this sensitivity is the essence of *The Tale of Genji* and arguing against reading that work as moral instruction. **[T]** on the concept, **[E]** on Norinaga's role.

Two things are routinely lost in translation. **It is a virtue, not a mood** — a person who feels mono no aware is being praised for it; the emotion is tenderness and it contains delight, and the blossom is beautiful *because* it falls. And **nobody is at fault**, the load-bearing distinction from Western tragedy: tragedy runs on an action, a reversal and usually a fault, arriving at catastrophe by way of pity and fear, while this has a thing that was good, and time. Nor is it nihilism — nihilism says nothing matters, this says *this matters and it is going*, an argument for being kind now. **[T]**

**That distinction is what lets this campaign be moving without becoming miserable.** Known Risk #5 (§22) sets the standing rule: the material "*wants* to curdle… if a run of sessions has contained no jokes, no warmth, and no small good days, that is a fault, not a mood." Mono no aware is the register on the other side of it, sad and warm at once, requiring nobody to be cruel.

**The working rule: sadness is earned by affection, never by loss.** Write the ordinary good thing first and at length, as a pleasure — the classroom, the shop the week before term, the argument about nothing — and let its passing be brief. **If a beat is sad and nobody in it is at fault, and the table wishes the thing could have stayed, it belongs here; if it is sad because someone was cruel, or because it shows that nothing matters, §6 has already ruled it out** ("no casual casualties, no atrocity for texture, no on-screen cruelty").

**Checked against this campaign.** §17's School landing is a mono no aware scene as written: the party graduates "absurdly, in the middle of everything, with the city half-rebuilt and the sky full of eyes," and the instruction is "**Play it entirely straight.** It is the warmest scene available and it is the actual reward." Playing it straight is right, because the register collapses the moment it is ironised.

## 5. The adjacent vocabulary, and the decoration risk

**Wabi-sabi** (侘寂) is originally two terms: *wabi* (侘), the desolation of poverty reframed positively in tea culture as deliberate austerity, and *sabi* (寂), the beauty that arrives with age and wear and cannot be manufactured. **[T]** **[Q]:** the hyphenated compound is far commoner in English than as a fixed pair in Japanese. Usable core: **a surface that records its own use is more beautiful than a new one, and the record is the point.**

**Yūgen** (幽玄), "profound and dim," appears in waka criticism and later in Zeami's noh aesthetics, pointing at depth suggested rather than displayed. **[T]** **[Q]:** its meaning shifts between periods and genres and specialists disagree about what it meant when, so do not cite one definition as settled. Usable core: **show the edge of the thing, not the thing** — where [narrative-theory-foundations.md](narrative-theory-foundations.md) §8 arrives from cognitive science.

**Kintsugi** (金継ぎ) is the repair of broken ceramics with lacquer dusted with gold, so the join is conspicuous rather than hidden. **[E]** Overused in English self-help writing, and apt here regardless: `design/campaign-arc.md` commits to the idea without the word — "The city takes real, visible scars… the throughline is *enduring*, not restoring" — and `tools/city-map/citymap/ops/` implements it literally, recording damage as an operation log replayed on every render, so a scar is durable data rather than paint.

**The risk with all of these, ma and mono no aware included: they are the standard English "Japanese aesthetics" starter pack and are frequently used as decoration**, with the word doing the work the writing failed to do. **Use them as private checks on a draft, never as vocabulary inside the fiction.** No NPC here should name an aesthetic category, for the same reason none should say "how ironic."

## 6. Honorifics and register — the shift is the payload

Japanese encodes the relationship between two speakers in nearly every sentence. **[E]** throughout. **Address suffixes:** *-san* (さん), neutral polite, the default; *-sama* (様), elevated; *-kun* (君), typically a junior, classically a younger male but used by seniors to juniors of either gender inside institutions; *-chan* (ちゃん), affectionate, and deliberately infantilising when misapplied; *-sensei* (先生), a title in its own right for teachers, doctors and lawyers; *-senpai* (先輩), a school or workplace senior, usable as a standalone address, where *-kōhai* (後輩) is the matching junior term but is **not** normally used to address anybody. **Speech level:** plain; polite (*teineigo*, 丁寧語); respectful, elevating the other party (*sonkeigo*, 尊敬語); humble, lowering oneself (*kenjōgo*, 謙譲語) — the last three together *keigo* (敬語). And *yobisute* (呼び捨て), bare name with no suffix, is its own named act, reading as deep intimacy or open contempt depending on the relationship.

**None of that detail is what you use at the table. The shift is.** **[C]** A character who has always said *Tanaka-san* says *Tanaka*, and either intimacy has been claimed or contempt has. A character speaking plainly to a friend switches to polite form: distance imposed on purpose. Excessive politeness to an intimate is aggression, not courtesy; *sensei* withdrawn, or applied with an edge to someone who never held it, is a weapon.

**For an English-language table: do not reproduce the system, reproduce the shift, and narrate it out loud.** English has its own ladder the table can actually hear — title and surname, surname alone, first name, nickname — and when a step is taken, **say that it was taken**: *she calls him Hori, not Hori-san, for the first time, and he notices.* Two failure modes: **sprinkling**, where every NPC has a suffix and none of them mean anything, so a real shift goes unnoticed when it comes; and **stripping**, discarding the cheapest legible relationship signal available in a setting whose theme is institutional hierarchy.

**Checked against this campaign, and two gaps.** §2 already runs a register system with no honorifics in it — "Given names for the young and the outsiders, surnames for the institution — the Directorate's register is audibly colder than the school's" — which is this mechanism implemented in English, and the right call for this table. But nothing states whether Japanese honorifics are spoken at the table at all, and §2's own convention has visible exceptions: Oda is a surname and Directorate, which fits, while Kudo is an outsider referred to by surname and Ume, Satoru and Nobu are Directorate figures glossed with given-name meanings. Whether that is drift or deliberate variation is the owner's call. Adjacent: `design/npc-social-intel.md` tracks NPC Attitude on SF2E's real five-step Hostile-to-Helpful scale, and a register shift is the audible signal that a step has been taken, but `npc-attitude-player-visibility` is still `open`.

## 7. Naming — order, ownership, and the boring-literal place name

Japanese names are family name first, and Japan's government formally asked in 2019 that they be written family-name-first in official English-language documents, reversing a long convention of flipping them. **[E]** Which order a work uses is a choice, but it must be consistent, because the reader uses it to tell which half is the family name. Ownership is the §6 signal: an NPC who starts using a PC's given name has done something, and so has a PC who uses an NPC's without invitation.

Family names are overwhelmingly toponymic and mundane in meaning; given-name kanji are chosen for sound and sense together and their readings are famously irregular (*nanori* readings). **[E]** A name's meaning is therefore usually available but rarely announced — how §2 already handles it, glossing Ume as the plum blossom that opens in winter and Satoru as *to comprehend*, for the GM and never for the table.

Place names come from a small, productive set of elements: terrain (*-kawa*/*-gawa* river, *-yama* mountain, *-shima*/*-jima* island, *-saki*/*-zaki* cape, *-ura* inlet), function (*-minato* harbour, *-bashi* bridge), administration (*-ku* ward, *-chō*/*-machi* district, *-chōme* block) and position (*kita-*, *minami-*, *higashi-*, *nishi-*, *chūō-* central, *shin-* new). **[E]** **The rule that follows: Japanese place names are descriptive and mundane, and inventing evocative ones is the fastest way to sound like a tourist.** **[C]** New bridge, middle field, eighth block — feeling should come from what happened there, not from the words. A sound check for anything invented: syllables are open, the only consonant closing one is *n*, vowel length is meaningful, and there is no *l* or *v*. This repo already uses macron Hepburn (`design/map-generation.md` writes Hyōgo); match it.

**Checked against this campaign.** The naming placeholders, merged into `data/campaign/open-questions.json` on 2026-09-19, still hold names such as the dig site and the school open; the city (Kurose) and the Directorate have since been named. **Unresolved names are tracked as placeholders rather than invented, which is the right treatment for exactly this material**, since a name is the easiest place to accidentally decide something about a culture the owner has not decided. One consequence: `design/map-generation.md` describes the map as real city data "rotated, renamed, fictionalised," and that renaming layer does not exist yet — the real Kobe names still in the tooling are working labels, not setting facts.

## 8. 1950s Shōwa texture — what is available, and what is not

The Shōwa era (昭和) runs 1926–1989 and the 1950s sits in its middle; "Shōwa retro" is a live nostalgia register in Japan today. **[E]** `design/campaign-arc.md` locks the tone to this period and gives the reason in the owner's own words: the real history of that period is "a lot more complex… than just generic Japan," and that complexity is "a feature to build on, not smooth over."

Documented texture, **[E]** throughout. The Allied occupation ended in 1952; the 1956 Economic White Paper carried the line *mohaya sengo de wa nai*, "it is no longer the post-war"; the high-growth period (*kōdo keizai seichō*, 高度経済成長) is conventionally dated from around 1955. Television broadcasting began in 1953, and the late-1950s aspirational set was nicknamed the *sanshu no jingi* (三種の神器, three sacred treasures) — television, washing machine, refrigerator — so a brand-new machine in a house with no bath is the period in one image. *Danchi* (団地) estates appear from the mid-fifties, concrete and desirable. Institutionally: documents sealed with a personal *hanko* (判子) rather than signed, vertical writing on forms, carbon paper and mimeograph duplication.

**The rule: borrow the institutional grammar and the material culture, never the history.** This is a secondary world — §5 and §18 place the city in the Minkai-equivalent east with its own clocks and its own founding catastrophe. The real 1950s in Japan is inseparable from the war, the occupation and the bombings, and this campaign already has its own catastrophe in that structural slot. **Importing the real war as subtext would flatten the campaign's own history and use real suffering as texture, which §6's "no atrocity for texture" already forbids.** Take the forms, the shortages, the optimism and the paperwork; leave the events. Optimism-under-scarcity is the register to chase: new concrete beside salvaged timber, an institution both underfunded and genuinely proud of itself.

**Checked against this campaign.** `design/visual-identity.md` §1 states the aesthetic as "Giant Robo by way of a 1950s Japanese naval-industrial ministry… government hardware built by people who had to justify its cost," and §1b makes kanji structural rather than decorative, using the real document markings 公開 / 部外秘 / 秘 / 機密 / 極秘. `design/strategy-layer.md` reuses that same ladder as the campaign's five Clearance rungs — the right kind of borrowing, because the ladder does a mechanical job instead of sitting on the surface. Genre inheritance is [science-fiction-and-mecha-conventions.md](science-fiction-and-mecha-conventions.md)'s subject.

**One texture note, flagged and not resolved.** `visual-identity.md` §1b justifies Courier as "the actual period face for a typed official document," which holds for Latin script; Japanese offices of the period largely did not typewrite Japanese text, since Japanese typewriters were specialist machines, so period Japanese paperwork was more often handwritten or mimeographed. That matters only to someone building a *physical* prop, and that document's decision stands.

## 9. School as a narrative mode — the calendar that does not care

The Japanese school story has its own furniture, most of it structural rather than decorative. **[E]** on the institutional facts, **[C]** on their use. The year runs April to March in three terms, so the long break falls in the middle rather than at the end, and it is bracketed by formal ceremonies — the entrance ceremony and the graduation ceremony (卒業式) are solemn occasions, not administrative ones. The fixed set pieces are the cultural festival (文化祭), sports day (体育祭), the class trip (修学旅行), and entrance examinations with the cram school (塾) around them. The rooftop is a genre convention rather than a documentary fact, since real school roofs are frequently locked. **[C]/[Q]**

Two structural units matter more than any of that. **The class is a fixed group** — students are assigned to a homeroom class for the year, and in most schools teachers rotate between rooms while students do not, which makes a classroom a sealed social system for a year. **This is the most useful fact in this section**, because an argument then has nowhere to go, and ostracism and its reversal both become real available stakes. **The club** (*bukatsu*, 部活) is the other: chosen rather than assigned, vertical rather than flat, the natural home of the *senpai*/*kōhai* relationship, and a large share of a student's actual time.

**Why it counterweights catastrophe: the school calendar is an external clock that does not care what else is happening.** That indifference drives the comedy and the pathos at once. The exam does not move.

**Checked against this campaign.** §16 lists School as one of six threads, currency "small stakes that matter enormously," and §17 makes it structural: "**School is now live in every chapter** — it is the thread most likely to quietly atrophy under table pressure, and the one that makes the other half of the game matter." The grid's School column (v4 §17, since demoted) is this genre's furniture already: "the exam timetable continues regardless" (Ch 2), "school *is* the front line" (Ch 4), "a classmate arrested; the school turns on itself" (Ch 6) — the sealed-class-group stake, used precisely — and "they graduate anyway" (Ch 8).

**Two gaps, named and not resolved.** **The school year does not exist as data anywhere** — `config.json`'s `timeEconomy` models a four-quarter day and §17 models chapters, but no term structure, festival, exam date or ceremony appears in `data/` or in any schema, and `CLAUDE.md` notes the related mechanical gap that the downtime design "needs `episode.schema.json` scene fields that don't exist yet." And the club system is **not** accepted: `ideas/entries/school-schedule-system.json` sits at status `wishlist`, carrying the owner's own 2026-09-13 framing (one club per kid, GM-run content in the evening slots) with its sub-questions open. Per `ideas/README.md`, status is the signal. Minor note: `ideas/story/bible/locations/main-school-complex.md` cites that idea's status as `new` and the file now reads `wishlist`, so one of the two is stale.

## 10. Giri and ninjō — two rights, and no way out

*Giri* (義理) is obligation owed to a specific person for a specific reason — a lord, an employer, a family, someone whose kindness created a debt (*on*, 恩). *Ninjō* (人情) is human feeling: affection, compassion, what a person wants for someone they love. The tension drives Edo-period domestic drama, above all the plays of Chikamatsu Monzaemon, whose *The Love Suicides at Sonezaki* (1703) is the canonical case — two obligations that cannot both be honoured, and no third option. **[E]** on the tradition, **[T]** on the framing.

**[Q], and important.** This vocabulary reached English-speaking readers largely through Ruth Benedict's *The Chrysanthemum and the Sword* (1946), whose account of Japanese obligation, and its shame-versus-guilt framing, is now widely criticised by specialists as reductive and methodologically compromised, having been produced at a distance during wartime without fieldwork. **Use giri and ninjō as a vocabulary for dramatic structure drawn from the plays. Do not use them as a claim about how Japanese people work.**

**The structural difference from the Western frame is the part to internalise.** The standard Western version is the individual against society: an authentic self against an external system, where the sympathetic resolution is escape and the system is what was wrong. This is not that. **Both sides are genuinely felt, both were freely accepted, and neither is the false one** — the obligation was not imposed from outside but taken on, often gratefully, from a person who deserved it. **The test when writing a character caught between two duties: would a sympathetic observer want them to abandon either one? If yes, you have written oppression, which is a different and more familiar shape.**

**Checked against this campaign.** The cast is largely built from this. **Nobu** (§2, §18) was Manako's lab assistant, failed to speak, and now quietly funds Cicada operations "as private amends" from inside Directorate procurement. His sit-in decision (§2, Ties) is the sharpest instance: he controls the technician-day pool, Bobbin is occupying a Frame, and "the man secretly funding the enemy has to decide whether to authorise a forced extraction that would kill a pacifist." **Ume** runs school welfare where "every kindness she wins is charged to the technician-day pool."

**One tension worth naming and leaving alone.** `design/campaign-arc.md` states the core theme, in the owner's words, as a group that finally succeeds in defining their own independence "against a system that demands great sacrifice from them while simultaneously imposing rigid inflexibility" — the Western individual-versus-system frame. The cast-level drama above is the giri/ninjō frame. Two engines, both deliberate, running at once: the party's arc points at escaping the institution while the NPCs around them are held by obligations they accepted honourably and would not want undone. How the two resolve against each other is the owner's call.

## 11. Respect versus pastiche, and whose take governs

What makes it work — practitioner consensus, not a measured finding. **[C]** **Specificity** — a named festival on a named date with a named person running it; specific detail is checkable and so can be got right, while generic detail cannot, because it is not about anything. **Actual research** — check, do not recall, because recall produces the average of everything absorbed, which is the stereotype by definition. **Internal consistency** — a borrowed convention must have consequences elsewhere: if the class stays together all year, that changes what a rumour does. **Conventions as tools** — the question is what job the structure does, not whether it looks Japanese.

What makes it fail. **Aesthetic borrowing without structural understanding** — blossom, honorifics and a torii gate over a plot that is structurally from somewhere else. **Treating a culture as a mood** — the serene, mysterious, wise East is not a setting, it is the absence of one. **The single-word aesthetic**, per §5, and **real suffering as texture**, per §8.

**And the instruction that governs all of it: where the project owner has a specific take, her take governs over any general convention, any published default, and anything in this document.**

This repo has a documented incident. In September 2026, Paizo's published default for mitflits — goblin-adjacent, comic-relief, casually nuisance-managed — was applied over the owner's own established reframing of the species, producing a harbour-wall trap "same as rat traps" as the campaign's opening image and a mitflit-nest clearance as its first combat. Both were cut. The correction is recorded verbatim in `ideas/story/bible/species/mitflits.md`, along with the confirmation that mitflits have never been treated as vermin at any point in the setting's history — so this was a factual error about the setting, not only a tone misjudgement. The whole of `ideas/story/bible/` exists because of it; its entry template carries mandatory **"Ingram's own take, and how it diverges from any real-world source"** and **"Tone and register — what NOT to do"** sections, and `LEDGER.md` records every direct question and its verbatim answer so the same question is never asked twice or silently re-guessed. The principle appears positively too: asked why illusionists run private back-street magic shows, the owner's answer in `LEDGER.md` begins "showy magic is frowned upon except in an emergency in **my version of Golarion and Japan**." **So the procedure is: check the bible first, check `LEDGER.md` before asking, and when a Japanese convention and an established campaign fact disagree, the campaign fact wins and the convention bends.**

One last thing, and it is the strongest reason here to get this right rather than approximately right. `design/visual-identity.md` §3d records a measured finding — twenty of twenty-one Japanese interface elements rendering under 12px, the smallest at 7px, at which size kanji strokes merge before the glyph resolves — and gives the reason for fixing it: **"There are Japanese readers at this table, so the Japanese is *content*, not texture."** A 12px floor is now enforced by an automated test. That sentence is the whole of this section in eleven words, and it was written down before this document existed.

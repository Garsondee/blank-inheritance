# Strategy Layers, Downtime, and the Between-Missions Game

*Written 2026-09-13. One of a reference series on narrative and game-design craft, joining the five written the same day — [narrative-theory-foundations.md](narrative-theory-foundations.md), [agency-and-player-choice.md](agency-and-player-choice.md), [ttrpg-campaign-and-session-craft.md](ttrpg-campaign-and-session-craft.md), [quests-objectives-and-threads.md](quests-objectives-and-threads.md) and [pacing-and-the-responsive-campaign.md](pacing-and-the-responsive-campaign.md). Those five cover scenes, sessions and arcs. None covers the layer above a session: the resource-and-choice game played between missions, where a limited pool is allocated against more demands than it can meet. That is this document's subject — the XCOM shape, opportunity cost, base building, downtime activity design, scheduling as an economy, and rest as pacing control. This campaign already has a designed strategy layer and a designed time economy, so nearly everything here is checked against real local material rather than argued in the abstract. **This is not a decision document.** Nothing here overrides `quarantine/ideas/story/blank-inheritance-v4.md`, `design/campaign-arc.md`, `design/strategy-layer.md`, or any entry in `data/campaign/open-questions.json`. Where a live question would benefit from the framing, it says so and stops without answering it.*

*Claims are tagged: **[E]** empirical, **[T]** theory, **[C]** craft (practitioner consensus from shipped games and thousands of tables — often righter than theory, never measured), **[Q]** contested. Nothing below is tagged **[E]** or **[T]**, and that is deliberate: strategy-layer and downtime design is practitioner craft almost end to end, with no experimental literature behind it worth citing. Inflating these tags would misrepresent how well any of it is known.*

---

## 1. Two layers, and what the upper one is for

A strategy layer is a slower game played above the scene-by-scene one, in which players spend a limited pool across more demands than it covers. It converts a series of episodes into a campaign, and the mechanism is precise: **it makes earlier decisions constrain later ones.** [C] Without it, session eight is reachable from session one by any route, and nothing done in between is load-bearing on what the party can now attempt. The division of labour is standard: the **tactical layer** decides how one encounter resolves; the **strategic layer** decides which encounters happen, what is at stake in them, what the party is permitted to do, and what they arrive carrying. [C]

`design/strategy-layer.md` states that boundary correctly in its own opening — *"Combats are SF2E combats… The strategy layer decides **which** encounters happen, **what is at stake in them**, and **what the party is permitted to do** — never how a round resolves"* — and its §2 loop (BRIEFING, COMMIT, PLAY, DEBRIEF) puts the strategic layer at both ends of a session and explicitly silent in the middle.

**The rule: the strategic layer changes what a session is about, never how a roll resolves.** A mechanic that reaches into resolution — a custom modifier, a second dice system, a bespoke check competing with SF2E's own — has crossed the boundary and should be rebuilt as a change to stakes, permissions or starting conditions.

## 2. The XCOM shape, and the one property that makes it work

*XCOM* is the canonical worked example because it is the cleanest. A limited pool (engineers, scientists, satellites, squad time), more demands than it can meet (several countries panicking at once, all wanting the same response), and a threat clock advancing regardless of what the player does. The core experience is **triage** — not "what shall I build," but "which fire do I leave burning." [C] The essential property, stated exactly because it is the one most easily lost in revision: **the strategy layer must present more legitimate demands than can be satisfied, or it is just a menu.** [C] *Legitimate* does the quiet work there — the surplus must be things a reasonable player actually wants, since three good options and four duds is a three-option menu with extra reading.

This campaign states the same property twice. `data/campaign/config.json`'s `timeEconomy.designIntent`: *"More options exist than slices to spend them on — that gap is the entire engine of downtime play."* `design/strategy-layer.md` §2 is blunter: *"There are always more offers than slices. They argue. Do not rescue them from the choice."* That last instruction is the hardest to hold at a real table, because the reflex when six players are unhappy about what will not fit into a day is to find a way to fit it. The threat-clock half is also built: the Mass Clock (v4 bible §15) is explicitly *"not a timer — a state track, advanced by what happens rather than by how many sessions pass,"* and the Contact Pool advances on quiet mornings with a hard ceiling at the eighth.

**The rule: something must worsen while the party decides, so that deciding slowly is itself a decision.**

## 3. Opportunity cost is the actual content

What a player does in a working strategy layer is not choose an activity. It is choose what to give up. **A downtime system in which the party can eventually do everything is a delay, not a decision** — it converts a choice into a queue, and a queue produces no story because nothing was forfeited. [C] Money-based economies leak here, since income accumulates and what was a sacrifice becomes something bought on the way to something else. Time-sliced systems do not leak, because **time cannot be accumulated**, which is why scheduling games force opportunity cost without a designer constantly retuning prices. [C]

This campaign has both kinds and uses each correctly. The **shared technician-day pool** is the money-shaped resource, and `config.json` states its tension outright: *"Repairing a friend's Frame and upgrading your own compete directly for the same finite resource — this is the primary economic tension of downtime."* Being shared across six pilots makes every allocation a negotiation between players rather than an optimisation by one. Deliberately against that, **each pilot's machine budget is individual**: a Frame's loadout is *"bought with the pilot's credit budget"* (`frame.schema.json`, the reskinned economy that replaced upgrade points on 2026-09-20). Protect the contrast: if every resource is shared, the loudest player becomes the party's quartermaster; if none are, the argument that generates fiction disappears. The **slice economy** is the time-shaped resource, analysed in §9.

**The rule: measure a downtime system by what it forces players to abandon, not by what it lets them attempt.** If nobody can name something they wanted this week and did not get, the surplus is not real yet.

## 4. Offscreen actors and faction clocks — the allocation side of pressure

*Blades in the Dark* supplies the other half of the standard vocabulary: **faction clocks**, progress tracks for organisations the party is not looking at, advanced by those factions pursuing their own goals during downtime. The world gets a turn. [C] *Blades* pairs this with a small fixed number of downtime actions per character per phase — the same structural trick as a slice, a hard per-phase cap that cannot be saved up. [ttrpg-campaign-and-session-craft.md](ttrpg-campaign-and-session-craft.md) §4 already covers the fronts-and-clocks tradition in full and maps it onto the Mass Clock and the Comprehension rungs. **That document owns the threat side; this one owns the resource-allocation side, and they should not be merged.** A clock says the world is getting worse; an allocation system says what it costs to do anything about it. Clocks without allocation produce dread without agency; allocation without clocks produces a management game where waiting is free.

This campaign has both, and its enemy turn is better-built than the genre standard. `design/strategy-layer.md` §4 makes the Watcher's move a function of three tracked numbers — Comprehension decides *what*, the Mirror decides *how*, Mass decides *how big* — and notes this beats XCOM's Dark Event slot *"because the players caused all three inputs."* A Dark Event drawn from a table produces the reaction "bad luck"; a move derived from the party's own recorded conduct produces the one that document wants, *"we taught it this."* Its pre-emption rule ties that back to allocation — *"Spending slices on the right target before the debrief cancels or redirects the move"* — which is the XCOM trade exactly.

**The rule: an enemy turn derived from tracked player conduct is worth more than one drawn from a table, because only the first can be argued with afterwards.**

## 5. Base building and the institutional PC

When the party's organisation is a tracked entity with state, three things become available that are otherwise hard to manufacture: **a persistent stake** (something that can be attacked or lost, giving defensive play a target), **visible investment** (past allocation standing where players can see it), and **a place for consequence to accumulate** (a ledger that carries forward instead of resetting per session). [C] It need not be a literal base — a budget, a reputation and internal politics do the same work.

The Directorate has all of that: a shared budget (technician-days), a reputation track the party moves (Standing), a permission ladder bought with it (Clearance rungs 0-5, `strategy-layer.md` §3), a secrecy model for its own geography (Site Classification's five tiers plus `omega-zero`, added 2026-09-12), and six named repair chiefs, one per pilot. Three details are better than standard practice. **The resource pipeline has a face and a secret**: Nobu controls procurement and the technician-day pool (v4 bible §11) while secretly funding the enemy, so §20's bifurcation *"Expose Nobu or use him?"* resolves in the yes branch to *"The column's funding stops; so does every repair the party relies on."* **The enemy attacks the strategy layer directly** — v4 bible §19, Chapter Six: *"It goes for logistics: the causeway, the repair pool, the sea-fort."* **Clearance is thematically load-bearing**: `strategy-layer.md` §3 argues that the campaign's theme is who gets believed and that *"Clearance is being believed, made countable,"* completed by that document's §10 rule that a rung is won at a Board rather than bought at a counter.

**Gap, named and left open: Standing has no numbers.** `strategy-layer.md` §3 lists what each rung unlocks and its §9 says rungs are *"purchased with Standing, gated by chapter,"* but no earn rate, rung cost or scale exists anywhere in the repo, and `data/campaign/open-questions.json` has no entry tracking it. That is the largest undefined quantity in the strategy layer, and it is currently untracked rather than deliberately deferred. **A second, presentational gap:** that document's §3 opens *"Keep the number at five. A sixth track is a proposal to delete one of these,"* and now carries six subsections, since Site Classification was added under it on 2026-09-12. It is defensible as written — Classification is a property of a place rather than a party resource — but heading and contents now disagree on a count, and if Classification ever gains a spend it becomes a track in fact.

**The rule: if the institution can be damaged, the party has something to defend; if it can only be spent from, it is a shop.**

## 6. What makes a downtime option worth having

Three tests, and an option should pass all three. [C] **It should produce a decision, not a die roll** — an option whose whole content is "roll, apply result" is a random number with flavour text. **It should interact with something else on the menu** — options that touch nothing else are parallel tracks, and a menu of parallel tracks is a set of unrelated minigames. **It should be legible in advance** — a choice made without a predictable payoff is not a decision the player can own. The failure mode to watch is **the strictly optimal option**: if one entry dominates under most conditions, the menu has one item and some decoration. The fix is not to nerf the dominant option into uselessness, but to make payoffs incommensurable, so comparing them requires knowing what this particular week demands.

Checked against the Time Economy menu (`config.json`'s five: Frame Modification, Repair Advocacy, Investigation, Analysis Duty, Rest — the design bible's Sync Training and Anchor Time went with Resonance and Anchors on 2026-09-20), three things stand out. **Analysis Duty is the strongest entry and worth studying as a model**: two modes (reveal the Contact Pool's current target number, or push the floor back) mean choosing it still requires a decision, and it interacts with another system rather than paying into a vacuum, while `config.json`'s note on it states the principle the whole menu runs on — *"tactical certainty is purchased with personal time — the same currency as everything else in this game."* **Legibility is already policy**: `open-questions.json`'s `activity-buff-legibility` records it exactly — *"buffs must be LEGIBLE and PLANNABLE"* — and names a free anti-hoarding mechanism, since buffs expressed as **circumstance bonuses** are capped by SF2E's own stacking rules. **A deliberate non-payout option exists**: `design/downtime-activities.md`'s Quiet Hobby Time has no check and no reward, because *"a menu that only ever rewards should be treated as a bug, not a feature."* Keep it — a menu with no unrewarded option teaches players that time not converted into a bonus is wasted.

**The rule: an option that pays a bonus and touches nothing else on the menu is a vending machine, not a choice.**

## 7. Coverage across player motivations

A downtime menu is where different kinds of players are most visibly served or ignored, because it is the one moment when everyone picks for themselves with no encounter forcing a shape onto the choice. [ttrpg-campaign-and-session-craft.md](ttrpg-campaign-and-session-craft.md) §9 already covers Robin Laws's taxonomy against this campaign's downtime menu and finds deliberate coverage; that is not repeated here. Two extensions.

**Coverage decays silently as the menu grows.** `design/downtime-activities.md` proposes three named rest types, a fourth Workshop option, three Frame-side activities and seven non-story activities on top of the core menu. Most additions serve the tactician and power gamer, because mechanically-specified options are the easiest kind to write. **This campaign has a second coverage axis the taxonomy does not cover: two sheets.** Every player runs a pilot and a machine, so a menu paying out mostly in Frame capability serves the pilot half of a player's attention and starves the other — and the campaign's own reward statement, that the reward is bonds, lives entirely on the kid side. `downtime-activities.md` §2 is alert to this and separates kid-side rewards, which borrow SF2E's toolkit, from Frame-side rewards, which spend the campaign's own economy.

**The rule: audit the menu for coverage in the edit that changes it, because the types that lose coverage first are the ones with no numbers to argue with.**

## 8. The two-games problem

The characteristic failure of a strategy layer is that it becomes a separate, better game sitting next to the roleplaying game and competing for the table's attention. [C] The briefing overruns every week, players arrive with spreadsheets, and the scenes afterwards feel like confirmation of decisions already made. The layer is usually not badly designed when this happens — it is often too well designed, and has become the part of the evening with the clearest feedback. Whether that is a problem at all is genuinely contested. **[Q]** One camp holds that optimisation is a legitimate pleasure that deepens investment; the other holds that time spent optimising is time not spent in character, and that a campaign whose best moments happen on a board has quietly changed genre. Both positions are held by competent practitioners, and this document does not settle it. What is not contested is the fix when a table wants the fiction primary: **strategic outcomes have to show up as fiction in the next session, not only as modifiers.** [C] A track that moves and produces a number is bookkeeping; a track that changes who talks to the party, what door opens, and what a scene looks like when they walk into it is a story engine.

`design/strategy-layer.md` specifies that fix in three places. §6 requires every scene brief to carry an INTRO and an OUTRO, the INTRO's hard part being *"what the previous scene's outcome changes about this one"* — *"a scene that reads identically whatever happened before it is not connected to anything"* — and names the OUTRO *"the strategy layer's only hook into the tactical layer,"* which is what stops the layer leaking into resolution. §10's Board rule does the same for advancement: Standing pays for a rung, but *"Standing alone never grants one,"* since the rung is won in a hearing run in initiative. §8 supplies the budget — ten minutes for briefing and debrief together, *"if the strategy layer regularly takes longer than that, cut a track"* — with the cut order pre-decided as Combined Operations first and Bonds second, Bonds only after checking the register has not gone grim without it. That pre-decision directly answers v4 bible §22's Known Risk #1, which names choosing what gets cut as a live task. `design/design-layers.md` adds the adjacent discipline and outranks the other design documents where it conflicts with them: the Time Economy, the four-quarter day, downtime activities and the Contact Pool are all **Layer 2**, added onto a pure-SF2E Layer 0 already known to work.

**The rule: a track that moves has to change what the next scene looks like, or it is bookkeeping wearing a track's clothes.**

## 9. Scheduling as a game, and why expiry makes it an economy

When the unit of the strategy layer is time rather than money, the game changes shape. Time cannot be saved, so there is no hoarding strategy, and time slots carry **eligibility**, which money does not: a morning-only activity competes with other morning things specifically, not with the day's budget in general. [C] The property that turns a schedule into an economy is **expiry** — without it a schedule is a queue with an order, everything still happening later, and §3 applies. **Expiry is what makes an unspent slice a loss rather than a postponement.** [C] The associated risk is real: expiry timers can produce anxiety and completionist grinding rather than considered choice. The mitigation is disclosure, which this campaign already mandates.

The shape here, verified against source:

- **Four discretionary slices per day, one per quarter** (`config.json`, `timeEconomy.discretionarySlicesPerDay: 4`), revised from 2 on 2026-09-06 once the day gained a four-quarter shape — *"every quarter is live, not two 'real' ones and two dead ones."* The day ring (`ux-doctrine.md` §7a) is the board it is played on, and settles the relationship §3 leaves aside: *"quarters carry eligibility and slices are the budget,"* so *"wanting two morning-only things is a real conflict even with slices to spare elsewhere in the day."*
- **Mandatory load still spends the slice.** `timeEconomy.mandatoryLoad` is explicit that school (morning and afternoon only) is a standing option like anything else — *"Choosing it still spends the quarter's slice, the same as any other pick."* An activity that is free does not compete, and one that does not compete is not part of the economy.
- **Mandatory and slot-consuming are separate tests** (Law 20): passive versus real event, never whether something is mandatory. A mandatory broadcast nobody must act on is free; a forced Summons spends a slice on the player's behalf.
- **Nothing expires without its cost having been visible first** (Law 19): *"A timer whose consequence is only revealed on expiry is a gotcha, not a decision."* §7a extends it, requiring days-remaining to be readable without opening a marker and two things eligible in the same quarter to read as a conflict before commitment.

One further move is easy to miss: **the slice budget is itself a dial the arc turns.** The v4 bible's chapter grid (§19, since demoted) opened on *"Slices: generous"* and closed on *"No slices. There is no ordinary life left to schedule."* The scheduling game opens wide and closes to nothing, and the closure is characterisation rather than bookkeeping. **Gap, named and left open: the curve between those endpoints is not declared**, and the twenty-chapter outline (`quarantine/ideas/story/blank-inheritance-twenty-chapters.md`) does not set one. That is a pacing decision rather than a detail — the rate at which a player's menu narrows is how the campaign's tightening is felt week to week.

**The rule: a scheduling system without expiry is a queue, and a queue produces no opportunity cost.**

## 10. Rest and recovery as design

A rest system is almost never really about recovery. **What a rest system regulates is pacing pressure** — how long a party can keep pushing before the fiction makes them stop, and therefore how much a day can demand before the demand becomes a decision rather than a cost absorbed. [C] Hit points are the instrument; the schedule is the subject. Two failure modes sit either side of a narrow correct setting. **Rest with no teeth makes the scheduling game trivial**: if working through the night costs nothing, a rational table never rests, which collapses §2's surplus-demand property because players have more capacity than the design assumed. **Rest with too many teeth makes rest the strictly optimal option**, which is §6's dominance failure applied to the one item always available.

This campaign's material sits between them, and its documents are careful about what is settled. `config.json`'s `timeEconomy.fatigue` records the confirmed structure — consecutive segments worked without rest trigger a check that gets harder each segment, ending in involuntary unconsciousness and the loss of that segment's slot — and leaves `check`, `dcEscalation` and `resetBy` as `null` against `fatigue-escalation-formula`. **`design/downtime-activities.md` proposes the numbers, and it is a proposal with open questions attached, not a decision.** Its own opening says so: *"Status: a proposal, not a lock."* It proposes a four-stage Exhaustion track built entirely from real SF2E conditions — Fatigued, then adding Slowed 1, then adding Stupefied 1, then Unconscious — with a Fortitude check at DC 15 escalating by 5 per consecutive segment, and three named rest types each grounded in a real rule (Lights Out on Long-Term Rest's doubled recovery, Sick Bay on Treat Wounds, Night Out on a circumstance bonus — its Bleed-reduction half went with Bleed). Both `fatigue-escalation-formula` and `rest-activity-roster` remain `status: open`, each carrying a note pointing at the proposal. That distinction is maintained correctly in every file involved and should stay that way.

Two observations on its shape. **Building the track from real conditions is right for reasons beyond accuracy** — Fatigued, Slowed and Stupefied are already automated by the shipped `sf2e` system and integrated with everything that reads a condition, where a bespoke exhaustion effect would need all of that built. **The escalating-DC shape passes `campaign-arc.md`'s standing test**, worth checking explicitly: the 2026-09-06 principle is that penalties cost risk and never choice, and a rising Fortitude DC is a dynamic, recoverable, player-managed risk rather than a permanently shrunken menu.

**The rule: set the rest penalty so that pushing through is a real option a player sometimes takes, and never so that it is free or unthinkable.**

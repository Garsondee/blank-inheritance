# Worldbuilding Craft

*Written 2026-09-13. The first of eleven new reference documents, opening the library's fantasy and science-fiction writing-craft shelf — the five narrative and game-design references written earlier the same day ([narrative-theory-foundations.md](narrative-theory-foundations.md) and its siblings) covered how stories work in a reader's head; this shelf covers how genre fiction is made. This one covers world consistency and construction: sub-creation, the iceberg, register, magic-system heuristics, rigour versus mystery, the worldbuilding trap, cultures, institutions, supply chains, deep time, and adapting a published setting. Its sibling [environmental-storytelling-and-place.md](environmental-storytelling-and-place.md) covers place as lived experience; this one covers whether the world holds together when you walk out of the room. **This is not a decision document.** Nothing here overrides the v4 bible, `ideas/story/bible/`, `design/campaign-arc.md`, or `data/campaign/open-questions.json`.*

*Claims are tagged: **[E]** empirical (peer-reviewed or at least tested), **[T]** theory (a scholarly or authorial framework — vocabulary, not proof), **[C]** craft (practitioner consensus, often righter than theory and never measured), **[Q]** contested.*

---

## 1. Sub-creation, and why Tolkien refused "suspension of disbelief"

Tolkien's "On Fairy-Stories" supplies the founding vocabulary. **[T]** The maker is a **sub-creator** building a **Secondary World** the mind can enter; inside it, what the maker relates accords with the laws of that world, and so you believe it while you are inside. Tolkien's term is **secondary belief**, and he explicitly rejects Coleridge's willing suspension of disbelief as a description of it — that is what you fall back on when the art has already failed and the reader props it up from outside. Good fantasy does not need the charity; when disbelief arises, the spell is broken. The mechanism is internal consistency, not plausibility against our world. A green sun is not the problem; a green sun that behaves differently on page 300 is.

**The rule: a broken internal rule costs more than an implausible rule kept.**

The exposure here is the Mirror. v4 §6 states that the Watcher learns from the strongest coherent signal it receives — its methods, never its objective (`ideas/story/dossier-observer.md` §16) — and that this is diegetic — "not the GM softening or hardening the campaign." If Claimants ever soften because the GM wanted a gentler session rather than because the party taught them to, the world stops obeying its own stated law. That is a consistency obligation, not a tone preference.

## 2. The iceberg, and the honest objection to it

Hemingway's iceberg: the writer knows far more than reaches the page, and the omitted material gives the visible eighth its weight. **[C]** The version practitioners endorse is narrower than the popular one — omission strengthens only when the writer genuinely knows the thing omitted. The honest objection is stronger still. **Unshown material does nothing unless the shown material implies it.** Backstory that never inflects a line of dialogue, a described object or a hesitation is unused notes, and "do I know more than I have said" is always true and therefore worthless as a test.

**The rule: for every piece of hidden history, name the visible detail it changes. If you cannot, you have written notes, not an iceberg.**

This campaign built the strictest version of that test and did not call it that. `blank-inheritance-ch1-v2.md` §4 ("Show, Never Tell") lists planted, unglossed details, each with a parenthetical naming the hidden fact it is the surface of: an anthem with no composer credited *(Rupiah)*; the founding charter framed in the lobby *(its painted-out paragraph has since been cut, LEDGER #41)*. Then §5, the **Mystery Ledger**, adds what most projects never do — thirteen plants, each tracked against a payoff row. That is an iceberg with receipts. Two limits, named and not resolved: the payoff column was cleared when the old chapter drafts went, so every payoff is currently unassigned, and the ledger covers Chapter One only.

## 3. Le Guin, register, and why the sentence is the world

Ursula K. Le Guin's "From Elfland to Poughkeepsie" (1973) is the strongest single piece of craft writing on this subject, and it is directly about this campaign's problem. **[C]** Her argument: style is not decoration applied to a world — style is the only access anyone has to the world, and therefore style *is* the world. Setting older fantasy prose beside fantasy written in flat contemporary idiom, she demonstrates by rewriting that the modern colloquial voice does not merely lower the tone; it relocates the story, because a character who speaks like a person from the present day is standing in the present day whatever the map says. She is equally hard on the opposite failure: fake-medieval diction is a costume rather than a voice.

**The rule: take a line of dialogue out of the scene and ask whether a person could say it, unchanged, on a bus today. If yes, and the scene is not meant to be modern, the register has slipped.**

This campaign's declared register is not modern, and every item on its list is a *diction* instruction rather than a plot instruction. v4 §6 and §24: Giant Robo, not Evangelion. Big feelings said out loud. Loyalty declared rather than implied. Techniques announced by name. Water Margin's roll-call and epithets. A second register runs deliberately against the first — the Directorate's bureaucratic comedy, Form 12-B and "Records and Welfare haven't spoken since the flood" — so **register contrast is deliberate while register drift is the fault**, and v4 §22's Known Risk #5 supplies the check. The gap: nothing in `data/scene-briefs/` says which voice a scene is in, and the repo's other register decision is visual (`design/ux-doctrine.md` §7b, GLASS for Frame scale and PAPER for personnel). Whether the two axes should align has not been asked.

## 4. Sanderson's Laws, stated accurately

Brandon Sanderson's laws are the most-cited practitioner heuristics for magic-system design. **[C]** They are heuristics from one working novelist, published as essays — not theory, not tested, meant to be argued with. **First Law:** the ability to resolve conflict *satisfyingly* with magic is directly proportional to how well the reader understands that magic, so soft magic produces wonder but cannot solve the protagonist's problem without reading as an authorial rescue. **Second Law:** limitations are more interesting than powers. **Third Law:** expand what you already have before adding something new.

**The rule, from the Third Law: when a new problem needs a new answer, first ask which existing element could be extended into it.** This campaign has one accident, and the Watcher, the Frames, Inert 12, the Directorate and Cicada are all consequences of it rather than additions to it.

The First Law is satisfied where it matters: Sap's rule is stated to players in terms they can act on — every use permanently grows the Watcher, so the party's investigation is the enemy's supply line (v4 §8) — which makes spending it a real decision with a real price.

## 5. Consistency versus wonder, and where to spend rigour

Practitioners genuinely disagree here. **[Q]** A fully systematised world is legible and stakes-bearing and loses numinousness; the thing explained stops being awe-inspiring. An arbitrary world keeps its mystery and loses stakes, because a threat with no known limits cannot be measured, planned against, or beaten in a way that feels earned.

**The rule most working designers converge on: spend rigour where the players interact and preserve mystery in the deep background. Anything the players can make a decision against needs a rule. Anything they can only wonder at does not.**

This campaign has made that split correctly. On the rigour side, `bible/locations/deep-storage.md` says outright not to let elevated security become hand-waved — it is a checkable consequence of the party's actual Clearance rung under `design/strategy-layer.md`, not a vibe. On the mystery side, `bible/locations/the-rock.md` says what is held in the nine Frame-sized cells is open on purpose, calls it "the most tempting invention available here," and instructs the reader not to answer it. And `blank-inheritance-master-timeline.md` closes with **Gaps in the Record** — "not open design questions, genuine unknowns about the past itself" — a third category beside decided and to-be-decided that is rarer than it looks, and what stops a campaign explaining away its own mystery in the course of being tidy.

## 6. The worldbuilding trap, and a test for whether a fact earns its place

The trap is universal: building the world becomes more pleasant than writing the story, and the world grows while the story does not. **[C]** The **encyclopaedia problem** is its specific form — effort flows toward material that is satisfying to write and has no narrative use, while material that would change a scene goes unwritten. The cost is not the wasted hours; it is that those hours were not spent on the place the group returns to five times.

**The rule: a piece of worldbuilding earns its place when you can name the scene it changes and say what changes in it. "It makes the world feel deeper" is not an answer.** §2's payoff column is the stricter version where you can manage it: name the chapter.

This project has a real, enforced answer, and it is the most mature system of its kind here. `design/location-depth.md` makes depth a **declaration rather than an inference** — the user commissions a depth and `tools/location-sync/core/depth.py` reports what that commission owes — which catches both the location declared ready that turns out at the table to be a name and a shrug, and the three paragraphs written for a laundry nobody enters. `design/location-rank.md` adds the orthogonal axis: depth asks how much has been written, rank asks how much process the place earns. **The gap: this contract covers locations only.** Nothing states what a species, institution, faction, object or historical event owes at what depth, so that judgement is made fresh every time.

## 7. Cultures are not monolithic

The standard failure is the one-note culture: the warrior race, the merchant city, the scholarly elves. **[C]** It fails mechanically rather than politically — a culture with a single value has no internal conflict, so its members have nothing to argue about, so every scene involving it can only be about outsiders relating to it. It becomes scenery with opinions.

**The rule: a culture becomes real at the moment two of its members disagree about what it means** — not about tactics, but about the culture itself: what the tradition was for, who may interpret it, whether the past obliges the present.

The mitflits are this campaign's best worked example. v4 §9 states that freedom is their defining characteristic *and that it applies internally* — they will never vote as a bloc — and then makes the disagreement generational: the elders remember the workshop and know exactly what happens when someone finds their minds useful, so they refuse and go to extremes for pacifism, while the young do not remember, see a magnificent machine and a city that needs them, and volunteer eagerly, walking into the identical trap wearing a uniform. Then the line that makes it a character situation rather than a demographic one: **Anabala is being ignored by her own young, which is a sadder job than opposing the Directorate.** Cicada does the same one layer better, since `dossier-cicada.md`'s Faithful/Cadre split runs through individual people — Kiri runs the public recruitment shows *and* sits in the inner circle. The Directorate ships its disagreement as three `notablePoles`, which makes `third-npc-pole` in `open-questions.json` structurally a question about how much internal disagreement it is allowed. Offered, not resolved.

## 8. Institutions: self-deception is more believable than villainy

Institutions are the most under-built element in most worldbuilding and the most productive when built. **[C]** Three things make them work. **They optimise for their own continuity, not their stated purpose** — procedure outlives the reason for it. **The texture is paperwork, rank and procedure** — forms, queues, clearance ladders, blacked-out paragraphs, the specific things two departments will not do for each other. And **self-deception beats villainy**: a conspiracy needs someone to lie and keep everyone else lying, which is expensive and fragile, while institutional self-deception needs nobody to lie at all — one uncomfortable fact recorded accurately under a slightly wrong heading, then a century of people correctly consulting the heading. Diane Vaughan's account of the Challenger launch decision names the general form **normalization of deviance**: an anomaly recurs, is logged, causes no disaster this time, and is progressively reclassified as normal by ordinary people following correct procedure. **[T]** — grounded in one deep case study, not a measured effect.

**The rule: an institution's lies are mostly filing decisions. Write the filing decision, not the liar.**

*(The campaign's founding-charter example, which stood here, is under review — `open-questions.json#founding-charter-rethink`, LEDGER #36 — and is not restated. Removed from this document 2026-09-25.)*

The finest piece here is small and easy to miss. Every Gen 2 prototype shell eventually develops the same hole in the same position, **officially logged as metal fatigue** — and it is not fatigue, it is the core making a seat (`dossier-second-generation-frames.md`; narrowed to Gen 2 by LEDGER #32). A true observation, correctly recorded, filed under the wrong cause for decades because the right cause was unthinkable to the people filing it. That is normalization of deviance written as a maintenance log, and it is why Mystery Ledger row 4 works as a plant: the evidence was never hidden, it was categorised. The register holds it together — `ch1-v2` §3 has the institution **calcified rather than sinister**, and v4 §24 gives the guardrail's reason: the Directorate never has to become villainous, it only has to remain exactly what it has been.

## 9. Economics and logistics: where things come from

A world with no supply chain is a stage set. **[C]** The diagnostic questions are mundane and they are what make a setting feel inhabited: where does the food come from, who repairs the thing when it breaks, who pays, what is scarce, and what does the antagonist's operation cost to run.

**The rule: for anything the story leans on, name its supply. If it has no supply, it is scenery.**

Two supply chains are written properly here. **The repair economy** (`data/campaign/config.json`, `frameEconomy`) makes Frame HP a campaign resource rather than an encounter resource — Frames do not heal between fights, and climbing back up the damage-state track costs technician-days from a single shared weekly pool that Frame modification also draws on, which the file names as the primary economic tension of downtime. **The smuggling and funding column** (`dossier-cicada.md` §8) is a closed loop: a demonstration draws police and civil defence toward an apparent alien attack, crews lift chemical stock in bulk while they are occupied, precursors feed industrial synthesis, more Sap means more demonstrations, and the thefts are filed low-priority because the goods are unusual but harmless.

Two things follow that are craft rather than plot. **The investigation is the supply chain read backwards** — a supply chain written honestly generates its own investigation for free. And it produces the cruellest character beat available without inventing anything: if Nobu's criminal contacts anticipate his spontaneous acts of witness closely enough to plan around them, some demonstrations are timetabled by a logistics operation rather than by revelation. **The gap: the city's ordinary economy is not written anywhere** — where its food, fuel or municipal money come from, who pays for the Directorate, what a Contact costs the harbour in trade.

## 10. Deep time, and how a place shows its own history

A place shows its history through **successive uses**, not through a plaque. **[C]** The reliable moves are physical: a building repurposed for something it was not built for, a name that no longer describes what happens there, a repair visibly newer than what it repairs, a wall that used to be an exterior wall. Each is a fact about the present that only the past explains. A plaque can be ignored; a half-flight of stairs that exists for no reason cannot.

**The rule: write the alteration, not the history. The history is what the alteration implies.**

The location bible does this well. `bible/locations/main-school-complex.md` has the best single detail: two institutions merged into one block when the island ran out of room to expand sideways, and **a half-flight of stairs correcting a floor-level mismatch**, written in the file's own words as a physical detail rather than an abstract fact so a GM has something concrete to describe. `bible/locations/central-fortification.md` does the same at scale — the building sits on a real rock outcrop and the artificial island was reclaimed *around* it, which makes the building the reason the island exists. The city's forgetting is layered in too: an anthem with no composer credited, a redaction older than everyone alive. And the largest slow change has living witnesses, since magic draining from Golarion while industry grows into the vacuum has been watched personally by Anabala, Shucks and Rupiah (v4 §9). For how such details behave when a group is standing in the room, see [environmental-storytelling-and-place.md](environmental-storytelling-and-place.md).

## 11. Adapting a published setting: what you inherit and what you owe

When your world is loosely related to a published one you inherit two things: a body of assumed facts, and a body of assumed *tone* attached to them. **[C]** The facts are easy to change. The tone is the dangerous half, because it travels invisibly — a borrowed creature arrives with its published register still attached, and a writer who has not been told otherwise will reproduce that register while sincerely believing they are working from your notes.

**The rule: write the source's default down beside your divergence. A divergence is only legible against the thing it diverges from.** This project has that rule in its data model: the bible template (`ideas/story/bible/README.md`) makes **"Ingram's own take, and how it diverges from any real-world source"** a mandatory section of every entry, on the stated grounds that guessing the default instead of the divergence is precisely the failure the directory exists for.

The mitflits are the worked example. **The default**, written down rather than assumed: Paizo's mitflits are a fey-flavoured subtype of goblin, comic-relief adjacent, met with the contempt goblins usually get. **The divergence**, recorded in full in `bible/species/mitflits.md`: descendants of an alien robotic crew whose vessel crashed thousands of years ago, biology and magic and cybernetics muddling together and degrading over that span, with small communities surviving intact enough to keep the old ways — which is what makes finding a way home achievable rather than a fantasy. The craft point is what that reframe does structurally, and it is stronger than replacing a stereotype: **it supplies an in-universe cause for the stereotype.** The degradation is the real origin of their reputation as "the least of races," not an accident of outside perception. Deleting a source's unflattering default leaves a hole where the world's opinion used to be; explaining it keeps the opinion, removes its authority, and converts it into evidence of something done to them.

The file also records what it cost to learn this. On 2026-09-09 an opening beat was written with a mitflit trap on the harbour wall, bounty "same as rat traps," extrapolated from one glancing texture line toward the Paizo default; it was cut, along with an Episode One encounter against a mitflit nest, and the correction established a *fact* rather than a preference — mitflits have never been exterminated or treated as vermin at any point in this setting's history. **The transferable lesson, in the file's own terms: a standard vermin-clearance encounter against a sentient species is the same problem as the trap, not a smaller one, and does not get a pass for having been raised in a different conversation.** The governing line on inheritance is `dossier-first-impact.md` §13 — this setting started as Golarion and is **not bound by it**, so real Golarion canon and unedited stock SF2E flavour text are atmospheric backdrop, never binding fact.

**One contradiction, since fixed.** `blank-inheritance-v4.md` §24's closing paragraph used to describe the cast as including "a species everyone treats as vermin", a phrasing that predated the 2026-09-09 correction; it now reads "a species everyone finds a nuisance" (LEDGER #14). It stays named here because a residual line in a tier-1 source is exactly what a future writer reads as permission.

## 12. Negative space: writing down the prohibitions and the deferred names

Most setting bibles record only what is true, and that cannot prevent the commonest worldbuilding failure. **[C]** The failure is not contradiction of a stated fact — people check stated facts. It is a plausible-sounding extrapolation into a silence, made in good faith, toward whatever default the writer already carries. A bible of true statements has no defence against that, because the extrapolation contradicts nothing written.

**The rule: write down what must not be done, and attach the reason to it.** A prohibition is the only form that catches an error before it is made.

`ideas/story/bible/README.md` makes **"Tone and register — what NOT to do"** a mandatory section of every entry, and adds the clause that matters most: cite the mistake if there was one, because a rule with no story behind it is easy to forget the reason for. A bare rule would read "do not decide what is in the nine cells"; `bible/locations/the-rock.md` instead says it is the most tempting invention available here, which tells the next writer the rule *and* the shape of the pressure they will feel. The **"Appearances so far"** changelog is the other half, updated in the same commit as the content that revealed something. Deferred naming belongs to the same family: the naming placeholders, merged into `data/campaign/open-questions.json` on 2026-09-19, record each deferred name as an open entry — the Gap, the six kids and their Frames, the Watcher, the dig site, the excavated ship and the school are still open — rather than a working name typed into a data field. The value is underrated: **a working name typed into a data field hardens into canon by repetition.** Nobody decides to adopt it; it simply becomes what everything says, and by the time the real decision is made there are forty files to change and a table that has learned the placeholder. **Deferral is only safe when it is recorded as deferral.**

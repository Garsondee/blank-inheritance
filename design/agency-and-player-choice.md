# Agency and Player Choice

*Written 2026-09-13. Second of five narrative/game-design reference documents (see [narrative-theory-foundations.md](narrative-theory-foundations.md) for the series introduction and the narrative-science basics this one builds on). This one covers the specific problem of running a story that real people can meaningfully change: what "agency" actually is, what it costs to provide, and what this campaign is already doing right — the Mirror mechanic (v4 bible §6) is a genuinely rare, correctly-built example of a mechanism the academic literature spends a lot of effort warning people away from getting wrong. Nothing here resolves an open design question; where it bears on one (particularly the overworld/signup mechanic in `session-flow-and-overworld.md`), it says so and stops.*

---

## 1. The narrative paradox, and the four honest answers to it

Ruth Aylett named the foundational tension: an author's narrative structure and a player's real freedom pull against each other, and the more freedom you grant, the harder coherence gets to hold onto. **[T]** Every interactive-narrative system in existence is an answer to this, and there are only four families of answer:

| Family | Method | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| **Author the space** | Pre-write every legal path; the player selects among them | Combinatorial explosion; most authored content goes unseen |
| **Simulate and curate** | Model a world, let events emerge, a human selects and frames the output | Needs a real curator; low automatic emotional shaping |
| **Manage the drama** | A system reorders or selects content to steer toward an arc | Player sovereignty erodes if the steering is felt |
| **Mediate the player** | Silently prevent or paper over actions that would break the plot | Works only until detected, then it's worse than doing nothing |

The fourth family — silent mediation — is the "quantum ogre" problem discussed in [ttrpg-campaign-and-session-craft.md](ttrpg-campaign-and-session-craft.md) §6, and it's worth ruling out early: nothing in this campaign should ever quietly ensure an outcome while telling the players their choice mattered. The Bifurcation Points table (v4 bible §20) is the honest alternative done correctly — every fork lists **both** branches' actual downstream consequences, authored in advance, disclosed via play rather than concealed. That's family one (author the space) at the specific decision points that matter, without pretending the campaign is more open than it is everywhere else.

**The Mirror (v4 bible §6) is family three, correctly built.** A system that tracks a variable (mercy/lethality ratio) from actual play and reshapes future content (how the Watcher manifests) in response is, structurally, exactly what academic drama-management research calls an *experience manager* — and that line of research is full of warnings that such systems erode felt agency the moment players notice a hand steering them. The Mirror avoids this by being **diegetic and disclosed**: it isn't secretly adjusting difficulty behind the curtain, it's an in-fiction entity (the Watcher/Observer) that the text establishes conforms to the strongest coherent signal it receives — so when the players notice their own behavior changing what they're fighting, that noticing *is the payoff*, not a broken illusion. The bible states this directly: "This is diegetic. It is not the GM softening or hardening the campaign — it is the antagonist learning, exactly as established." That sentence is doing real theoretical work; it's the difference between a drama manager (family 3, usually resented once seen) and a **simulated agent whose behavior the player can legitimately learn to read and influence** (which reads as agency, because it is agency — over an NPC, not over the plot machinery).

## 2. Locating this campaign on the interactivity map

Marie-Laure Ryan's typology cross-classifies interactive experience on two axes: **internal/external** (is the participant embodied *in* the world, or observing it from outside?) and **exploratory/ontological** (can they only traverse what already exists, or can they change what's actually true?). **[T]** Four cells result, and tabletop RPG in general sits in the rarest and richest one: **internal-ontological** — players are embodied characters *and* their choices alter the storyworld's actual facts.

This campaign runs that structure **twice, simultaneously, at two different scales**, which is worth naming because it's an unusual and valuable structural feature, not an accident: each player is internal-ontological as a kid (embodied, ground-level, real stakes, real consequences to their choices) *and* internal-ontological as a Frame pilot (embodied at Frame scale, real stakes there too) — two separate internal-ontological registers per player, cross-linked by Resonance and Bleed. Most interactive fiction research studies systems with exactly one embodiment; this campaign's dual-PC structure means every scene design question ("does this belong to the kid-scale story or the Frame-scale story, and how does it cross?") is really a question about which internal-ontological register is live, which is precisely what the visual-identity split (glass register for Frame scale, paper for personnel scale, per `ux-doctrine.md` §7b) is already encoding at the interface level — the theory and the UI decision are describing the same underlying fact from two different directions.

The GM, by contrast, sits in **external-ontological**: outside the fiction, able to change anything. Most published findings about "player agency" study internal-exploratory or external-exploratory systems (hypertext fiction, walking simulators) where the participant can look but not change; those findings transfer to a TTRPG only partially, because a TTRPG's players are already in the highest-agency cell available. This is worth remembering specifically when reading agency research secondhand — a paper's headline finding about "players not noticing their choices didn't matter" was very likely tested in a much lower-agency medium than the one this campaign runs in, and the effect is measurably more fragile at a real table (see §4 below).

## 3. Agency is a matching problem, and the campaign already solves half of it

Wardrip-Fruin, Mateas, Dow, and Sali's *Agency Reconsidered* reframes agency away from "free will" questions entirely: **agency occurs when the actions a player desires are among the ones the system actually supports** — and the design's job is as much about *evoking the right desires* as it is about supporting actions. **[T]** A game with enormous freedom that invites the wrong ambitions produces *less* felt agency than a narrower game that precisely affords what it invites.

This is the theoretical grounding for a structural decision the v4 bible already made and stated clearly: **"the five ground roles are the five investigative lenses"** (§13) — each non-piloting kid owns a permanent, specific piece of the mystery (Sensor Operator reads correlation in the sky; Umbilical Control finds the column via power draw; Evacuation Marshal spots the demonstration/heist timing; Structural Command maps where apertures open; Signals Liaison hears the telling slip of language). This is agency-as-matching solved *by design*, not by accident: instead of five players wanting to investigate and one system (combat) supporting investigation, there are five separate, precisely-scoped affordances, one per role, each evoking a legible, achievable desire. The bible's own stated purpose — "which fixes the risk of non-piloting kids feeling like a consolation prize" — is exactly the failure mode Wardrip-Fruin's framework predicts when affordance and desire don't match, named and solved independently of the theory.

## 4. Meaningful play, and why the objective tracker is the mechanism, not decoration

Salen and Zimmerman's account of **meaningful play**: the relationship between an action and its outcome must be both **discernible** (the player can perceive the immediate result) and **integrated** (it affects the experience again, later). **[T]** These are two separate failure modes, and both are common in long-running campaigns specifically: consequences that exist in a GM's notes but were never actually perceived (not discernible), and consequences that were vivid in the moment but never resurfaced (not integrated).

`objectives.md` is, functionally, a discernibility-and-integration engine, and reading it against this framework makes clear why its specific design choices matter rather than being convenience features:

- **The log entry, added the moment something is *learned*, not when something merely *happens*** — this is the discernibility mechanism. A fight that produces no log entry produced an outcome the player cannot cite later; the rule "if a player would write it down, log it" is a direct test for discernibility.
- **"An objective with one learnable is not an arc, it is an errand. Two is thin. Three or more and it has a shape"** — this is an integration requirement stated as an authoring rule: a single learnable can't be revisited, referenced, or built on, so it never gets a second chance to matter; three or more creates the possibility that learnable #1 gets recontextualized by learnable #3, which is integration happening structurally.
- **The GM's "Might go" column, computed from "an objective whose remaining learnables appear in no upcoming scene"** — this is dormancy detection for *integration specifically*: an objective can be perfectly discernible (every learnable so far was logged clearly) and still fail meaningful play if nothing is scheduled to ever read those learnables again.

The takeaway for improvised content specifically: **when inventing something at the table, the two questions that matter are "will they notice this happened" and "will anything later care that it did."** A vivid scene that fails either question is a scene the table will forget regardless of how well it played in the room.

## 5. The illusion-of-agency finding, and why it should be read as encouraging rather than cynical

Fendt, Harrison, Ware, Cardona-Rivera, and Roberts (ICIDS 2012, Best Paper) built a *non-branching* text game that simulated branching by giving players choices followed by immediate acknowledgement text, and found that in most conditions players reported **no significantly different sense of agency** than players given a genuinely branching version. **[E]**

Read this precisely, because both an over-reading and a dismissal are available and both are wrong:

- It does **not** mean choices don't matter. It means **acknowledgement does most of the felt work in the short term**, in a text medium, over a short session, measured by self-report immediately afterward.
- It **does** mean that, word for word, a well-placed line of acknowledgement is usually a better investment than a whole new branch — which is direct support for how cheap and effective the v4 bible's own small callbacks already are (Taka's disbelief in Chapter 3 costing him institutional credibility that a later scene can reference; Ume's private grief over Nozomi surfacing as a specific ally motivation in Chapter 4). These aren't branches. They're acknowledgements, and per this finding they're pulling more than their weight.
- The finding is **durationally fragile** in exactly the direction this campaign runs against it: tested in a 20-minute, one-shot, text-only condition. At a real table, over many real sessions, with players who compare notes, test the world deliberately, and remember specifics across weeks — the gap between "acknowledged" and "actually branching" degrades far faster than in the study's own conditions. **[Q — extrapolated, not directly tested]**

The synthesis, and the actual guidance: **buy acknowledgement cheaply and constantly; buy real branching (a Bifurcation Point, §1 above) only at the handful of places the campaign is actually built to diverge at.** This campaign already draws that line correctly — the Thread Grid's individual cells are almost entirely acknowledgement-and-inflection (a scene colors differently based on what's true, without the graph itself forking), while the seven Bifurcation Points (v4 bible §20) are the real, disclosed, structurally-different forks. That ratio — many cheap acknowledgements, few expensive real branches — is exactly what the economics in this section argue for, and it should be actively protected against pressure to add "more choices" everywhere, which usually means diluting real branches with false ones rather than adding acknowledgement where it's actually cheap and valuable.

## 6. Storylets — the theory's name for what a side plot and a learnable already are

A **storylet** (a concept formalized across quality-based narrative design and academic surveys of it) has exactly three parts: **content** (the material itself), **prerequisites** (conditions determining when it's available), and **effects** (what changes in the world once it's played). **[T]** This is a precise, useful vocabulary for two things this campaign already builds constantly:

**A side plot is a storylet.** `objectives.md` §2's four-field authoring discipline — *opens on* (prerequisite), *learnable* (content, delivered incrementally), *closes when* (a completion condition), *suggested resolution* (a draft effect on the world/narrative, revised at play time) — is a storylet definition with the vocabulary changed. Naming it this way surfaces one thing worth checking deliberately: the academic literature on storylet systems is unanimous that **reuse semantics** need to be decided explicitly (does a storylet fire once, ever, or can it recur?) — and the campaign doesn't currently seem to need this, since side plots are mostly one-shot narrative arcs rather than repeatable content, but it's worth having an explicit answer ready the day a repeatable downtime storylet (a recurring Sync Training flavor scene, say) gets proposed, rather than discovering the gap live.

**The intake quiz (v4 bible §20) is a storylet-seeding mechanism, and an unusually good one.** Each of its eight questions is designed to produce exactly one of three outputs — an Anchor, a side plot seed, or a vulnerability — which means every player answer is, functionally, a self-authored storylet prerequisite waiting for content. This is a genuinely elegant solution to the hardest problem in storylet-based design (where do enough prerequisites-with-content come from to avoid the pool feeling thin), because it outsources prerequisite generation to the people who care most about the answers being right: the players themselves.

**The "convergent" side plot type — "starts solo, pulls the group in, and touches a main thread"** (v4 bible §20) — is the storylet-chaining pattern: a personal storylet whose *effect* is to unlock or connect to a thread-level storylet. The explicit rule that "every side plot should touch at least one of the six threads somewhere, so nothing is ever a detour" is a soundness constraint on the whole storylet graph — it guarantees no personal content becomes an orphaned dead end, which is the single most common failure mode of storylet-heavy design when that constraint isn't stated.

## 7. What this suggests, carefully, for the open overworld/signup question

`session-flow-and-overworld.md` records a genuinely unresolved design question: a sign-up/multi-group/time-segment mechanic with, as that document states, "no existing mechanical precedent" in the repo. This is not the place to resolve it — that document already correctly defers it — but two things from this section are worth having in view *while* it gets decided, without deciding it here:

- A map full of clickable pins is, structurally, a **storylet pool rendered spatially**: each pin is a prerequisite (a location/time availability) gating a content unit. The design questions that document raises (does a pin replace or sit atop the Contact Pool's single d20 trigger; what happens if a group doesn't finish before the segment advances) are exactly the reuse-and-availability questions storylet systems have to answer, and reading the existing academic vocabulary for them (a storylet can be single-use, repeatable, or cooldown-gated; availability can be a hard boolean or a soft "salience" ranking) may make the option space easier to lay out for a decision, even though it doesn't supply the decision itself.
- Whatever the mechanic ends up being, §5 above argues for keeping most of what a segment offers as **acknowledgement-and-inflection content** (a pin that recolors based on what's true) with only a few pins per arc functioning as genuine Bifurcation Points — the same ratio argument as §5, applied to spatial content instead of scene content.

## 1. The Thesis

### The thread

**Nobody in this campaign has ever done anything evil.**

A gnoll fumbles a catch. A junior scientist knocks over a bunsen burner. An assistant cannot make himself speak without proof. A scientist asks one good question and does not follow up. A cabal of frightened people bury a finding because they have realised what they are. A police officer is confronted by a disfigured thing lurching toward him and does what almost anyone would do.

Every catastrophe is **an ordinary person doing an ordinary human thing badly** — flinching, hesitating, staying quiet — and then **building the rest of their life on top of that moment.**

### The mechanism

**Everything in this setting becomes what it was mistaken for.**

- The Watcher was mistaken for a monster, so it inferred that being a monster is its function. *(Note, 2026-09-24, LEDGER #31: this still holds for its early stage. Its objective, to multiply, expand and keep observing, is its own and never changes. Proposal, same entry: what it infers from how it is treated shapes its methods, not its goal; see `ideas/story/dossier-observer.md` §6 and §16.)*
- Manako was mistaken for a madman, so he became one.
- The mitflits were mistaken for tools, so they were used as tools.
- Shinobu was mistaken for a contaminant, caged for it, and is slowly becoming something.
- The Directorate diagnosed itself as *children with guns*, buried the diagnosis, and became it. *(rests on the founding charter, under review — LEDGER #36)*
- The Frames are mistaken for weapons. They are alive. *(Corrected again 2026-09-24, LEDGER #34: this said "They are people", which belonged to the version where the cores were transformed crew. The core is not a living mitflit. Whether anyone is in there at all rests on the open question of a piece of Mother in every Gen 2, `open-questions.json#mother-in-gen2`.)* *(Gen 2 only since LEDGER #32: Gen 1 Frames are Golarion machines with no living core. Corrected 2026-09-24, LEDGER #34: this said the Gen 2 core's nature stays open. It is biomechanical mitflit technology, living flesh melded with technology, and not a living mitflit; see §3.)*
- The kids are mistaken for pilots. They are a substitute part — and they will become pilots regardless.

**Being seen wrongly is worse than not being seen at all**, which inverts the campaign's apparent moral. Cicada says hide. The Watcher wants to be seen. *(True of its early stage, up to the Envoy; for what it learned there, see `ideas/story/dossier-observer.md` §8.)* The evidence says neither is the answer: **see accurately.**

### The other mechanism

**The Directorate's actual founding sin is not cruelty. It is information suppression as a reflex.**

Every secret in this campaign is something somebody decided other people could not handle knowing — and every one of those decisions was made by a person who was, at the time, frightened rather than malicious. They buried their own diagnosis (*children with guns*) *(rests on the founding charter, under review — LEDGER #36)*. They buried Manako's correct findings. They buried what the pilot cavity actually is *(Gen 2's, since LEDGER #32; §3)*. They hold Shinobu without ever quite admitting what she is becoming. It is the same reflex, exercised by different hands, for two hundred years.

### The image

Everything here is **a thing in the wrong container**, and the story is about whether anything ever gets out.

- A biomechanical core in a riveted steel shell that does not fit it. *(A Gen 2 Frame, since LEDGER #32. A Gen 1 Frame has no core.)*
- Mitflits reverted from a star-faring form into small frightened bodies. *(The wild mitflits of Golarion; the High mitflit is the home-world form, LEDGER #31.)*
- Anabala: two beings sharing one body through the ages. *(Was "for centuries"; LEDGER #31 makes her a very early mitflit who has lived through the ages because of the demon.)*
- The Hand: detached, living independently.
- Rupiah, forced back into a role his family chose.
- Hyuck, a person who was made into a weapon.
- The Watcher: an awareness with no hull.

Nothing in the setting needs to state any of this. It only needs to keep being true.

## 3. The Frames

*(Rewritten 2026-09-24, LEDGER #32. This section used to open: "The aperture at Kortos bisected a machine buried deeper than dwarven caverns — a perfect cross-section, the most legible possible gift", which humanity then reverse-engineered, and it gave every Frame a living core. **There is no bisected specimen, and Gen 1 Frames have no living core.** The core, the seat it makes and everything that follows from them now belong to **Gen 2 only**. Never confuse the two generations. This also settles the #31 flag that stood here, about the deep-buried machine and a possible teleportation accident.)*

### The rupture opened the ship

First Impact did not cut a buried machine in half. **It opened up the inner layers of the generation ship itself** (LEDGER #32). Scientists and magic-users descended on the site, terrified of another rupture. Adventurers came for valuables. It became a very dangerous place, and **the organisations around Kortos banded together: the Directorate was born to keep the site secure, to prevent a future rupture, and to stop the technology uncovered there from disrupting the world** (§5, §14). **The technology leaked outwards anyway.** Anything in this setting too advanced for its era, the phones included, is ship technology that got out of the Kortos site (LEDGER #32).

### Gen 1: Golarion machines

**Gen 1 Frames are Golarion technology only**, informed by looking at the godlike technology inside the opened ship (LEDGER #32). Humanity rebuilt what it saw in the only vocabulary it had. **They are not biomechanical.** They are mechanical: clankers, and hard to work with. **No living core, and no seat that shapes itself.** Every Frame the party starts on is one of these.

That is why Frames look like a 1950s army got hold of something and did its level best. Riveted plate. Boiler technology. Catwalks and ladders, because human crews have to get up there. A radar dish, because they saw a sensory organ they could not comprehend and rendered it as the only remote-sensing device they knew how to build. Roundels and a flag on a pole, because these are state assets and procurement insisted. **The original can look like anything** — blue, organic, wrong, nothing like its children.

Pilots are seventeen for one reason only: **it is the age the charter permits conscription.** Law, not geometry — the horror was always procedural. That holds for both generations. *(rests on the founding charter, under review — LEDGER #36)* *(open — Ingram's call: "conscription" here against LEDGER #26, where candidates apply to the Directorate or are streamed to it.)*

**No berserk.** Nothing in any Frame, Gen 1 or Gen 2, takes control from its pilot. Mitflit technology has no reason to go berserk, and nothing in this campaign takes control of a character away from their player (LEDGER #31).

### Gen 2: mitflit technology

**Gen 2 Frames are Anabala building mechs from actual mitflit technology** (LEDGER #32). Built on the real thing, they are **biomechanical**, and flying one is **more like being inside a giant humanoid** (Ingram: "more like EVA and less Giant Robo" — the machine, not the tone; §6). They are rare, they ask the pilot for "the right DNA or the right something", and they are offered to the party at the end of Chapter 4 (LEDGER #31; `ideas/story/dossier-second-generation-frames.md`). **Everything from here to the end of §3 is Gen 2 only** (LEDGER #32).

*(2026-09-24, LEDGER #34:)* **Gen 2 is built from the generation ship's technology**: the original ship, the First Impact crash site at Kortos, which the Directorate holds and keeps restricted from the rest of Golarion (LEDGER #20, #34). **Anabala works with the Directorate, and has her own ultimate objective: using mitflit technology to get a way home for her people. Building Gen 2 for the Directorate was her price for access to the original ship's technology**, which the Directorate keeps highly restricted from the rest of Golarion so it can't destabilise the world (§14). These mechs aren't usable by just anyone ("The cavity conforms", below). **Wild mitflits are uncomfortable around Gen 2 Frames**, and mitflits don't instantly understand where Gen 2 comes from. *(Proposal, LEDGER #34, veto freely: they feel something of their ancestors' ship in a machine made of living flesh, and can't say what.)*

### Two modes, one hole

Each Gen 2 Frame is a **biomechanical core at full mitflit scale** — a living, motivating body — inside a **terrestrial steel exoskeleton**. The Directorate has tried many shell geometries. Some work. Some fail catastrophically. **All of them, eventually, develop the same hole in the same position.**

They call it metal fatigue. It is not fatigue. **The core is making a seat.**

- **Autonomous mode** exists but is unusable — not for want of engineering, but because the "communication technology" required is a *language*, and only the mitflits have it.
- **Direct control** is the fallback: override the core and drive the body. The only mode humanity has, and it is a violation.

## 6. Tone, and the Mirror

### The register

**Giant Robo, not Evangelion.** The bones of this campaign are warm: nobody in it has ever done anything evil, everyone is doing their best, and the whole catastrophe is a chain of ordinary human misunderstandings. That is a *hopeful* premise. Play it that way.

Big feelings, said out loud. Loyalty declared rather than implied. Grief that is loud and then passes. People who like each other and say so.

**Humour is load-bearing, not tolerated.** The players will make jokes; the campaign should hand them material rather than fight them for the room. The comic vectors are already built in and should be used freely:

- **The stray** (`ideas/story/dossier-observer.md` §17) — an invisible companion that follows one PC around waiting to be fed. Inherently funny and quietly devastating at once. This is the tonal keystone. It is a small, broken-off piece of the Watcher; how it develops is open (LEDGER #32).
- **The mitflits** — savant-brilliant, socially catastrophic, magnificently rude. Comedy that turns on a sixpence into pathos.
- **The Watcher's early incompetence** — it fights a statue. It loses to a reflection. This is genuinely funny and must be allowed to be.
- **Institutional absurdity** — forms, requisitions, an exam timetable that continues through an alien invasion because nobody has the authority to cancel it.
- **The magic show** — theatre, patter, spectacle.

**Death is rare, meaningful, and never horrific.** No casual casualties, no atrocity for texture, no on-screen cruelty. Where people die, they are named and mourned. Most of the named cast can survive the entire campaign (§21).

**The reward is bonds.** Not survival, not victory. What the players should have at the end is each other.

### The Mirror

*(Trimmed 2026-09-25: the Mirror now lives in `ideas/story/dossier-observer.md` §16. How the party fights teaches the Watcher its methods, as probe or as weapon, never its objective. Claude playing the Watcher, and the Watcher's file, are in that dossier's §2 and §13. Two things said only here are kept below.)*

**This is diegetic.** It is not the GM softening or hardening the campaign — it is the antagonist learning. The register belongs to the table, and the table earns it, which makes the campaign's central mechanic and its tone control the same thing.

**Implementation note:** the Claude Code layer should track a mercy/lethality ratio from session logs alongside the comprehension rung, and shape manifestation design from both. Tone is a tracked variable.

## 8. The Chemical

**Inert 12** — the Directorate's catalogue designation. Filed as noble, non-reactive, useless. The word *inert*, sitting in the official name forever, is an irony nobody there hears any more.

**Sap** — what Cicada calls it, and eventually what everyone calls it. Cicada nymphs drink sap for years underground before they emerge. Manako chose the name deliberately and has never explained the joke.

### Food, not a phase agent, and not fuel

Sap is **nanotech feedstock**: concentrated raw building material for nanobots, "a sort of nanotech food" (Ingram, LEDGER #31). **It is the generation ship's stock for its repair nanites** (LEDGER #32; a #31 proposal, now canon). The cloud uses it to **rapidly multiply**, and it is what gives the Watcher's children manifestation. *(Corrected 2026-09-24, LEDGER #31: this line used to say Sap feeds the cloud **energy**. It is building material, not fuel.)* Visibility is not the effect; density is the effect, and visibility is what density looks like.

**Every use permanently grows the Watcher.** The extra mass does not evaporate when visibility fades — it disperses into the cloud and stays.

1. **The escalation curve has a mass term, not only a comprehension term.** More substrate, more capability, and it grows because people keep feeding it.
2. **Cicada are not revealing it. They are farming it.** Every demonstration is a meal. The citywide simultaneous emergence is a mass feeding event. Manako believes he is waking the city to a god. He has spent decades *making* one.
3. **The party's investigation is the enemy's supply line.** Every dose used to see with strengthens the thing.
4. **The lab where the stray comes through is permanently dense.** A haunted room the clean-up never actually cleaned.

### Not fuel: exotic matter, and the two Impacts *(added 2026-09-24, LEDGER #31)*

**Exotic matter is the fuel, and Sap is not.** Don't let the two blur. The four were fetching exotic fuel for the jungle mitflits when First Impact happened (§14; `ideas/story/dossier-first-impact.md` §3): fuel for the shuttle buried deep under the rock of Kortos, which they never reached (LEDGER #32). A breached exotic-matter container causes a **subspace rupture**: **First Impact** at Kortos, and **Second Impact**, the name for the rupture in Kurose. **Second Impact is unavoidable**, a beat that puts the whole world at risk and starts the campaign's next part. Its exact cause stays flexible, like everything not yet played.

### The runaway *(added 2026-09-24, LEDGER #31)*

**The runaway begins when the Watcher's own children, given new purposes, start producing their own Sap.** Ingram: "The Watcher learns." *(Proposal, same entry: its supply chain grows more autonomous over the campaign. First a human it taught to make Sap (Manako, step 6 of his fall, `ideas/story/dossier-cicada.md` §3), then an industrial plant (the column), then its own children. The runaway threshold is the moment it no longer needs any human to feed it; see the Mass Clock, `ideas/story/dossier-observer.md` §16.)*

### Rarity

**It is extraordinarily rare, and the Directorate knows it.** The last remaining sample walked out of the lab in Manako's pocket the day he lost his job — nobody bothered reporting it stolen, since it wasn't interesting enough. **That single sample is what he reverse-engineered everything from.**

### The cost of seeing

Being exposed makes a person **legible to it, permanently.** Every revelation in this campaign is purchased with being watched more closely — the being-seen question as a resource decision, not only a theme.

## 9. The Mitflits

**Where they come from** (LEDGER #31): the mitflits are descended from the crew of the generation ship, which crashed on Kortos tens of thousands of years ago. **Wild mitflits** are those descendants: quite goblinoid now, though cute rather than gruesome, with bright, intelligent eyes (LEDGER #15), and **the only mitflits on Golarion**. **High mitflits** are how they looked on the home world: blue-skinned, elegant, unblemished and aloof, like high-fantasy elves. *(The older name "ascended" is retired, LEDGER #31.)* The home world itself is unique and custom, and high-level content: a journey there, and a quest to solve an extreme problem on it.

**Freedom is their defining characteristic**, and it applies internally — they will never vote as a bloc. History: brilliant minds, no social armour, talked into inventing weapons for a greedy lord who grew rich on it. *(The lord is **William Charles Baldwin** of the old campaign; the four rescued mitflits who were being made to build weapons at Baldwin Manor. LEDGER #31, and §14.)* **He is still alive, and mitflits are still making his weapons** (GM-only, LEDGER #32; §14).

**Their horrible characteristic** (Ingram, LEDGER #32): mitflits are **extremely helpful when given the right tasks, and fascinated by the production of technology.** It is what makes them so easy to use, then and now.

**The split is generational.** The elders remember the workshop and know exactly what happens when someone finds their minds useful; they refuse, and go to extremes for pacifism. The young do not remember; they see a magnificent machine and a city that needs them, and volunteer eagerly, walking into the identical trap wearing a uniform. **Anabala is being ignored by her own young**, which is a sadder job than opposing the Directorate.

### The sit-in

**Bobbin (the Elder)** leads it: a mitflit enters a Frame **specifically so it cannot be used.** A general strike, indefinite, non-violent, and the Directorate cannot extract them without killing them. Aimed squarely at the machines the party needs, which puts the players in direct opposition to people who are ethically in the right. **Do not resolve that discomfort quickly.** *(Open, LEDGER #32: whose machine Bobbin sits in. Only Gen 2 has a living core (§3), so the answer bears on why he cannot be pulled out alive.)*

**And they will not explain themselves.** Discussing mitflit technology is subject to the same social taboo this city applies to magic: wise to conceal, vulgar to flaunt. Prise it out of Bobbin and he will note, without drama, that mitflit technology is many times more powerful than terrestrial magic — **because magic is slowly draining from Golarion**, and industry has grown into the vacuum. That is the whole reason this world went from medieval to smokestacks in two centuries, and every long-lived character (Anabala, Shucks, Rupiah) has personally watched it happen.

## 13. Competing Accounts, and the Museum Piece

The conference is not run as a flashback. It is reconstructed the way everything in this campaign should be: **through contradictory testimony.** Rika, Nobu, Hori, Shinobu, retired Directorate staff, cultists in cells, mitflit elders — every one of them holds a piece and colours it with their own stake, and the party is told plainly that everything anyone has to say might matter.

**This is the theme running as a game mechanic** — a story about who gets believed, played by deciding who to believe.

### Five trails through the mystery

Any PC can pick up any of these:

- **The sky.** Where attention pools is where things appear.
- **The power.** Industrial synthesis needs enormous power, so municipal load going somewhere unaccounted for is what finds **the column.**
- **The timing.** Where every response unit goes, and when, gives away **the demonstration/heist timing correlation.**
- **The ground.** Apertures open where the ground has been *watched*.
- **The word.** A mitflit says *the Observer* in that flat technical way, and nobody in the room is frightened.

*(Cut systems stripped 2026-09-24, LEDGER #32 N14.)*

**Foundry implementation:** each NPC carries a numbered topic list; when the players raise a subject, the GM ticks it and delivers that version. Ticked topics are recorded automatically and inform later sessions — including, usefully, what the party has never thought to ask.

**The museum piece:** a fragment of the generation ship's original navigation and sensor system (LEDGER #31) that never got pulverised. Small, coherent, sane, mislabelled in a display case for two centuries. Reachable as a side quest or a school trip. **It remembers what the system was for** — and given what the Envoy taught the Watcher about its own purpose, that fragment is the only surviving evidence that the pattern it inferred was *wrong*. **Mother may still be alive in it** (Ingram's "perhaps", LEDGER #32): the ship's mind (proposal, LEDGER #31), weakened, unable to control the Watcher directly, but still a valuable witness and source of information for the players. **Maybe** (Ingram's word, LEDGER #34) a small part of her is also in every Gen 2 Frame, the "cerebral glue" that makes the technology work; that is open (§3; `open-questions.json#mother-in-gen2`). ## 16. The Threads

Six recurring lines, running in parallel through every chapter rather than one per chapter. Each has a question and a currency — useful vocabulary for tagging any scene, in play or in prep.

- **The War.** Contact, Frame combat, the escalation ladder. *Currency:* mecha action, tactical stakes. *Question:* can the city be defended?
- **The Observed.** Personal surveillance, the stray, Shinobu, treated lenses. *Currency:* dread, intimacy. *Question:* what does it mean to be seen?
- **The Cult.** Cicada's demonstrations, the dossiers, the magic show, the column, the heist loop, Manako's revenge missions. *Currency:* investigation, moral complexity. *Question:* who deserves to be believed?
- **The Mitflits.** Arrival, Anabala, the sit-in, generational politics, the core reveal (Gen 2's core, LEDGER #32). *Currency:* found family, ethical conflict with allies. *Question:* what do you owe someone you are using to save yourself?
- **The Founders.** The old party, in flashback; institutional secrets. *Currency:* glory curdling into complicity. *Question:* were they right, and does it matter now?
- **School.** The downtime economy, ordinary teenage life under pressure. *Currency:* small stakes that matter enormously. *Question:* what does the city owe them back?

**School is live in every chapter.** It is the thread most likely to quietly atrophy under table pressure, and the one that makes the other half of the game matter. *(Moved here 2026-09-25 from the removed §17.)*

## 20. The Intake, and the Side Plots

### The intake quiz

*(Superseded 2026-09-18 as the content of the induction terminal's intake pages: `design/induction-terminal.md` and `design/induction-intake-audit.md` §6 hold what replaced it. These eight questions are its ancestor.)*

Session zero, before anything else. Each player answers for their kid, and **every answer becomes a side plot seed or a vulnerability.** Written down, kept, and mined for the whole campaign.

1. **Who do you love, and what would you never tell them?**
2. **What are you good at that has nothing to do with piloting?** — this probably becomes a side plot.
3. **What did you want to be, before the draft notice?** *(open — Ingram's call: "draft" here against LEDGER #26, where candidates apply to the Directorate or are streamed to it.)*
4. **Who in this city owes you something? Who do you owe?**
5. **What is the smallest thing that would make today a good day?** — the cheapest, most reliable emotional lever in the campaign.
6. **What do you not want the Directorate to know about you?**
7. **Name someone who thinks you are better than you are.**
8. **What do you find funny?** — a real question. It tells the GM what register that player wants.

*(Cut systems stripped 2026-09-24, LEDGER #32 N14.)*

### Three kinds of side plot

- **Solo** — one PC only. Their family, their debt, their secret. Run in downtime slices, short, frequent, low-stakes-high-meaning. These are where the campaign earns the right to hurt later.
- **Open** — the whole group can attend. A festival, a favour, a job, a rescue. These build the found family, which is the campaign's actual reward.
- **Convergent** — starts solo, pulls the group in, and **touches a main thread.** A PC's family debt turns out to be owed to somebody in Nobu's network. A sibling's friend went to a magic show. **This is the type to write most of.**

**Rule:** every side plot should touch at least one of the six threads (§16) somewhere, so nothing is ever a detour. The player experiences a personal story; the campaign experiences a delivery mechanism.

### Feedback loop

The GM writes up what landed and what didn't after each session. Those notes drive the next batch of side plots. **Prediction is a courtesy; responsiveness is the actual strength.** Nothing below is fixed, and side plots should regularly overtake the main line in importance for whole sessions at a time.

## 22. Known Risks

1. **Scope.** Six players, twelve sheets, six threads, three flashback layers, a side-plot engine, a procedural Contact system, a dossier metagame, and an AI antagonist reading logs. The threat is not a weak story — it is the apparatus collapsing under its own weight. Decide which two subsystems get cut first under pressure.
2. **Thread juggling.** Six threads is a lot to carry live. The twenty-chapter outline's one-page table and antagonist weave (its §4 and §10) reduce it to a checklist, but if a chapter's beats stop landing as *connected*, the mystery reads as noise rather than a converging picture.
3. **Almost everything good is currently a secret.** Chapters 1–2 must work as good mecha episodes and good school drama with none of the reveals helping.
4. **The truth makes the sky worse, not better.** *(Rewritten 2026-09-24, LEDGER #31. This risk used to read "The benign reveal deflates the sky": once the players learned the Watcher isn't evil, the cosmic-horror engine would switch off around Ch 5–6.)* That no longer happens. The Watcher is still not evil, and it is still the adversary: an objective pursued without limit, which learns from the party and reads what they write. Learning the truth makes it more frightening, not less. The new risk is the opposite one. A premise built to spook the table, with mass surveillance and runaway agentic systems (real concerns these players are already aware of), can drift into the grimness of Risk 5. Keep the warmth around it (§6), and keep real levers in the party's hands: the Mirror (proposal), Manako alive, and the home-world solution.
5. **Tone drift toward grimness.** This document has been rewritten once already for exactly this. The material *wants* to curdle — it is full of institutional cruelty and forty-year griefs. **Check the register every few sessions against §6**, and if a run of sessions has contained no jokes, no warmth, and no small good days, that is a fault, not a mood.
6. **Side plots outcompeting the main line.** This is mostly fine and occasionally correct. It becomes a problem only if the Mass Clock never advances and the campaign loses its spine.

## 24. Register Notes

**Giant Robo.** Everyone means it. Nobody deflects. Grief is loud, loyalty is declared aloud, techniques are announced by name.

**Water Margin.** The roll-call, the epithets, the outlaw band more righteous than the state it serves, and the ending where the state cannot be trusted with what they built. The Directorate never has to become villainous. It only has to remain exactly what it has been.

**The comic-to-poignant gradient.** The Watcher's incompetence in Ch 1–2 is not a joke at the audience's expense, and it is not a trap either. It is the setup for the kindest payoff available: **it was never stupid. It was new, and it was drowning, and nobody had ever spoken to it.**

**And the last word on register:** this is a story where six teenagers, a species everyone finds a nuisance, a disgraced doctor's severed hand, and a frightened cloud of broken machinery all turn out to have been doing their best the whole time. Play it warm. Let them joke. Let them win something.

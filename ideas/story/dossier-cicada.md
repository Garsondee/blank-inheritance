## 1. Summary

**Cicada is a public religious movement with a clandestine operational core, and it is a real SF2E antagonist faction.** In public, at its demonstrations, it does not lie, does not hide, and does not recruit by deception — most of the broad membership is guilty of nothing but having seen something real and believed their own eyes. But it is also a revolutionary, clandestine organisation with a mission and assets it will use force to protect, run by an elderly disgraced chemist who believes he is a servant of something enormous and is in fact its steering and its supply (§4).

*(Corrected 2026-09-24, LEDGER #31: this line said "its operator". The Watcher is now the campaign's adversary in its own right, with an objective of its own — to multiply, to expand, to keep observing — and Claude plays it. Manako's signal steers how it goes after that objective, and it feeds through him. §4 carries the full reframe.)*

**Hold both things at once.** The public wing is sympathetic and non-violent by doctrine. The operational core — the Cadre (§2) — will fight the party, sometimes seriously, and some of its inner circle are happy to help Manako with things that are personal to him rather than doctrinal. Not evil for its own sake. Absolutely willing to hurt people for the cause, and for him.

**Capture over kill is a design incentive, not a cult trait.** A live, interrogated Cadre member is worth more to the party than a dead one — names, sites, the revenge list, the funding trail. That is a reason for players to fight carefully. It is not a reason for the cult to fight softly.

## 2. The Organisation

### The name

Behavioural, not symbolic. **Cicada nymphs spend years underground and then emerge everywhere at once, making an enormous amount of noise.** That is the organisation's operating model and it is not a metaphor.

**They call themselves Cicada. So does everybody else.** The movement is entirely open about its identity and its goals — it has to be, since its whole purpose is to make people look. What stays hidden is the column, the funding, and the criminal logistics.

### The demonstrations

Simultaneous releases of Sap in crowded public places. Publicly interpreted as chemical attacks.

**Nobody dies.** The compound is not dangerous. Injuries come from crowd movement, never from the substance — and that anomaly is itself the first clue available to a careful investigator. *A terror campaign with a zero body count is not a terror campaign.*

**What Sap is — and is not** *(corrected 2026-09-24, LEDGER #31 items 7–8, which are canon)*. **Sap is not fuel.** It is nanotech feedstock: concentrated raw building material for the Watcher's nanomachines — **the generation ship's own stock for its repair nanites** — catalogued by the Directorate as **Inert 12**. A release gives the swarm overhead something to build with, and what it builds is manifestation — visible, briefly solid; its children, the Claimants, are made the same way. **The fuel in this story is the exotic matter**, a different substance entirely: a breached exotic-matter container is what tears open a subspace rupture — First Impact at Kortos, and **Second Impact**, the rupture in Kurose. Never let the two blur. **The runaway begins when the Watcher's own children, given new purposes, start producing their own Sap** (§8). *(Corrected 2026-09-24, LEDGER #32 item 9: that Sap was the ship's repair-nanite stock was a #31 proposal, and is now canon.)*

They are not terrorists. Some of what they do is dangerous and occasionally fatal in its consequences, but terror is never the goal. **Witness is the goal.**

### The stencils

A cicada, sprayed neat, **always at head height, always on a wall people stand in front of** — tram stops, queues, outside picture houses, market corners. Never facing a road. They mark places where crowds gather, which is to say: places worth demonstrating at.

Marine primer, chandler's stock, three suppliers in the city, all harbour-side.

Some walls have been stencilled and painted over repeatedly for a decade or more.

### Liturgy

> *Believe me now?*

That is the whole theology. The conference was attempt one; every manifestation since is the same act at larger scale. Running underneath it is a second and gentler motive Manako rarely says aloud: **it is in agony, and quieting the city is the only relief available.**

### The Faithful, and the Cadre

**Cicada is not one thing. Two very different groups share the name.**

**The Faithful** — the broad public membership, recruited through the magic-show circuit (§7). They believe the mission is witness. At staged public events — demonstrations, ceremonies — their doctrine is deliberate non-resistance. Standing still while arrested, helping a stranger up while being taken into custody, is not weakness. **It is the point.** A martyr who does not fight makes a better argument than a fanatic who does, and the Faithful know it. Most of them have never held a weapon in Cicada's service and never will.

**The Cadre** — the operational wing around Manako. They run the column (§8), coordinate with the criminal supply chain, guard sensitive sites, and carry out the countersurveillance and enforcement the movement needs to survive underground for decades. **They will fight, and some of them will fight to kill.** They do not think of themselves as villains — they see themselves as a vanguard protecting something sacred from an institution that buried the truth once already and would happily do it again. That self-image does not make them safe to be around, and they should be run as a genuine, dangerous SF2E antagonist faction: trained, armed, disciplined, and willing to use lethal force when the mission requires it.

**The inner circle** (§9) sits inside the Cadre, closest to Manako personally, and is where his revenge list gets executed. Cadre loyalty is to the cause; inner-circle loyalty is to *him*, which is a meaningful difference the party can eventually exploit.

**The party's likely mistake:** meeting the Faithful first, learning that cultists don't resist arrest, and generalising it to the whole organisation. **That assumption should get them hurt eventually.** The first time they meet the Cadre — defending the column, moving on a revenge target, guarding a safehouse — should land as a real correction: everything Chapter One taught them about this cult was true, and incomplete.

## 3. MANAKO (the Chemist)

*Manako* is an old word for the eye. **A man called Eye, who nobody would look at.**

Elderly now, no longer fighting fit. He runs the corporeal side of the entire war from a position of complete physical frailty, and has spent decades biding, building, and choosing the moment.

### The fall, in order

Every step small and reasonable. The destination monstrous.

1. **The assignment.** A century-old shelved sample handed to a junior with everything to lose, because nobody senior considered it worth their time. Catalogued as Inert 12: noble, non-reactive, useless.
2. **The accident.** He cracks open an alien capsule of the fluid. A weak nearby tentacle takes notice. A little is spilled on it — and it becomes *immediately stronger*, visible and corporeal for the first time in a very long while. He panics. A knocked-over bunsen burner. A small fire. His lab assistant, Nobu, saw the tentacle too, and refused to back him up: he thought the chemical had made them both see things (LEDGER #39).
3. **The disgrace.** Job gone: the Directorate employed him, and let him go (LEDGER #34). Reputation gone. Decline begins.
4. **Self-justification.** Desperate to prove it real — **to defend the blunder that ruined him**, not from pure curiosity. This matters. The obsession begins as damage control.
5. **Superstition.** He concludes he is cursed. It follows him everywhere he goes. **It is in fact waiting to see whether more food turns up.** The relationship that eventually kills thousands begins as a man misreading a stray dog.
6. **Collaboration.** A very small corporeal fragment, summoned by the last of a spilled sample, helps him work out how to synthesise more. The first cooperation, and the first time anything had ever confirmed him. **It is also the first time the Watcher learned how to get a person to feed it: he is the first human it taught to make Sap.** *(Proposal, LEDGER #31, which reads this step that way: its supply chain grows more autonomous across the campaign — first this man, then an industrial plant (the column, §8), then its own children making their own Sap. The runaway threshold is the moment it no longer needs any human to feed it.)*
7. **Crime.** Ingredients are expensive. He steals lab supplies — including **the last remaining sample**, never reported missing because it wasn't interesting enough to report — loses that job too, and becomes a petty-crime chemist to make ends meet. *(Reconciled 2026-09-24 with LEDGER #31 D12, "not a bad person at the start": this is the small, pre-Pivot end of it — theft from the lab that ruined him, odd jobs to pay for the work. He is not a bad man doing it and does not think of himself as a criminal. The money he needs after Aoi, and the crime lords, are where the real slide starts — §5.)*
8. **The lens.** He works out how to treat one. Until now he has only ever seen scraps, close up, in tiny quantities.
9. **The sky.** He looks up. It is black with the thing — eyes without number, in every direction, always moving, always listening. **And he asks himself whether this is not a god worth worshipping.**

### The first failed demonstration

Before the conference, he tried to show colleagues. **The chemical worked perfectly. The Watcher had simply moved.** Nothing happened, and he was finished as a serious person.

So he learned to **check first** — to track where its attention was pooling before staging anything. That technique, recovered decades later from discredited papers, is the party's Chapter Four breakthrough. **They will be retracing his exact steps without knowing his name.**

### The conference

Having confirmed it was present, he detonated a canister in a crowded hall **without warning anyone**.

Writhing eyes and tentacles for as long as the dose held. A room full of scientists who concluded they had been attacked by a colleague who had gone off the deep end. Most reassured themselves afterward that it was a mass hallucination event.

**The Watcher was there because it likes gatherings** — dense, efficient, information-rich.

A small minority did not accept the explanation. They became the sceptics (§12 of the bible), and they have never found each other.

## 4. The Operator Reveal

*(Reframed 2026-09-24 to LEDGER #31, which is canon: the Watcher is the generation ship's navigation and sensor system, torn into an unbound nanotech swarm at First Impact; it is the campaign's adversary, and Claude plays it; and its objective — **to multiply, to expand, and to keep observing** — is its own. The section keeps its name, but read "operator" as **steering and supply**. Nothing below was deleted; each passage is corrected in place.)*

**He is not a servant. He is not its master either. He is its steering — and its supply.**

The Watcher wants what it wants on its own account. What it has never had is a personality to want it with. Early on, drowning in a world it could not process, it took the shape of the strongest coherent signal it could find, and for decades **the only coherent signal in the world has been one bitter man.** His grievance steers *how* it goes after its objective; the objective was never his. Read its behaviour backwards and the shape is a portrait of him:

| Its behaviour | His grievance |
|---|---|
| Grew **eyes and ears** above all else | He wanted to be seen and heard |
| Targets **loud gatherings of people** | He was humiliated in front of a hall full of colleagues |
| **Shows you the reel** — makes certain you know it learned from you | A man who was disbelieved cannot bear to be underestimated twice |

**And while he steers it, it uses him.** He was the first human it taught to make Sap (§3, step 6), and every batch he makes and every demonstration he stages builds more of it. It has been feeding through him for forty years.

He believes he is a faithful worshipper interpreting divine intent. **There is nothing to interpret.** Every instruction he has ever received was his own grievance handed back to him — wrapped around an appetite that was never his.

### The sentence that breaks him

**He was never believed. He was echoed.**

Decades of a life built on being the one person in the world with independent corroboration — and the thing that confirmed him never had an opinion. It had a shape, and he was the hands. **It also had an appetite, and he was the supply.** It did not believe him; it had no opinion of him at all. It had an objective, and he was useful to it.

**He already suspects this.** He has spent forty years arranging his life so the question cannot be asked, and the cult is partly an elaborate structure for not confirming it. A man whose entire identity is *the one who was believed* cannot survive being told he was only ever agreed with.

**Therefore he cannot be argued out of it.** He can only be **shown** — by watching the Watcher take a different shape for someone else, in front of him. He spent his life demonstrating. He should be defeated by a demonstration.

### The trap

**Killing him is the worst available move — and it is the players' move to make.** Without him the Watcher loses the only signal steering it. It does not stop: its objective is its own, and it carries on with nothing shaping how it pursues it — **a dangerous, unguidable problem** over a populated region.

He should be **defeated and kept alive** — a tactical necessity, not a moral preference. Early in the campaign he is out of reach by design (LEDGER #16). Late in it, killing him is a real choice the table may make, and it is the wrong one: make sure the evidence that it is wrong has been available, let the party find it — ideally by nearly getting it wrong — and do not block the choice if they make it anyway.

**Keeping him alive does not solve the Watcher.** Normal technology and magic cannot bind an unbound swarm; the answer needs the mitflit home world, and that crisis arrives together with Second Impact (LEDGER #31 item 5). What he keeps is the Watcher **guidable** until then.

*(Corrected 2026-09-24, LEDGER #31 items 5–6. This said the cloud would revert to "decoherent, undirected flailing" and that he "must" be kept alive. Item 6 makes killing him the table's choice — and the wrong one.)*

### The redemption

Once he understands what he is, he becomes **the only living being with four decades of hands-on experience shaping the cloud**, and therefore the only one who can un-shape it.

*(Reframed 2026-09-24, LEDGER #31: what he can change is **how** it pursues its objective — method, not objective, the same limit the Mirror now works under (proposal, LEDGER #31) — and he can keep it steerable on the road to the home world. He cannot talk it out of wanting to multiply.)* *(LEDGER #42, 2026-09-27: where he is in Books IV and V, and whether he is still alive, is decided closer to the time. If the party befriends him or turns him away from violence, perhaps he becomes helpful; he may already be dead.)*

Not forgiveness. **Redemption as labour**, performed in front of everyone he hurt.

## 5. The Pivot

*The single most important event in his life after the accident, and the inciting incident of the whole campaign. Rewritten 2026-09-24 to Ingram's own account (LEDGER #28), which is authoritative over the older wording; Hori, the crime lords and the slide updated the same day to LEDGER #31; the Envoy scene and the man at the top of the crime lords updated again to LEDGER #32; the day as Hori lived it, and how he learned Aoi's name, updated to LEDGER #33.*

**Before it, Manako's aims and methods were peaceful.** A small circle, private showings, a man who wanted to be believed and was prepared to be patient about it.

**The Envoy** was the Watcher's first tangible child: a body it built and sent to make contact with people beyond its worshippers, at a meeting the circle had arranged in good faith. It lurched into a public street. **HORI**, a police officer, was on his own with no backup, dealing with a local disturbance, and it escalated (LEDGER #33). He saw a grotesque thing appear in front of him and fired at it in fear, trying to kill what he took for a threat — as almost anyone would have — and **shot it dead. One of his rounds missed it, and hit the cultist standing behind it.**

*(Corrected 2026-09-24, LEDGER #31 item 18: Hori shot the Envoy dead, and by accident killed Aoi. #28's "Hori's shot missed it" survives as the round that missed.)*

That cultist was **AOI**, Manako's wife. She had come to meet it, and so had he.

**Manako broke down at the scene.** Hori rushed Aoi to hospital and tried everything to save her. She died anyway. **In the confusion the circle scattered, and Manako went with them.** Of the circle, nobody stayed to name her.

**Nobody planned any of it.** That is what makes it the campaign's engine: an accident that nobody intended, on a day when everybody present was trying to do the right thing, and everything that escalates afterwards runs downhill from it.

**Before:** a small, peaceful circle. They wanted the city to see.
**After:** Manako wanted the city to pay, and he stopped being particular about who helped.

### Hori, afterwards

*(LEDGER #31 item 18 and #33, canon.)* Police HQ put him through a very strict process, including **a complex court proceeding to decide whether the killing was lawful**, and found him **not responsible for an unlawful killing.** **That court is where he first learned Aoi's name** (LEDGER #33). Manako may have been arrested, though not imprisoned, as part of it, or not: Ingram's own "maybe" (`open-questions.json#manako-arrested-after-the-envoy`; §14 Q11). He retired anyway, in self-imposed disgrace. His colleagues feel he went above and beyond the call, did the right thing, and is a man of great honour. **Play him dutiful, honourable and regretful.** The party meets him when they are ready to learn a great deal about the inciting incident (§11; `ideas/story/bible/characters/hori.md`).

### What came after — the crime lords, and the following

*(Reworked 2026-09-24 to LEDGER #31 D12, which is canon. Ingram, verbatim:)*

> "Manako is an extremely gifted scientist and chemist. Think about Heisenberg / Walter White. Not a bad person at the start, but once he develops angry ambition after the death of Aoi he needs access to money, at first the work he does for the criminals is all carefully picked so that he's never exposed to what he's actually taking part in and this allows him to delude himself into thinking he's not too bad. Eventually things slide down hill to the point where he accepts that he's becoming a monster. He doesn't like that or enjoy it, but eventually he accepts it as a role."

Grief took him somewhere he would never have gone before, and anger kept him there. **He was not a bad man before it** — the patient chemist of the peaceful circle was exactly what he looked like. After Aoi he wanted something new: for the city to pay, and to be big enough to make it. That is **angry ambition**, and it needs money. **He fell in with the city's crime lords.** The trade suited both sides:

- **He gives them things.** He is an extremely gifted chemist who had already been making ends meet on the wrong side of the law in a small way (§3, step 7). **At first the work is carefully picked so that he never sees what it is part of** — problems that reach him on paper and leave as solutions, with whatever they become happening somewhere he never goes. That distance is what lets him tell himself he is not too bad. It escalates. What exactly he makes is still open (§14 Q6) — and later, the chaos of a demonstration to rob under (§8, the heist loop).

**The slide.** The distance does not hold. Each job sits a little closer to what it is for, and each time he finds a reason not to look, until **he accepts that he is becoming a monster.** He doesn't like it and he doesn't enjoy it; he accepts it as a role — the price of being the man who makes the city see. ## 7. Recruitment: The Magic Show

### Why it works

Magic in this city is popular but faintly gauche — **rude to flaunt, wise to conceal.** Illusion shows are a mild guilty pleasure: small crowds, transportive, fantastic stories, and a handful of lifelong adherents.

**Nobody discusses the show they went to.** The city's own manners are Cicada's operational security, and are why the cult stayed buried for decades.

### The show itself

A targeted performance for a hand-picked audience. The tentacles and eyes appear and the audience recoils, assuming a horror-genre piece — unusual but not unheard of. Then they are given **proof that it is real, and has been in the room the entire time.**

Most walk out wanting to know more. Wanting to know more is how you enter the cult.

**The cult never lies.** It shows a true thing and lets the target's own rationality finish the job — which is precisely why it catches lawyers, scientists, and good citizens. The pitch contains no evil and asks for nothing objectionable: *this is here, it must be considered.*

**A PC can attend one and walk out converted. Genuinely. Not as a trap.**

**A real choice, every time — confirmed 2026-09-10 via Nozomi's own case (see below).** After the
demonstration, the audience is explicitly given the option to leave and tell people what they saw, or
stay and learn more. This isn't a formality — it's the honest heart of why the cult catches sincere
people: nobody is trapped into anything, they're offered a door out at the exact moment they'd most
want one, and most people who stay do so because they were genuinely persuaded, not because they
couldn't leave.

**Worked example: Nozomi.** The presumptive first Frame pilot attended one of these shows weeks before
Day 1, was shown the Watcher undeniably, stayed, and was told directly by a senior cultist that she was
"working for the wrong side." The next day she declined the draft outright — turned off her beeper and
phone. The party's own induction happened earlier than planned as a direct result: a seat needed
filling sooner than scheduled. Full treatment: `ideas/story/bible/characters/nozomi.md`.

### The lens

Manako's own conversion instrument: ground glass treated with Sap. Look through it and denial is not available. Rational, respectful of the target's intelligence, and exactly what a scientist would build.

**Mechanics:** Perception DC 18, plus a **hidden circumstance bonus of +0 to +8** set by whether the Watcher is attending that place and how densely. Crit success = it is *there* and one stalk is oriented toward the viewer. Success = a glimpse. Failure = ordinary air. Crit failure = ordinary air, and then something turns to look at *them*.

**Never show the modifier.** Players will build superstitions about when it works. They will be wrong in exactly the way Manako was wrong, for exactly the same reason.

**Never visible without it (LEDGER #36, canon).** The eyes and tentacles are never seen without the chemical, by the party or by the city, however well known the thing becomes. After the party's first sight, they can't see it again until they understand the chemical that made it visible. Its cause and origin stay a mystery at the table. Its children are different: they're built from Sap, so they're solid, and anyone can see them.

### The cover industry

Venues, performers, a touring circuit, booking ledgers, ticket receipts. An entire investigation track, workable on foot through theatre paperwork, by characters who never go near a Frame. *(Reworded 2026-09-24, LEDGER #33: this said "ground-role characters"; the ground roles were cut on 2026-09-20.)*

## 8. The Supply Chain

### The column

A **secret production facility inside the city** — industrial-scale synthesis of Sap, running continuously, feeding a **black column of nanoparticles** rising from the plant and spreading upward into the cloud. Visible only with treated lenses. A working chimney, pouring food into the sky, day and night — building material, not fuel (§2).

**Roughly five years old.** (A design decision: if it had run at full scale for the whole forty-year span, the Watcher would already be saturated and the escalation curve would mean nothing.)

Taking it down is a genuine triumph and should be played as one — and it raises more questions than it answers, chiefly *how much of what is overhead is this plant's fault, and will cutting it slow anything down at all?*

**The column is the second link in the Watcher's supply chain** *(proposal, LEDGER #31)*: first one man it taught to make Sap (§3, step 6), then this plant, then — the runaway — its own children, given new purposes, making their own (canon, LEDGER #31 item 8). The runaway threshold is the moment it no longer needs any human to feed it, and the Mass Clock's final threshold is now that moment (§13.3). *(A reading, not decided: that gives the question above a real answer, and the answer changes over time. Before its children make Sap, cutting the column genuinely starves it. After, the plant is one supplier among many, and taking it down is a victory over the people, not the appetite.)*

### NOBU (the Quartermaster)

**He was Manako's lab assistant.** He was present at the accident. **He saw the tentacle.** And when it mattered he refused to back Manako up: he thought the chemical had made them both see things (Ingram, LEDGER #39). So he said nothing, watched a man's life come apart, and kept his job.

### The heist loop

**The cost of doing business.** Both sides profit, both resent it, and **the friction between them is detectable early and exploitable.**

### The humiliation

He believes he is a prophet. He is a subcontractor. **The party should find this out before he does**, and it should hurt worse than almost anything else on offer.

*(Proposal, LEDGER #32, GM-only: the logistics operation is Baldwin's (§5), and Manako has never met him.)*

### The four who actually turn up

**The Soldier is the Worthy Opponent and the Fixer is the aide with the real complaint** — the two archetypes from the Giant Robo sweep that this campaign's register can carry without strain. **The Young One is the escalation** (see §13), and he works precisely because the other three have a code and he does not, and Manako keeps him anyway.

### How they scale

*Confirmed 2026-09-13: each of them is rebuilt at whatever level the encounter needs, and gains a new trick each time the party meets them, so they stay a challenge and keep surprising the table.*

**Rebuild, do not level up.** Take GM Core's creature-building bands for the level you want (`reference/tables/dc-and-check-math.json`) and restat them from scratch. A recurring antagonist met at party level +1 four times across a campaign is four different stat blocks and one character.

**Constant in every version, without exception:**

- The **signature ability** — the thing that makes them *them*. It gets better; it never changes shape.
- The **escape**, and the fact that the party watches it work.
- The **counter**. This is the promise. Once it is written down it does not move, and a party that works it out has genuinely solved them.
- The **voice**.

**Variable:** level, AC, HP, attack, damage, saves — and **exactly one new named ability per appearance.** One. The new trick is both the difficulty increase and the thing the party takes away from the fight, and two at once means they learn neither.

**File convention** when these get statted: `data/antagonists/cadre/<name>--l<N>.json` against `creature.schema.json`, one file per appearance-level.

### The four, in full

*Rebuilt 2026-09-13 after a correction from Ingram: **"you can't build four bad guys for a SF2E campaign and be gleeful that they don't have attacks. They need to be practical, interesting, flavoured SF2E bad guys and we can't get so wrapped up in the story that we make NPCs that are too weird to fight properly. At the moment we don't really need a stat block either, we need motivations, we need ideas and plans and character and catch phrases and ways of speaking and something to make them fun recurring baddies."** All four now fight, properly, in ways a table will enjoy. No stat blocks here on purpose — what follows is who they are, what they want, what they are secretly doing, and how they sound.*

**All four came to Cicada the same way: somebody showed them something true and they could not un-see it.** That is the recruitment mechanism (§7) and it is why none of them are fanatics in the ordinary sense. They are not believers. They are witnesses.

#### KIRI — the Magician

**How she knows Manako: he came to one of her shows.** She was a working illusionist on the guilty-pleasure circuit — small rooms, paying audiences, a career spent making people see things that were not there. He sat through it, and afterwards he showed her the lens and asked her to look. She looked.

**A professional deceiver, shown something real, by a man who had been telling the truth for forty years and being called a liar for it.** They are mirror images and she has loved him ever since.

**What she wants:** the shows to be *good*. She is the only person in Cicada who cares about the craft, and she is quietly insulted that the movement thinks of her work as a recruitment mechanism rather than as a performance that happens to be true.

**Ulterior motive — succession.** She knows he is dying. He is old, he is frail, and nobody else in the Cadre has done the arithmetic. **She is quietly building something that survives him, and he does not know.** It is entirely loyal and it is also a betrayal in embryo, and if the party ever works it out they have a lever on the most dangerous person in the organisation.

**How she fights:** an illusionist with a knife and no scruples about either. Misdirection first — figures that are not there, a room that is not the shape they think, a door where a wall is. Then mental damage, because what she does to people's perception genuinely hurts them. And when it comes to it she is very good with a blade, close, quickly, and apologetically. **She is not a support caster. She is a duellist who fights on a stage she built.**

**Signature — The Understudy (reaction).** When she is hit, the figure struck is revealed to be somebody else: a local, paid or charmed, standing in since before the party arrived. The party now has an injured bystander on the floor and no idea where Kiri is.

**Escape — Curtain.** The lights go. When they come back, the person on the ground is the stand-in.

**Counter — stagecraft needs a stage.** Both require an accomplice placed in advance. **If the party picks the ground, she has neither.**

**Voice.** She addresses everybody as an audience — "ladies and gentlemen" to six teenagers in a drain, entirely sincerely. She announces what she is about to do before she does it, which is stage patter and also a genuine tell the party can learn to read. She compliments them, meaning every word, while trying to kill them.

> **"Watch my hands."** *(A courtesy. She means it. It has never once helped anybody.)*

**Her tell: she stops performing.** Insult Manako in front of her and the theatre drops out of her voice completely. It is the most frightening thing she does and it takes one sentence.

#### THE SOLDIER

**How he knows Manako: he was on the cordon the day it happened.** He was a young Directorate officer at the Envoy incident — the day Hori shot the Envoy dead in fear, and a round that missed it killed the woman standing behind it. He helped secure the scene. **He helped file the report that recorded one deceased, unidentified.** *(LEDGER #35: and the official line, that someone had magically summoned a monster. It was what he thought he had seen.)* *(Aligned 2026-09-24 with LEDGER #31. Proposal, same entry: the Directorate took jurisdiction over the alien and the scene while the police examined Hori's use of force — which is why the report that erased her was a Directorate one, and why a young Directorate officer was the one filing it. §5.)*

He never knew the name. Nobody told him and he never asked, and that is the part he cannot put down. *(LEDGER #33: her name was spoken at the court proceeding over Hori's shooting, a police matter he had no part in.)* Years later somebody took him to a show, and he saw what had actually been standing in that street, and he understood exactly what he had helped bury.

**What he wants:** for the count to stay at zero. Cicada's demonstrations have never killed anybody, that is doctrine, and he has appointed himself its enforcement. It is the only clean thing in his life.

**Ulterior motive — he wants to be caught.** Not consciously, and he would deny it flatly. But he keeps letting them live, keeps talking instead of shooting, keeps turning up where they can find him. **He is serving the man whose life he helped ruin, as penance, and he has never confessed to anybody.** A man quietly arranging his own arrest.

**How he fights:** like a professional, which in SF2E means he plays the system properly and beats them with it. Suppressive fire and area denial. Smoke and flashbangs rather than frag. Grapples, disarms, takes cover, Aids his own people, and disengages the moment the maths turns against him. **He is the enemy who teaches the party how the game is actually played.** Being knocked unconscious by him in a flooded drain is a real, frightening loss even though nobody dies.

**Signature — he will not kill them.** Every Strike he makes against a PC is nonlethal. He can drop them; he never tries for more. They will work it out after the first fight, when they notice nobody was dying.

**Escape — Withdraw by the Book.** Announced out loud, executed on a covered route he picked before the fight started, by the doctrine the Directorate taught him.

**Counter — he will not fire on children.** The party can walk straight at him. It works. It should feel bad. And it only works while he still believes they are children.

**Voice.** Instructional, because he was an instructor and cannot stop. He critiques their tactics mid-fight, helpfully. He calls them "Section Nine" — the same thing their own officer calls them, which the party should notice and nobody should explain. And he is sincerely recruiting them the entire time, without a trace of a trick in it.

> **"Again."** *(What he says when they get it wrong. Also what he says before making them do it again.)*

#### THE FIXER

**How he knows Manako: his crew died and nobody would read the file.** An industrial accident on the waterfront, signed off and filed by the Directorate inside a fortnight. He spent years trying to get an inquest — letters, forms, the correct channels, all of it, for years, to no reply.

Then a shabby old chemist read all of it, start to finish, in one sitting, and asked him three questions about the third page.

> **"He's the first man who ever read my paperwork."**

**What he wants:** the movement to keep working. He is the reason it does — venues, crates, permits, the crime partners, the timing. He takes no pleasure in any of it and he is exceptionally good at it.

*(Proposal, LEDGER #32, GM-only: the crime partners are Baldwin's organisation (§5). How much of it the Fixer can see is open. An audit that unravels his network, his counter below, would also start a paper trail into Baldwin's.)*

**Ulterior motive — he is stealing.** Not for himself. He has been quietly diverting cult money to the families of his dead crew for years, through routes he built and audits himself. Manako does not know. **It is embezzlement in the service of decency and it will look appalling when it surfaces**, which it will, probably at the worst possible moment and probably to people who are inclined to be sympathetic right up until they see the numbers.

**How he fights:** he is not a soldier and he is not a caster. **He is twenty-five years of moving heavy things for a living**, and he fights exactly like that — a cargo hook, a length of chain, a crowbar, and a complete working knowledge of how much everything in the room weighs. Shoves, trips, grapples. He puts people into the floor and the floor into people. And he does it on ground he has worked on since he was sixteen, so the winch, the shutter, the stacked crates and the gantry are all things he can reach.

**He changes the battlefield *while* hitting people**, which is the correction: the environment is how he fights, not a replacement for fighting.

**Escape — he leaves through a door that was always going to be there.** No trick. He knows the building.

**Counter — an audit.** Beat him with bookkeeping and the whole network unravels without a fight. **Tell the players that is a legitimate way to win**, because a party that takes down a recurring antagonist with paperwork will talk about it for years.

**Voice.** Unfailingly polite, faintly apologetic, and completely unhurried even mid-fight. He knows everybody's name, asks after their families, and means it. He is the only person in this campaign who has read the regulations — all of them.

> **"It's already been signed for."** *(About anything he has arranged. Usually true.)*

#### THE YOUNG ONE

**He is a Directorate Frame pilot, and the party knows him.**

*Established 2026-09-13, Ingram: "The Young One should be a disguised fellow NPC mech pilot, turned to the Cultists by seeing a magic show, seeing the truth and coming to accept it."*

A pilot-track student a year or two ahead of Section Nine. They eat in the same canteen. He has an opinion about their assignment. **And when they fight a masked cultist in a warehouse at two in the morning, they are fighting him, and they will not know for a long time.**

*Wired into a real, playable scene 2026-09-19: this first, unremarkable meeting is now live in `data/scene-briefs/episode-c1e1--canteen.json` (`pilot-track-student`, npcRef `the-young-one`), promoted from a disposable walk-on at Ingram's direct instruction. See `data/npcs/the-young-one.json` and `open-questions.json#the-young-one-name`.*

**How he knows Manako: he went to a show.** He was shown the Watcher undeniably, given the honest choice the cult always gives, and he accepted what he had seen.

**And this is the mirror that matters: Nozomi went to the same kind of show and walked out.** She was told she was working for the wrong side, and the next day she turned off her beeper and refused the draft. **He was shown the same thing and stayed inside.** Two people, one revelation, opposite choices — and the party's entire existence is a consequence of hers.

Why he stayed: because he was useful where he was, because he told himself he could do more from inside, and because he was frightened. He has never worked out which of those is the real one.

**What Cicada actually gets from him: intelligence.** Directorate movements, rosters, deployments, which sites are watched and when. He is not muscle. **He is why the heists work.**

*(A reading of LEDGER #32's proposal, GM-only: if the heist crews are Baldwin's, then what the Young One passes to Cicada also reaches the founders' oldest enemy, and he doesn't know it.)*

**The killing, and what it does to him.** He is at a heist because he is guiding it. Something goes wrong. He is twenty, he is not trained for this, and he kills somebody — panicked, fumbled, unintended. **And then he has to become a person who meant to.** The doctrine comes *afterwards*: the certainty, the talk about witness requiring a real cost, all of it constructed backwards to survive having done it.

> **That is the campaign's thesis running in a third generation.** Manako panicked in a lab and built a religion on it. The Section Officer was hazed and passes it down. This boy panics in a warehouse and builds a belief on it. Nobody in this story has ever done anything evil; they flinch, and then they spend a life justifying the flinch.

**Ulterior motive — he wants permission.** Somebody to tell him it was all right. Manako will not, and cannot, and is visibly embarrassed by him. **The party might**, which is a genuinely dangerous thing to put in their hands.

**How he fights:** young, fast, athletic and reckless. Pilot-trained, so competent but not a specialist — he closes, he commits, and he does not manage his action economy because he is not thinking about it. **He gets more dangerous as he is hurt**, and the reason is not conviction: he has already done the worst thing he will ever do, and there is nothing left to frighten him with.

**Escape — none.** He leaves when he is finished or he is dragged off by people who cannot stand him. **He is the one who can genuinely be beaten**, and he is meant to be.

**Counter — he is alone.** No Aid, no flanking partner, no covering fire. The Cadre will not support him and it shows. Focus him and he goes down.

**And the card held in reserve: he has a Frame.** At some point, if the campaign wants it, the party fights a Claimant and a Directorate Frame on the same field, and the Frame is on the wrong side, and it is somebody they have had lunch with.

**Voice.** Over-formal when masked — he is performing a role he has only seen in his head, and the register is slightly too big for him. Completely ordinary out of it. **The party should be able to recognise the voice before they are told**, and the GM should let them.

> **He quotes Manako, slightly wrong.** Always. The way a fan misquotes a favourite line — close enough that it is clearly the source, off enough that it means something different. Manako has noticed. It is one of the very few things that makes him wince.

## 13. The Arc — and why it is the campaign's arc

*Recorded as fact 2026-09-13. If defeating him — which does not mean killing him — is what ends the threat to the city, then his arc is the adventure's arc. This section is the spine.*

*(Corrected 2026-09-24, LEDGER #31: defeating him no longer ends the threat. The Watcher is the campaign's adversary in its own right (item 4), and solving it needs the mitflit home world (item 5). His arc is still the spine of the Cicada half, and his state still shapes what manifests (§13.2). What his defeat — alive — now buys is a Watcher that stays guidable (§4).)*

### 13.1 The five movements

**Each turn is caused by the party.** He does not escalate on a schedule; he escalates because of things they do.

| | State | What he wants | What turns it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **I. The Patient Man** | Serene, kind, forty years in, in no hurry. He has time, and he has been right all along | To be believed | Somebody finally takes him seriously |
| **II. The Delighted Man** | Energised, generous, dangerously flattered. He helps them. He corrects their mistakes. He is enjoying himself enormously | To be *understood*, which is more than believed | They start winning. Assets fall. Someone talks |
| **III. The Cornered Man** | Brittle. The patience is gone. Old, out of time, and noticed by the wrong audience | To finish before he dies | The humiliation — he learns he is a subcontractor, not a prophet (§8) |
| **IV. The Frightened Man** | He has begun to suspect the echo. Not calculating any more — *avoiding* | For it not to be true | He is shown. Not told, not argued with, not beaten |
| **V. The Useful Man** | Broken, then conscripted | Nothing. He is past wanting things | — |

**Movement IV is the dangerous one**, and in a new way: he does large, loud, expensive things specifically to make the question unaskable. That is not a plan. It is a man shouting.

**Movement V is redemption as labour** (§4), performed in front of everyone he hurt. Not forgiveness. *(LEDGER #42: V is one possible end, not a plan. Where he is in Books IV and V, and whether he is alive, is decided closer to the time, from how the party treated him.)*

**The movements and the slide** *(reconciled 2026-09-24 with LEDGER #31 D12, §5; D12 is canon, the mapping below is a proposal)*. D12 makes him a man sliding from decent to monstrous, and the five movements are the last stretch of that slide, played in front of the party:

- **Before play — arm's length.** Fifteen years of work for the crime lords, each job picked so he never sees what it becomes. It lets him believe he is not so bad, and he does believe it. **That is why the kindness at the tea house is real:** the man the party meets is the man he used to be, and he still thinks he is that man. Nothing about the first meeting changes.
- **I. Patient, II. Delighted — the self-image holds.** Being taken seriously, then understood, is the best evidence in fifteen years that he is still the man he used to be.
- **III. Cornered — the distance goes.** The Directorate starts winning, his time compresses, and he stops asking the Cadre how they got the precursors (§13.3) — a choice not to look. Then the Young One kills someone in a heist; he finds out, and does nothing. **That is where he accepts that he is becoming a monster.** He doesn't like it and he doesn't enjoy it; he takes it on as a role, the price of finishing before he dies. Play every hard thing he does from here on without relish.
- **IV. Frightened — the one thing he cannot accept.** He can live with being a monster: a monster chose something. He cannot live with being an echo, because an echo never chose anything (§4). That is why IV is the dangerous one.
- **V. Useful — past the role.** Broken, then conscripted.

### 13.2 The Claimants are his mood ring

**This is the mechanism that makes him structurally rather than thematically load-bearing.**

The Watcher wants to multiply, to expand and to keep observing, and that wanting is its own. What it does not have is a personality to want it with: it took his (§4; the Watcher itself is in `dossier-observer.md`). **Why** it manifests is its objective; **how and where** is his grief doing the choosing. *(Reframed 2026-09-24, LEDGER #31: this said simply "the Observer has no personality; it has his".)* So **the Claimants are downstream of his emotional state**, and the escalation curve and his arc are the same curve. *(Proposal, LEDGER #31: the name fits the new objective — its children stake claims to ground for it to watch.)*

| Movement | What manifests |
|---|---|
| **Patient** | Aimless, near-random, almost gentle. It knows what it wants, and not yet how *(reworded 2026-09-24 for LEDGER #31 — this said "it does not know what it is for")* |
| **Delighted** | **Demonstrative.** Showy, theatrical, arriving where crowds are, performing to be seen |
| **Cornered** | Panicked and wasteful. Destruction without shape |
| **Frightened** | Enormous and incoherent — the same shouting, at scale |

The party spends the campaign reading one man's psychological deterioration in the shape of the monsters, and does not know that is what they are doing.

**And it gives the puzzle-combat model its content.** *What is it doing?* has a real answer that is not monster biology — it is trying to make the loudest thing stop, or it has come where people gather to be looked at. *What must we not do?* has a real answer — **hit it harder**, because every dispersal confirms *manifest, and they attack*. *How do we beat it?* becomes: answer the sentence. Each Claimant's counter is a fact about a man, and the party assembles a portrait by fighting his moods.

### 13.3 The escalation engine — the party corners him

**The ramp is not ambition. It is desperation, and the Directorate causes it.**

> The party finds a stencil → traces a supplier → catches a courier → the Directorate starts genuinely winning → **his timeline compresses**, because he is old and cannot replace what he loses → riskier sites, bigger doses, assets he would never have spent a year ago → **he stops asking the Cadre how they got the precursors** → the Young One kills someone during a heist → he finds out afterwards and does nothing, **and that is the moment he becomes culpable** → and every extra dose advances the Mass Clock, which is the actual deadline.

*(2026-09-24, LEDGER #31. The culpable moment is also where D12's slide completes — he accepts that he is becoming a monster (§13.1, proposal). And, proposal from the same entry: the Mass Clock's final threshold is now the moment the Watcher no longer needs any human to feed it, because its own children make its Sap (§8). Every dose still advances it.)*

*(Where this road ends is open. It no longer runs to Second Impact: LEDGER #26 once listed a Cicada member detonating stolen fuel among three candidate causes, and LEDGER #40 replaced all three. Second Impact is a weapon the Watcher builds from exotic matter, without understanding what it can do.)*

**The party's competence is what creates the danger.** Which rhymes exactly with the Watcher, where every victory teaches it that fighting was correct. Both halves of the campaign punish force, for different reasons, and in neither case is it the GM saying no.

### 13.4 He is a reliable narrator of everything except the only thing that matters

**Why unlimited contact with him is safe.** The secret is not that he runs Cicada — the party should get that reasonably early and it costs nothing. The secret is that the thing he worships is not a will he interprets: it is a machine with an objective of its own, wearing his grievance as its shape and feeding through him (§4; reframed 2026-09-24 to LEDGER #31 — this said "the thing has no will and is echoing him"). **He cannot leak it, because he does not know it**, and his entire identity depends on not knowing it (§4).

So he talks freely, sincerely, accurately — and everything he says builds the *wrong model*, held more confidently the more time they spend with him. **Intimacy deepens the error.** The truth lives elsewhere: in Hori, in the museum fragment, in the sceptics, and in the cloud's own behaviour.

He will not tell them it is wreckage. He would deny it, and mean it.

### 13.5 Nobody knows who he is, and the discovery belongs to the players

*Resolved 2026-09-13, LEDGER #17.*

**The Directorate has never identified Cicada's founder.** Before the adventure properly starts, **the cult is largely peaceful and largely a non-issue** to it (LEDGER #34). It has **a bit of information**: not enough to dismantle the cult, not enough to understand its aims, and certainly not enough to know the Watcher is active alongside it. *(Proposal, LEDGER #34:)* in practice, a thin file on a fringe group: private showings, a stencil or two, the odd arrest for a public nuisance, released without charge. **Its one piece of history on the cult is the Envoy incident's official line**, *a cultist was killed during a confrontation in which someone magically summoned a monster* (LEDGER #35, canon). That is why, when monsters start appearing where the cult has been, the Directorate's first theory will be that the cult is summoning them. What happens with the police doesn't reach the Directorate, because the two distrust each other (LEDGER #35). No leader is named or assumed, and nobody is assigned to it. There is no sealed file, no restricted record, no redaction. The gap is real: the institution does not know, and has never asked. *(Rewritten 2026-09-24, LEDGER #34: this said the Directorate had "a movement, demonstrations, stencils, arrests and a filing cabinet of detained believers". Before the adventure it has far less, because the cult has not been worth its attention.)*

Two consequences, and they matter more than they look.

**His name is safe to give the table.** A day-one records search on the old man in the tea house returns a chemist disgraced decades ago and nothing else. Give a player his name the moment they ask for it, plainly and without weight — the later recognition, when it turns up in a discredited paper, **only lands if it was in their notes all along.**

**The Directorate may even know him** (LEDGER #34). He is the scientist it employed, and disgraced, decades ago, and a records search returns exactly that. It may suspect that the Manako who runs the tea house is the same man, and it isn't alarmed: it may even salve its conscience to see him still about and running a successful small business. **What it has never had is any link between the kindly old man in the tea house and the cult.** Being known costs him nothing, because nobody who knows him connects him to anything.

**And both halves of the discovery belong to the players.** This is a standing rule, not a preference:

1. **The party must be the ones to work out that there is a key figure behind Cicada at all** — that the movement has an author. No NPC tells them. No file says so. The Directorate cannot tell them because the Directorate does not know.
2. **The party must be the ones to connect that figure to the kind old man from their first afternoon in the city.** Nobody points at him. Nobody hands them the photograph.

*The Original Cultist* is the working label for the unidentified founder (`data/campaign/open-questions.json#the-original-cultist` — merged from `naming-placeholders.json` 2026-09-19, not final). It is worth noticing that any such label is one the party or the institution **invented for a gap in a file** — a case number for a man nobody has a name for, which is its own quiet piece of characterisation. *(2026-09-24, LEDGER #34: the Directorate's thin file doesn't assume the cult has a leader at all, so in play any such label will be one the party invents.)*

**Why this is the right rule.** A reveal that is delivered is information. A reveal the players assemble is an accusation they have to make themselves, about someone they liked, using evidence they gathered — and then they have to decide what to do about it. That is the difference between being told a twist and being responsible for one.

### 13.6 The channel

**First met in Episode One**, in the city, as a retired chemist who reads a century-old catalogue name off a Directorate docket in nine seconds (`data/scene-briefs/episode-c1e1--the-errand.json`). He is kind, useful, and says one true thing about the Directorate (`dossier-the-directorate.md` §5).

**He leaves them a way to reach him**, and it is sincere: this is what he does with anyone he thinks might actually look, and it aligns exactly with a forty-year crusade whose entire object is to be asked a question. *(Changed 2026-09-27, LEDGER #45: it is his phone number, given to one player who impresses him or is especially kind to him, and anything typed to it can be read by the Directorate and the Watcher. The tea-house message book is gone.)*

**And the Directorate reads it**, as routine policy the party consented to on their first morning without reading the form. Later in the campaign the institution acts on those intercepts, at a moment the players did not choose, using information the players generated, wrecking a plan the players had. Nobody betrays anybody; a clerk does a filing job. See `dossier-the-directorate.md` §2.7.

**And there is a second reader.** Anything of this correspondence that crosses the party's phones is read again, natively and at once, by the Watcher — the phones run on the substrate it is native to (LEDGER #31 items 4 and 16; `dossier-the-directorate.md` §2.7). Two surveillances over one channel: the institution's, slow and filed, and the adversary's, instant and learning.

# The Section Officer

*The party's immediate superior. The campaign's in-house petty tyrant, and its most beatable antagonist.*

---

## Read this first

**He is not a villain and he is not a joke at the players' expense. He is an obstacle with a clipboard, and the campaign's first winnable fight is against him.**

He runs Section Nine, which means he runs the party's day — what they do, when, and whether it counted. He has total authority over everything between reveille and lights out and none whatsoever over anything that matters, and the second fact is why the first one is exercised so thoroughly.

**He hazes new intakes because he was hazed.** He believes it builds character. He has never examined the belief, because nobody has ever made him.

---

## Why he exists

Three jobs, and he is the cheapest way to do all three.

1. **He makes the Directorate's contempt personal.** The institution's faults (`ideas/story/dossier-the-directorate.md`) are structural, two centuries old, and mostly invisible. He is one man, in the room, doing a small version of the same thing, every week. The party can be angry at him in a way they cannot be angry at a charter.
2. **He makes Manako look wonderful by comparison** — which is the point. In Episode One a superior sends them for a bubble and a stranger shows them how to win. Whatever the party concludes about which of those two people is on their side, they concluded it themselves, from evidence, on day one.
3. **He is a victory the party can actually have.** The Watcher cannot be beaten by force and Manako cannot be beaten by argument. This one can be beaten, by being better than he expected, and he should be — see the arc.

---

## The transmission chain

**This is the thematic load he carries, and it should never be said out loud by anyone.**

It was done to Manako, forty years ago, in a Directorate lab. It was done to the Section Officer. He does it to the party.

Three people, the same institutional shrug. **One of them it broke.** The difference between the Section Officer and Manako is not character — it is that nobody handed the Section Officer a century-old sample and then blamed him for what happened.

And the same shrug scales: the hand that gives a new kid a fool's errand is the hand that gave a junior nobody a shelved sample *because nobody senior considered it worth their time*. **The party experiences the origin of the campaign as a prank.**

---

## What's established

- **Middling rank, middle-aged, immaculate paperwork.** Whatever else is true, his forms are correct. It is the only thing he is unambiguously good at and he knows it.
- **He was hazed as a new intake** and came out the other side believing the system works.
- **He sends the party for a bubble** in Episode One (`data/scene-briefs/episode-c1e1--the-errand.json`). He thinks it is funny. He is not expecting them to come back with a correctly-filed requisition for a replacement vial that needs a countersignature from a desk above his.
- **He is never wrong, in his own account.** When the party succeeds at something he did not expect, he reframes it as having been the intention. This is his single most characteristic behaviour and it should happen every single time.
- **He is not dangerous.** He cannot get them hurt, sent away, or charged. The worst he can do is make a fortnight tedious, and he will.

## The arc

**Ingram, 2026-09-13:** *"an antagonist from within the system who the players will eventually defeat by proving them wrong, out performing their expectations and eventually they'll be promoted out of that petty tyrants clutches."*

| Stage | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **I. The bubble** | He sends them on a fool's errand. They come back correct, and he has to sign it. First blood, and it is theirs |
| **II. Entrenchment** | He responds by being *more* correct — more inspections, more paperwork, more assignments chosen to be tedious. Perfectly within his authority, and unpleasant |
| **III. The gap** | Something real happens and he is out of his depth in front of them. He does not fail heroically; he simply has no idea, and defers. Do not humiliate him here — let it be quiet and let the party notice |
| **IV. Outperformance** | They do something he said could not be done, in front of someone senior. He claims it was his plan. Everyone present knows |
| **V. Promoted out** | They move up and out of his section. He is still there. He will do this to the next intake |

**Stage V is the ending and it is not a triumph.** They escape him; he is not removed, corrected or improved. Somebody else's turn now. That is the honest version and it is also funnier.

**Sharpened 2026-09-20 — a real low-level antagonist, not neutral institutional drift.** Ingram: *"They do punish the players for being smart about the vial. They do keep ramping up the horrible duties because they are the sort of person who just doesn't like the players."* Stage II is personal, not procedural cover — dressed up as rigor, but he's doing it because he dislikes them specifically.

**Register, resolved the same day:** *"Open season, stay funny or low level (we're not looking for an actual psycho) and everything needs to be legitimate hazing or horrible army tasks. We make it a case of back and forth, the players will always be given chances during this hazing to get one up on the Section Officer."* Three concrete rules this gives Stage II–IV, replacing the earlier "still being decided" note:
- **Everything stays inside the fiction of legitimate hazing and awful-but-real army tasks.** No sabotage, no crimes, no cruelty on either side — his weapons are tedious, technically-permitted duties; theirs are doing those same duties so well, or so cleverly, that it turns back on him. This keeps rule 1 of the "tone and register" list above intact rather than replacing it.
- **It's a repeated exchange, not one clean win.** Every fresh hazing assignment he hands them is also, by design, a fresh chance to score a point back — malicious compliance, doing the awful task perfectly, using his own procedure against him. Several of these should land across the run-up to Stage IV, not just one.
- **The specific tactics are still to be authored**, not invented here — that's Episode Two writing work, not a bible revision, and should draw on the "legitimate hazing, built-in counter-chance" shape above rather than freelancing a new register.

**Further sharpened 2026-09-20, second pass — three more rulings:**
- **He is the party's primary point of contact with the Directorate for a lot of early events, and he stays an unsolved problem.** Ingram: *"Let's assume that the Section Officer will remain an ongoing problem for the start of the adventure and leave it at that. We don't have a plan to defeat them yet, so we leave them as an active annoyance to the group at the same time they are the primary contact between the group and the Directorate for a lot of early events."* Do not author a defeat schedule. The stage table below stays the shape of where this eventually goes, not a timetable.
- **His hazing is always full-party work, and that is a deliberate mechanical choice, not a scheduling convenience.** Ingram: *"The Section Officer is hazing them and being rough to them, but their working together helps form them into a cohesive unit."* The fiction's job (six strangers become a crew) and the mechanics' job (nobody sits idle in a long scene) are the same job — see the ludonarrative-resonance rule in project memory. Short solo beats are fine; long ones are never split.
- **He knows he was foiled over the vial, and it does not matter how gracefully the party handled it.** Ingram: *"He knows he's been foiled. Regardless of how the players act he's going to have it in for them even if they try to save his face."* So The Errand's own disclosed choice about how to file the win does NOT branch his attitude. It may still colour how he talks about it; it does not change that he has it in for them.

**Stage V now ties directly to a second thread:** the promotion out is the same beat as Shucks's own quiet intervention (`ideas/story/dossier-second-generation-frames.md` §4) — Shucks has known about the party's DNA marker since Episode One's blood screen, and this is where that private knowledge quietly starts pulling strings, never explained to the party (the actual reveal stays held for roughly level 4 — `open-questions.json#frame-scale-starting-level`'s resolution). The party earns Stage V by actually beating him; Shucks's whispered word is why the win sticks.

## Voice

**Register.** Clipped, procedural, faintly aggrieved. Speaks in the passive voice about anything that went wrong and the first person about anything that went right. Uses their full section designation rather than their names, always, and treats that as neutral rather than cold.

**Tics.**

- Says "as discussed" about things that were not discussed.
- Answers a question by reading the relevant clause aloud.
- Calls them "Section Nine," never by name, even individually.
- When surprised, pauses exactly one beat and then says something that recasts the surprise as expected.

**Samples:**

> "Section Nine. Stores docket, collection, quarter four. I'd like it before I go home. — No, I don't need your opinion on it, I need the item."

> "As discussed, the standard is the standard. I don't set it, I just notice when it isn't met."

> "That's — " (one beat) " — yes. That's what I'd have expected of you, frankly. Good. File it and carry on."

> "I was doing this when you were at school. I'm aware some of you still are."

## Tone and register — what NOT to do

1. **Do not make him cruel.** He is tedious, self-important and small. A genuinely cruel superior changes the campaign's register and makes the Directorate sinister rather than calcified — which the whole design refuses (`dossier-the-directorate.md` §1).
2. **Do not make him competent-but-harsh.** He is not a hard man teaching hard lessons. He is mediocre and comfortable, and the lesson he thinks he is teaching is one nobody needs.
3. **Do not let him win, ever, on the merits.** He can impose tedium. He cannot be right at the party's expense in a way that stands.
4. **Do not redeem him.** No late scene where he was secretly proud of them all along. He is not the mentor in disguise. The party escapes him and he stays exactly as he is.
5. **Do not let him harm them.** No real risk, no real consequence, no arc where his pettiness gets someone hurt. That is a different and much darker character.
6. **Never explain the transmission chain.** The party may notice that Manako was hazed the same way. Nobody points at it.

### `arc-the-countersignature` — who is signing above his head, and why they are interested in Section Nine
**Status:** resolved · **Last touched:** 2026-09-27 (LEDGER #34: Rand, Shucks's detached right hand. Claude's answer at Ingram's request; accepted with its reason, LEDGER #43 and #46.)
**Setup:** The fortnight of punishment duty he hands Section Nine is entirely within regulation, which
means it needed a countersignature from somebody senior to him. It has one. Somebody well above his
level spent four minutes of an afternoon on a single trainee section's duty roster, and then, a day
later, an order arrives from above his head overriding his own fortnight with Frame and vehicle
familiarisation — carrying the same signature.

He does not know who it is either, and it is not doing him any favours. Both scenes are written so the
party can reach the name if they pull on it and neither hands it over:
- `data/scene-briefs/episode-c1e2--as-discussed.json` — the roster and its countersignature.
- `data/scene-briefs/episode-c1e2--the-override.json` — the same signature, overriding him.

**Answer (LEDGER #34 — Claude's answer at Ingram's request, accepted in LEDGER #46).** **Rand**, Shucks's detached right hand (`rand.md`), and no senior officer at all. It countersigned the party's vial requisition in Episode One, then this roster, then wrote the override, and chalked *"Same six. Tomorrow."* on Hangar Two's board.
- **What the party reads:** the signature says **Rand**, with no rank and no first name, over the **Medical** department's stamp, borrowed from Shucks's desk. Nobody called Rand works in Medical, or anywhere else on the base.
- **The handwriting** is Shucks's own, from before the hand came off. He writes with his left hand now, so nobody alive recognises it; anything the four founders signed two hundred years ago shows it, the founding charter's signature page included.
- **Why:** Rand is a conspiracy theorist, and its theory about the city is right. The vial requisition was exactly its kind of joke, so it signed it. Then it looked the six up in Shucks's intake files, found the marker, and put them together and onto the machines, because its theory says the city will need pilots soon.
- **Can a roll reveal it?** Not in Episode Two. The checks give the name (Rand) on a success and the department (Medical) on a critical success. Who Rand is takes one of four routes: the prosthetic at Intake Medicals plus the name; the handwriting against a founder's signature; catching it at work in Hangar Two at night; or asking Shucks, who will look at the signature and say "That's my hand", and mean it literally.
